To store negatives well, keep them clean, dry, cool and in sleeves made for the job. A negative is the original of every print you will ever make from it. Stored well, it outlives you. Stored badly, it can scratch, stick together or grow mould.

:::callout{kind=summary}
- Cut film into strips and slide them into archival sleeves as soon as they are dry.
- Keep the sleeves in a binder or box, upright, in a cool, dry room.
- Hold negatives by the edges only, or wear cotton gloves.
:::

## Sleeves and boxes

Use sleeves sold as archival or photo-safe. Cheap plastic sleeves can give off chemicals that damage film over time. Paper sleeves also work. Keep the sleeves in a ring binder or a box, standing upright so they do not press together.

## Where to store negatives

- **Not in the loft or the cellar.** Both swing from hot to cold and from dry to damp.
- **A cupboard in a lived-in room** is ideal: steady and dry.
- **Away from sunlight, radiators and outside walls.**

## Label everything

Write the date, the subject and the film on each sleeve. Keep the [contact sheet](/blog/contact-sheet/) of each roll in the binder next to its negatives, so you can find a frame without handling the film.

## Scan them as a backup

A scan of each roll protects you against loss, such as a flood or a fire. Scan the frames you care about, or the whole roll, and keep the files in two places. A scan is a copy, not a replacement: the negative still holds more detail than most scans, and you need it to make a darkroom print. So store negatives with the same care after you scan them.

## Handle with care

Fingerprints leave oils that etch the film. Hold negatives by their edges, or wear thin cotton gloves. Blow dust off with a hand blower, never with your breath.

The Library of Congress gives more advice on [preserving photographs](https://www.loc.gov/preservation/). If you are still choosing a film, our notes on [choosing a film stock](/blog/choose-film-stock/) cover what to buy.
