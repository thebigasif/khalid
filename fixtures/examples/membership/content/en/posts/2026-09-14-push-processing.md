Push processing means rating a film faster than its box speed, so you can shoot in dim light, and then developing it for longer to make up for the missing light. It turns a dark evening or a dim concert hall into usable negatives. The price is more contrast and more grain, and you have to push the whole roll.

:::callout{kind=summary}
- Decide before you shoot, and shoot the whole roll at the same speed.
- Develop longer than normal, using the push time on the data sheet.
- Expect darker shadows, brighter highlights and stronger grain.
:::

## When push processing helps

Push a film when the light is too low for the shutter speed you need, and you would rather have a grainy picture than none. Concerts, evening streets and indoor family moments are the classic cases. Do not push to rescue a roll you shot at box speed. Only the roll as a whole can be pushed.

## How we push film at Silverline

1. **Set the camera's film speed** to the higher rating before the first frame, and mark the film canister so you remember.
2. **Shoot the whole roll** at that rating. Meter for the shadows you care about.
3. **Find the push time** on the maker's data sheet for your film and developer. Most sheets list a time for each step up.
4. **Develop for that time** at the normal temperature. Agitate as usual.
5. **Note the result** on the sleeve, so you can adjust next time.

The basics of [developing film at home](/blog/develop-film-home/) still apply. Only the time changes.

## What to expect in the print

Pushed negatives are denser in the highlights and thinner in the shadows. Print them on a softer grade of paper to bring the contrast back down. The [entry on push processing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Push_processing) explains the chemistry behind this.

## Choosing a film to push

Fast films with classic grain push most gracefully. Our notes on [choosing a film stock](/blog/choose-film-stock/) list which kinds we keep on the shelf.
