This case study is about a bakery's first email list. The bakery has one shop and a small team, and it bakes a few new things each week. The owner wanted regulars to know about them without relying on social media, where few of her posts were seen.

:::callout{kind=summary}
- The bakery had loyal regulars but no way to reach them directly.
- We set up a sign-up card at the till and a form on the website.
- A welcome email and a short Thursday letter now go to everyone who joins.
:::

## The brief

The owner's [brief to us](/blog/agency-brief/) was a single line: "I want my regulars to know what is on the counter this weekend." We liked it, because it named the reader and the result.

## How we set up the email list

1. **Two ways to join.** A small card at the till with a short web address, and a sign-up form on the website. Both say what the email list is for in one line.
2. **A welcome email.** It comes from the owner by name, says the letter arrives each Thursday, and includes this week's bakes. It follows Clara's [guide to welcome emails](/blog/welcome-email/).
3. **A short weekly letter.** A photo of the weekend's specials, a line about each, and the opening hours. It takes the owner a quarter of an hour to write each week.

We asked every person for clear consent at sign-up, as UK rules require. The [overview of email marketing at Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Email_marketing) covers the wider practice and its rules in other countries.

## What changed

The owner says regulars now mention the letter at the counter, and some come in on Friday to be sure of a loaf they read about. The letter also gives her a reason to plan the weekend's bakes on Thursday, and the email list belongs to the bakery, not to a social network.

:::callout{kind=note}
We publish no list size or sales figures. They belong to the bakery, and we only share what we can show.
:::
