Leaf mould is what fallen leaves become when you leave them to rot slowly in a pile. It is dark, crumbly and smells of a wood after rain. It does not feed plants much, but it improves soil better than almost anything else, and it is free. I am writing this in August so you have time to get ready before the leaves fall.

## Why leaf mould is worth the wait

Leaves rot slowly, mostly through fungi rather than the bacteria in a compost heap. That is why they take longer. What you get at the end holds water like a sponge and loosens heavy soil. On my clay, a layer of it each autumn has made the beds far easier to work.

## How to make leaf mould

::::howto{name="Make leaf mould"}
:::step{name="Collect the leaves"}
Rake up fallen leaves after rain, when they are damp. Leaves from oak, beech and fruit trees rot well. Leave out evergreen leaves, which rot very slowly.
:::
:::step{name="Bag them or pile them"}
Pack damp leaves into old compost bags with a few holes punched in, or into a simple cage of wire netting on four posts.
:::
:::step{name="Wait"}
Tie the bags or leave the cage in a shady corner. Check after a year: the leaves are ready to use as a mulch when they crumble.
:::
::::

Leaves left for a second year turn finer still, good enough to mix into seed compost.

## Where I use it

- As a mulch on [no-dig beds](/blog/no-dig-bed/), each autumn.
- On damp soil around thirsty crops in summer, to keep the water in.
- Under the cover crop I sow in autumn, which you can read about in my letter on [green manure for winter beds](/blog/green-manure/).

:::callout{kind=note}
Ask your allotment site whether you may collect leaves from paths and hedges. Most are glad to have them cleared.
:::
