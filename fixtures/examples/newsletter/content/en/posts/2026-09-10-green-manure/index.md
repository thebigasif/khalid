Green manure is a crop you grow to protect and feed the soil, not to eat. Sown on bare beds in early autumn, it covers the soil through winter, stops rain washing it away, and keeps weeds out. In spring you cut it down, and it rots back into the bed. I sow green manure on every bed that would otherwise stand empty.

## Which green manure to sow

| Crop | When to sow | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Grazing rye | Early to mid autumn | Grows in cold weather, and its roots open up heavy soil |
| Field beans | Autumn | Add nitrogen to the soil, and the young tops are edible |
| Crimson clover | Late summer | Adds nitrogen, and flowers for bees if left into spring |
| Phacelia | Late summer | Fast cover, and loved by bees if it flowers |

Field beans and clover belong to the pea family, so they add nitrogen to the soil through their roots. Do not follow them with more peas or beans, to keep pea and bean diseases from building up.

## How to sow it

Rake the bed level, scatter the seed thinly, and rake it in. Water it if the soil is dry. That is all.

## Cutting it down in spring

:::callout{kind=warning}
Cut green manure down before it sets seed, or it becomes a weed. Grazing rye in particular grows tough if left late.
:::

Cut it down a few weeks before you plan to plant. Leave the cut tops on the surface to rot, or cover them with [leaf mould](/blog/leaf-mould/) or compost. On a [no-dig bed](/blog/no-dig-bed/) you can lay cardboard over the top instead of digging it in.

Green manure is also a good use of the bed where you lift potatoes, before you [plant garlic](/blog/plant-garlic/) in late autumn. And in a hot summer, a covered bed loses less water, as I wrote in my letter on [the dry spell](/blog/dry-spell/).

The [entry on green manure at Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_manure) lists more crops and how they work.
