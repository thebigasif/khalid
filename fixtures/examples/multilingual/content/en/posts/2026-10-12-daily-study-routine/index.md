A daily study routine is a small, fixed time each day when you work on your language. It matters more than any method, because a method you use every day beats a better one you use when you feel like it. The hard part is not starting. It is still studying in the second month, when the first excitement has gone.

## Make the daily study routine small

Choose a time so short that you cannot argue with it: a quarter of an hour, or even ten minutes. On good days you will do more. On bad days you will still do the minimum, and the habit survives.

## Tie it to something you already do

Attach your study to a moment that happens every day anyway: the first coffee, the bus ride, the walk with the dog. "After I pour my coffee, I do my flashcards" is a plan. "I will study in the morning" is a wish.

## Plan the week, not just the day

Give each day of the week one kind of practice, so you never waste time deciding:

| Day | Practice |
|---|---|
| Monday | Review cards with [spaced repetition](/blog/spaced-repetition/) |
| Tuesday | Read a chapter of [a graded reader](/blog/graded-readers/) |
| Wednesday | Review cards |
| Thursday | Speak with a partner, or shadow a recording |
| Friday | Review cards |
| Weekend | Something fun: a film, a song, a recipe in the language |

## Track it where you can see it

Put a calendar on the wall and cross off each day you study. A chain of crosses is surprisingly hard to break. If you miss a day, do not miss two.

:::callout{kind=note}
Notice what you learn, too. Once a month, write down one thing you can do now that you could not do before, such as [spotting false friends](/blog/false-friends/) or following a podcast.
:::

## Measure progress by what you can do

Levels from A1 to C2, set out in the [Common European Framework of Reference](https://www.coe.int/en/web/common-european-framework-reference-languages), describe what a learner can do at each stage. Reading those descriptions every few months shows you how far your daily study routine has taken you.
