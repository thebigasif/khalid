A content refresh is the work of bringing an old post back up to date so it serves readers again: new facts, a clearer answer, better links. It is the cheapest growth most blogs ignore. Writing a new post starts from nothing. A refresh starts from a page that search engines already know.

This playbook shows how we choose which posts to refresh, what we change, and what we leave alone.

## Why old posts slip

Posts lose ground for ordinary reasons. The facts change. A tool gets renamed. Competitors publish something clearer. Readers' questions shift. None of this shows on the post itself, which looks exactly as it did on the day you published it.

The cost of leaving posts alone is real. Our benchmark found that in 2026, 38% of small business blogs had not updated a single post in twelve months. Posts updated within the year earned, in 2026, 1.6 times the search clicks of untouched posts at the median. A content refresh is how a blog collects that gain.

:::pullquote{by="Priya Raman, research editor"}
A blog is not a stack of finished pages. It is a garden, and the oldest beds need the most weeding.
:::

## Find the posts worth a content refresh

Not every old post deserves your time. Look for posts that once did well or that answer a question your customers still ask. Three signals point to the best candidates:

- **Falling clicks.** The post's search clicks are lower than they were a year ago.
- **Near misses.** The post shows up in results often but sits just below the top few places.
- **Out-of-date facts.** The post names a year, a price or a tool that has changed.

Sort your posts by these signals and pick a few per month. A steady habit beats a big rewrite project. We keep a refresh slot every second week in [the calendar our team plans with](/blog/content-calendar/), so the work never waits for a quiet month.

## Decide what each post needs

A content refresh can be small or large. Match the work to the problem:

| Problem | What to do |
|---|---|
| Facts out of date | Replace them and cite the new source with its year |
| Answer buried | Move a short answer to the top |
| Thin on detail | Add an example, a table or a step list |
| Wrong question | Rewrite around the question readers ask now |
| Overlaps a newer post | Merge the two and redirect the weaker one |

## Run the refresh in seven steps

::::howto{name="Refresh an old blog post" time="2:00:00"}
:::step{name="Read the post as a stranger"}
Read it top to bottom on a phone. Note every place where you would stop reading or stop trusting it.
:::
:::step{name="Search its question again"}
Search the question the post answers and read the top results. Note what they cover that your post does not.
:::
:::step{name="Check what AI search says"}
Ask an AI search tool the same question. See which pages it quotes and what kind of answer it gives.
:::
:::step{name="Update the facts and sources"}
Replace every number, name and screenshot that has changed. Give each new number its source and its year.
:::
:::step{name="Sharpen the answer"}
Put a plain answer near the top. Cut sections that no longer earn their place.
:::
:::step{name="Refresh the links"}
Link to posts you have written since, and check that every outside link still works.
:::
:::step{name="Show the date and say what changed"}
Set the updated date, and write one sentence at the top that tells readers what is new.
:::
::::

Before a content refresh goes live, run the post through [the editors' pre-publish checklist](/blog/editing-checklist/). A refresh deserves the same care as a new post.

:::callout{kind=warning}
Keep the post's address. A new address throws away the links and trust the old one earned. If you must move it, redirect the old address to the new one on the same day.
:::

## Tell readers what changed

An updated date on its own can look like a trick. Readers have seen posts whose date changes every month while the text stays the same. Be specific instead. One sentence at the top, such as "We added the new pricing and a section on exports", tells readers the update is real.

Google's guidance on [showing dates in search results](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/publication-dates) asks for the same honesty: change the date only when the content really changes.

## Measure whether it worked

Write down the post's clicks and results before you change it. Check again after six to eight weeks, and compare the same length of time before and after. Some posts climb fast. Others need a second pass.

:::callout{kind=read}
Our method for [measuring what each post earns](/blog/content-roi/) works for a content refresh too. Count the hours the update took, and compare them with what the post brings back.
:::

## Make the content refresh a habit

The teams that get the most from old posts do not run a big project once a year. They keep a short list, refresh a few posts each month, and treat the archive as part of the blog rather than its attic. Start with the post that once did best and has slipped the most. It is usually the fastest win you have.
