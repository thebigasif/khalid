A content brief is a one-page plan that a writer reads before they write a word. It says who the post is for, which question it answers, what it must cover and how it will be judged. Ours takes twenty minutes to fill in, and it saves hours of rewriting.

This is the exact template we use for client work, with a filled-in example below it.

## The sections of our content brief

Every brief has the same seven parts, always in this order:

- **The reader.** One sentence on who they are and what they already know.
- **The question.** The one question the post answers, in the reader's words.
- **The short answer.** Two or three sentences that settle the question. The post supports this answer.
- **What the results page shows.** The format and depth of the pages that rank now, and what they miss.
- **Must cover.** The points a reader needs. Each one usually becomes a heading.
- **Sources.** Where the facts and numbers will come from, with the year of each.
- **Links.** The posts this one links to, and the posts that should link back to it.

## Why the short answer comes first

Writing the answer before the post feels backwards. It is the most useful line in the brief. If the person writing the brief cannot answer the question in three sentences, the writer will not manage it in two thousand words. Fix the thinking in the brief, where it is cheap, not in the third draft.

This habit comes straight from our [guide to writing posts that rank](/blog/seo-writing-guide/), which builds every post around one question.

## A filled-in content brief

Here is a real brief, lightly edited, for a post on our own blog:

- **The reader.** A marketer at a small software company who writes the blog alone, alongside other work.
- **The question.** How do I plan blog posts so they actually get published?
- **The short answer.** Keep a short shared calendar with one owner per post, a pace you can hold, and time kept free for editing and old posts.
- **What the results page shows.** Long lists of tools and big-team templates. Nothing for a team of one or two.
- **Must cover.** Why calendars fail, the few columns that matter, a pace that lasts, a weekly check-in, and a free template.
- **Sources.** Our own benchmark data from this year, and one outside guide to editorial planning.
- **Links.** Out to the pillar guide, the editing checklist and the ROI post. In from the benchmark and the refresh playbook.

That brief became [our post on keeping a content calendar](/blog/content-calendar/). The writer finished the first draft in one sitting.

## How to use the template

Fill in the brief with whoever asked for the post, not alone. The conversation is half the value: it is where you find out that the sales team wants a different question answered. Save each brief beside its draft. When the post needs a refresh a year later, the brief tells you what it was meant to do.

For a wider view of planning content around reader needs, the UK government's guide to [writing content for the web](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/content-design/writing-for-gov-uk) is one of the clearest free resources there is.
