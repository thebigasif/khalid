In the first episode of The Ledger, out loud, Priya and Maren talk about one habit: putting the answer first. AI search tools build their replies from short passages on many pages. A passage that answers the question plainly, with its source in view, is far easier to quote than one that warms up for three paragraphs.

## Show notes: why the answer first matters

What we cover in this episode:

- Why a reply from an AI search tool is built from passages, not whole pages.
- How to write an [[answer-capsule|answer capsule]]: a short, plain answer right under a question heading.
- Why a source and a named author make a passage safer to quote.
- What to do with the rest of the post once you put the answer first.

Further listening and reading from our blog:

- Maren's guide on [how to write answer capsules under question headings](/blog/answer-capsules/).
- Priya's study of [which pages AI tools choose to cite](/blog/ai-search-sources/).
- Our full [guide to being found in AI search](/blog/ai-search-guide/), the hub of this series.

## The transcript

:::toggle{summary="Read the full transcript"}
**Priya:** Welcome to The Ledger, out loud. I'm Priya Raman, research editor at Larkspur Studio.

**Maren:** And I'm Maren Okafor, head of content. We're starting with the habit we ask every writer for: the answer first, everything else after.

**Priya:** Let's start with why it matters more now. When someone asks an AI search tool a question, the tool doesn't hand back one page. It reads many pages and builds a reply from short passages. Google's own features work like this too, such as the [[ai-overview|AI Overview]] at the top of some results.

**Maren:** So the unit that gets chosen is the passage, not the post.

**Priya:** Right. Often the tool splits one question into several smaller ones and searches for each. That is called [[query-fan-out|query fan-out]]. Your page might be picked to answer just one of those small questions.

**Maren:** Which means each section has to stand on its own. If the answer to "how long should a meta description be" only makes sense after the three paragraphs above it, it is hard to lift out.

**Priya:** So what does a section with the answer first look like?

**Maren:** The heading asks the question in the reader's words. The first two or three sentences answer it plainly. No "great question", no history. Then the detail comes after: the reasons, the exceptions, the example.

**Priya:** How long is that first answer?

**Maren:** Our house rule is forty to sixty words. Long enough to be complete, short enough to quote whole. Khalid's linter checks it for us, so it never slips.

**Priya:** And it should make sense out of context. If you read only that paragraph, do you know what "it" refers to?

**Maren:** That's the test I use most. I copy the paragraph into a blank note. If it still answers the question, it's done. If it says "as we saw above", it's not.

**Priya:** Let's talk about sources. In my work on how these tools cite pages, the passages they quote tend to state a fact plainly and show where it came from.

**Maren:** Which is what a good editor asks for anyway.

**Priya:** Exactly. A number with a link to where it came from is easier to trust than a bare number. And a page with a named author, who has real experience of the subject, gives the reader a reason to believe it.

**Maren:** Google says much the same. Its guidance on AI features says the usual good practice applies, and there's no special trick. We link it in the notes.

**Priya:** So nobody needs a separate "AI version" of their post?

**Maren:** No. Write one good page. Make each section answer one question, plainly, with its source. That serves the skimmer, the search engine and the AI tool at once.

**Priya:** What about the rest of the post? If you put the answer first, why would anyone keep reading?

**Maren:** Because the answer is the start, not the whole job. Below it you give what a short reply can't: the worked example, the step-by-step, your own data, the edge cases. That is where you add something new, what we call [[information-gain|information gain]].

**Priya:** And that's often what gets a page cited over a dozen others saying the same thing.

**Maren:** If ten pages give the same answer, the one with a real example from real work has a reason to be chosen.

**Priya:** Any traps?

**Maren:** Two. First, writing the question heading and then not answering it. The heading promises, the paragraph dodges. Second, stuffing every section with the same keyword. It reads badly and helps no one.

**Priya:** And from my side: don't invent numbers to sound precise. If you don't have a source, say it's an example. A made-up figure is worse than none, because once it's quoted, it travels.

**Maren:** So, one thing to try this week?

**Priya:** Pick your best post. Find the three questions it answers. Under each heading, rewrite the first paragraph so it answers in a few plain sentences.

**Maren:** Then copy each one into a blank note and check it still makes sense alone.

**Priya:** That's it for episode one. Thanks for listening.

**Maren:** See you next time.
:::

## What to do after listening

Pick one post and try the exercise from the end of the episode: the answer first under every question heading. Then read Google's own page on [AI features and your website](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/ai-features), which Maren mentions. It confirms that good pages need no special treatment to appear in AI search features.
