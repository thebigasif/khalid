Search intent is the goal a person has when they type a search. They might want to learn something, reach a page, weigh options or buy. If your post serves a different goal from the one the searcher has, it will not rank for long, however well it is written.

This post is part of [our guide to writing posts that rank](/blog/seo-writing-guide/). It shows how to read the goal behind a search before you plan a single heading.

::toc

:::callout{kind=summary}
- **Every search has one main goal.** Know, go, compare or buy.
- **The results page shows you the goal.** Look at what already ranks before you write.
- **Match the format, then beat it.** A guide cannot rank where searchers want a product page.
:::

## Four goals behind a search

Writers sort search intent into four broad groups. Most searches fit one of them:

- **Know.** The searcher wants to learn or understand. "What is a canonical tag" or "why is my sourdough flat".
- **Go.** They want a page they already have in mind, such as a login page or a brand's pricing page.
- **Compare.** They are weighing options before they decide. "Best project tools for small teams" or "monthly or yearly plan".
- **Buy.** They are ready to act. "Book a dentist near me" or "order business cards".

:::figure{src="intent-types.svg" alt="Four cards that show each kind of search intent a searcher can have"}
The four goals. A blog post usually serves "know" or "compare". Product pages serve "buy".
:::

A blog is at its best on "know" and "compare". When a search is really about buying, the results page fills with shops, and a blog post has little chance of a place, however good it is.

## Read the results page first

You do not need a tool to find the goal. The search engine has already worked it out, and it shows you on the results page. Search the phrase in a private window and study the first page for ten minutes.

Look for these signals:

- **The kind of page.** Guides, lists, shop pages, tools or videos.
- **The angle.** "For beginners", "in five minutes", "free", "for small teams".
- **The extra boxes.** A short answer box means a quick fact. A map means a local need. Product cards mean buying.
- **How fresh the pages are.** Dated titles suggest searchers care about recent advice.

:::callout{kind=tip}
Write down the shared angle of the top results in one line before you plan. If six of the top pages say "for beginners", your reader is a beginner, whatever you assumed.
:::

## Know pages compared with compare pages

The two goals a blog serves most often need different pages. The table sets them side by side.

| | Know pages | Compare pages |
|---|---|---|
| What the reader wants | To understand one thing | To choose between options |
| Best opening | A plain answer in the first lines | The options and who each suits |
| Shape | Headings that explain, then examples | A table, then a section per option |
| Proof | Clear examples and sources | Honest pros and cons, tested claims |
| Ending | What to do next | A recommendation for each type of reader |

## Mixed goals on one results page

Some searches carry two goals at once. "Email marketing" might mean "what is it" for one person and "which tool" for another. The results page shows the mix: a guide at the top, a list of tools below it, maybe a video.

::::columns
:::column{title="Serve the main goal"}
Find the goal behind most of the top results and write for it. Give the second goal one short section near the end, with a link to a page that serves it in full.
:::
:::column{title="Or split the page"}
If both goals are strong, write two posts. One answers "what is it", the other compares the options. Link them to each other so a reader can move between them.
:::
::::

## Shape the page to the searcher's goal

Once you know the goal, it decides most of your choices:

- **The title.** It should promise the thing the searcher wants, in their words.
- **The first lines.** Settle the question early for "know" searches. Name the options early for "compare" searches.
- **The depth.** Match the depth of the pages that rank, then add what they miss.
- **The next step.** Point to the page a reader needs after this one.

The words searchers see before they click matter too. Our post on [writing a meta description that earns the click](/blog/meta-description/) covers the snippet under your title.

## When search intent changes

The goal behind a phrase can shift. A phrase that once returned guides can start to return tools, or news, when something changes in the market. In our [yearly benchmark of small business blogs](/blog/content-benchmark/), posts that lost their place often still matched the old goal, not the new one.

Check the results page again whenever an important post starts to slip. If the kind of page that ranks has changed, change your page, not just its date.

## Search intent and AI answers

AI search tools read the goal too. For a "know" question they often give a short answer and quote the page that put it most plainly. Our research on [how AI tools pick the pages they quote](/blog/ai-search-sources/) shows what those pages have in common.

A page that serves the right goal, with a plain answer near the top, is the page both readers and AI tools want. Google's own guide to [how search works](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/how-search-works) describes the same idea: results are chosen for what the searcher meant, not just the words they typed.
