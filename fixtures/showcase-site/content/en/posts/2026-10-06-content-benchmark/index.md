The Larkspur content benchmark is our yearly look at how small companies that sell to other businesses run their blogs. During August 2026, 140 of their public blogs went through our hands. We read each one post by post and counted what any reader can see: who wrote each post, when it was last updated, how it answers its question and where its numbers come from.

This is the summary. Members get the full tables by category.

::toc

:::callout{kind=summary}
- **Most blogs hide their writers.** In 2026, 61% of the blogs put no author name on their posts.
- **Old posts are left alone.** In 2026, 38% of the blogs had not updated any post in twelve months.
- **Updates pay.** Posts updated within the year earned, in 2026, 1.6 times the search clicks of untouched ones at the median.
- **Few plan together.** In 2026, 23% used a content calendar the whole team could see.

## Who wrote this post

Readers trust a person more than a logo, and search engines look for signs that a real expert stands behind a page. Even so, most blogs we read in 2026, 61% of them, showed no author at all. Fewer still had an author page with the writer's experience.

:::callout{kind=stat}
In 2026, **61%** of the small business blogs we read put no author name on their posts.
:::

The fix takes an afternoon: a name on each post, a short bio, and a page per writer that says why they know the subject.

## The forgotten archive

The most common habit we found was neglect. Blogs kept publishing, but they rarely went back. In 2026, 38% had not changed a single post in the past twelve months, including posts that named prices or tools that no longer exist.

:::callout{kind=stat}
In 2026, **1.6 times** the search clicks went to posts updated within the year, compared with untouched posts, at the median.
:::

We cannot prove the update caused every extra click. Posts worth updating may have been stronger to begin with. But the pattern held in every category we read, and it matches what we see with clients. Our [playbook for posts that slipped](/blog/content-refresh/) shows how to choose which ones to update first.

## Planning and pace

The median blog in our sample published, in 2026, 2 posts a month, usually in bursts followed by long gaps. Only 23% in 2026 used a content calendar shared with the whole team. The blogs with a shared plan published at a steadier pace and had fewer gaps longer than two months.

If your team plans in a chat thread, our guide to [a calendar a small team can keep](/blog/content-calendar/) is a gentle place to start.

## The parts searchers see first

Titles and descriptions are the first thing a searcher reads, and many blogs left them to chance. In 2026, 54% of the blogs left the meta description blank on at least one post in five. Search engines then write their own snippet from the page, which rarely sells the click.

Titles fared better, but many were vague labels like "Our thoughts on pricing". Our post on [headlines that earn the click](/blog/writing-headlines/) shows what works instead.

## Sources and AI search

Only 19% of the blogs in 2026 linked their numbers to a source. That matters more each year. AI search tools prefer passages that state a fact plainly and show where it comes from, so an unsourced number is less likely to be quoted. Read [how AI search picks which pages to quote](/blog/ai-search-sources/) for the full picture.

## The content benchmark by category

The full data splits the blogs into four groups: software, professional services, manufacturing and logistics.

- **Software blogs** were the most active, with a median of 3 posts a month in 2026, and the most likely to show an author.
- **Professional services** had the oldest archives. In 2026, 52% of them had not updated any post in a year.
- **Manufacturing blogs** published least, often 1 post a month in 2026, but their posts were the longest and the best sourced.
- **Logistics blogs** had the most blank meta descriptions: 68% in 2026 left at least one in five empty.
:::

## What the content benchmark means for your blog

Four gaps, ranked from the cheapest to close.
:::

You do not need to fix everything at once. Pick the gap that is cheapest to close:

1. **Add author names and bios.** An afternoon of work, and it helps every post at once.
2. **Refresh your best old post.** Start with the one that once did well and has slipped.
3. **Write the missing descriptions.** Ten minutes per post, and you choose what searchers read.
4. **Share one calendar.** One sheet the whole team can see, with an owner per post.

Measure the effect the same way we do: count the cost and the results, post by post. Our guide to [return on content](/blog/content-roi/) shows how.
