A content calendar is a shared list of the posts you plan to publish, who writes each one, and when it is due. It sounds like admin. In practice it is the one tool that turns "we should blog more" into posts that actually go live, week after week.

Most small teams try a calendar once, fill it with twenty ideas, miss the first deadline and give up. This post shows a lighter way: a calendar small enough to keep, with time built in for the work nobody plans for.

::toc

## Why most calendars fail by week three

The usual calendar is a wish list. It has a title for every Tuesday and no owner, no question and no time for editing. When one post slips, the whole plan slips with it, and the calendar becomes a record of what did not happen.

Our benchmark found that in 2026, 23% of the small business blogs we read used a content calendar shared with the whole team. The rest planned in someone's head or in a chat thread. Their median pace in 2026: 2 posts a month, with long gaps between bursts.

A calendar that survives has three traits. It is short, it names one owner per post, and it leaves gaps on purpose.

## What a content calendar should hold

Keep the columns few. Every column you add is one more thing to update, and a calendar nobody updates is worse than none. These six are enough:

:::table
| Column | What goes in it | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Week | The week the post goes live | Sets the pace you can see at a glance |
| Working title | A plain title you can change later | Tells the team what the post is about |
| Question it answers | One sentence in the reader's words | Keeps the post focused on one job |
| Author | One name, never "the team" | Someone owns the deadline |
| Status | Idea, planned, drafting, editing or published | Shows where work is stuck |
| Refresh due | The season you will check it again | Keeps old posts from going stale |
:::

The question column does the most work. A post with a clear question is quicker to write and easier to edit, which is the core idea of [our guide to SEO writing](/blog/seo-writing-guide/).

## Pick a pace you can hold

Start slower than you think you need to. One good post a fortnight, kept up for a year, gives you a library of useful pages. Weekly posts that stop in the third month give you a few orphans and a guilty feeling.

::::columns
:::column{title="A pace that lasts"}
One new post every second week. The weeks between are for editing, refreshing an old post and planning the next one. The calendar always has slack, so one late draft does not break it.
:::
:::column{title="A pace that breaks"}
A new post every week with no slack. Editing gets squeezed, old posts never get touched, and the first holiday or sick day pushes every date back.
:::
::::

:::callout{kind=tip}
Put the refresh slots in the content calendar first, then fill the new-post slots around them. Time for old posts is the first thing a busy team drops.
:::

## Fill the calendar from real questions

Do not brainstorm titles. Collect questions. Good sources are close at hand:

- **Sales and support.** The questions customers ask before they buy and after they start.
- **The search bar.** Type your main topic and read the suggestions and the questions on the results page.
- **Your own posts.** Readers who finish one post often need the next step. That step is a post.

Write each question into the calendar as it comes, then pick the best ones when you plan the month. Before a post starts, turn its row into a short brief. Our members can use [the content brief template we use](/blog/content-brief-template/) for that.

## Run the calendar in one short meeting

The calendar needs a rhythm, not a project manager. We use one fifteen-minute meeting a week:

1. Look at this week's row. Is the post on track? If not, move it now and say so.
2. Look at next week's row. Does the author have what they need?
3. Look at the refresh slot. Which old post gets attention this time?
4. Add any new questions to the bottom of the list.

That is the whole meeting. Anything bigger goes to a separate conversation.

:::toggle{summary="What if a post is late?"}
- Move it to the next free slot and tell the team in the meeting.
- Never squeeze editing to keep a date. A late, edited post beats an on-time, rough one.
- If the same post slips twice, the question may be too big. Split it into two posts.
:::

## Leave room for editing and refreshing

Every new post needs an edit before it goes live. Budget about half the writing time for it. We run each draft through [the checklist our editors use](/blog/editing-checklist/), and that step belongs in the calendar as a date of its own, not as a hope.

Old posts need time too. Our benchmark shows that in 2026, 38% of the blogs had not updated a single post in twelve months. Yet posts updated within the year earned, in 2026, 1.6 times the search clicks of untouched ones at the median. A refresh slot every second week is a cheap way to collect that gain.

## Check what the calendar produced

At the end of each quarter, look back. Which posts brought readers, sign-ups or sales? Which questions led nowhere? Move more of next quarter's slots toward what worked. Our post on [measuring the return on content](/blog/content-roi/) shows a simple way to do this without a data team.
