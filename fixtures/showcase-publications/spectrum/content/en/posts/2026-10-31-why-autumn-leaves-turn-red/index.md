Autumn leaves turn red, orange and yellow for different reasons. Two of the colours were in the leaf all along. The red is made fresh each autumn, and scientists are still arguing about why.

## Green hides the other colours

All summer a leaf is full of chlorophyll, the green pigment that turns sunlight into food. Chlorophyll breaks down quickly and the tree keeps making more. There are also yellow and orange pigments in the leaf, called carotenoids, but the green hides them.

In autumn, shorter days and cooler nights tell the tree to stop. It pulls nutrients back from its leaves and stops making chlorophyll. The green fades, and the yellow and orange that were there all along show through.

## Why autumn leaves turn red

Red is different. It comes from pigments called anthocyanins, the same ones that colour red cabbage and blackberries. Many trees, such as red maples and sweetgums, make them new in autumn, in the last weeks of the leaf's life.

Why spend energy on a leaf about to fall? One idea is that the red acts as a sunscreen. It shields the leaf while the tree pulls back its nutrients, so it can save more of them. Another idea is that it warns insects that the tree is healthy and well defended. The [autumn leaf colour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autumn_leaf_color) debate is far from settled.

## Why some years are brighter

The brightest reds come after a run of sunny days and cool, but not freezing, nights. Sun lets the leaf make sugar, and cool nights trap that sugar in the leaf, where it turns into red pigment. A warm, cloudy autumn gives dull colours. An early hard frost can end the show before it starts.

## Where to see it

North America's eastern forests are famous for their reds, because they have many maples. In Europe, look for the reds of cherries, rowans and the Virginia creeper on old walls. Japan has a word, momijigari, for going out to see the red leaves.
