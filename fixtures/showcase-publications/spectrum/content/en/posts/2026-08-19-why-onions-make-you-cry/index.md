Why onions make you cry is a question about defence. An onion cannot run from an animal that wants to eat it, so it carries a chemical trap. When you cut it, you spring the trap, and your eyes pay the price.

## A trap in two parts

An onion's cells keep two things apart: sulphur compounds in the main part of the cell, and an enzyme in a small sac beside them. Cutting breaks the cells and lets the two mix. The enzyme turns the sulphur compounds into new ones, and another enzyme turns those into a gas called syn-propanethial-S-oxide.

## Why onions make you cry and not sneeze

The gas drifts up from the board and reaches your eyes. It touches nerve endings on the surface of the eye, which sense it as an irritant. Your eyes answer the only way they can: they make tears to wash it away. The [lachrymatory factor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syn-Propanethial-S-oxide), as chemists call it, was only found in 2002.

## Why some onions are worse

Strong yellow onions carry more sulphur than sweet onions or spring onions, so they make more gas. Old onions that have been stored a long time are often worse than fresh ones. Red onions sit in the middle.

## Four ways to cry less

1. **Chill the onion.** Thirty minutes in the fridge slows the enzymes, so less gas forms.
2. **Use a sharp knife.** A sharp blade cuts cells cleanly instead of crushing them, so fewer break open.
3. **Leave the root end till last.** It holds the most sulphur, so cut round it.
4. **Work near a cooker hood or an open window.** Moving air carries the gas away from your face.

Wearing glasses helps a little. Holding bread in your mouth, an old kitchen tip, does almost nothing.

## Cooking takes the sting away

Heat breaks down the enzymes and the gas, which is why cooked onions are sweet and never sting. The sugars in the onion brown slowly in the pan, the same way the crust of a loaf browns in the oven, as Jonah explains in [the slow art of sourdough](/blog/the-slow-art-of-sourdough/).
