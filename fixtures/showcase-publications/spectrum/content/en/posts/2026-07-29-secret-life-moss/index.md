Moss is easy to overlook. It is small, green and grows where little else will: on walls, roofs, rocks and the north side of trees. Yet these tiny plants were among the first to live on land, and they still grow on every continent, Antarctica included. The [moss family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moss) holds about 12,000 kinds.

## A plant without roots

These plants have no true roots. They hold on with fine threads called rhizoids, but they take in water and food through their leaves, straight from the rain and the air. That is why they grow on bare rock, where a rooted plant could not.

They also have no flowers or seeds. Instead they spread by spores, tiny cells that drift on the wind. In spring you can see their capsules on thin stalks above the green.

## It can dry out and come back

Many kinds can dry out completely, go brown and brittle, and stay that way for months. When rain returns, they turn green again within hours. In 2014, scientists in Britain even grew new shoots from a clump that had been frozen in Antarctic ice for centuries.

## What it does for the forest

A green carpet on the forest floor holds water like a sponge. It soaks up rain, lets it go slowly, and keeps the forest floor damp in dry spells. Insects and spiders live in it, and birds use it to line their nests. In bogs, [sphagnum moss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sphagnum) builds up over thousands of years into peat, which stores a great deal of carbon.

## Learning to love moss

Many gardeners fight it on their lawns. Some are giving up and letting it grow. A lawn of these small plants needs no mowing, no feeding and little water once it is settled, and it stays green in winter. It grows best in shade, where grass struggles anyway.
