The Amsterdam canals are best seen slowly, from the bank. Boats are fun, but from the water you see roofs and bridges. On foot you see doors, gables and the life of the street. This walk takes a day, with stops.

## The plan of the city

The three main canals, the Herengracht, the Keizersgracht and the Prinsengracht, curve round the old centre in rings. Merchants dug them in the 1600s to move goods and to give the rich a place to build. The [canal ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canals_of_Amsterdam) is now a World Heritage Site.

Start at the Brouwersgracht, in the north west. It crosses the ends of all three canals, so you see the shape of the plan at once.

## Walking the Amsterdam canals

Walk south along the Prinsengracht, the outer ring. It is the busiest, with houseboats, cafés and the Westerkerk tower. Then cross to the Keizersgracht and walk back north. It is quieter, with wide houses built for wealthy families.

Finish on the Herengracht. Its bend between Leidsestraat and Vijzelstraat is called the Golden Bend, because the houses there are twice as wide as anywhere else.

## How to read the houses

The tall, thin houses lean forward a little. This is on purpose: each has a beam with a hook at the top, used to haul furniture up the front, and the lean stops loads hitting the windows. Look at the gables too:

1. **Step gables** look like small stairs and are the oldest.
2. **Neck gables** rise in one tall, narrow block.
3. **Bell gables** curve like a bell and came last.

## Where to stop

Stop for coffee by the Noordermarkt on a Saturday, when the farmers' market fills it. Eat lunch in the Nine Streets, the small lanes that cross between the canals. End the day by the Amstel river, where the canal ring meets the water that gave the city its name.
