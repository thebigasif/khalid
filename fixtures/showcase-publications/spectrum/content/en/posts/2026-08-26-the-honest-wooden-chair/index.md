A good wooden chair is the hardest thing a furniture maker makes. It must hold a moving person, often leaned back on two legs, for decades. And it must look right from every side, because nobody puts a chair against a wall and leaves it there.

## What honest means

An honest chair shows how it is made. You can see where the legs meet the seat and how the back is held. Nothing is hidden under paint or padding. When it starts to wobble, you can see which joint needs glue.

## The joints that matter

The weakest point of any chair is where a leg meets a rail. Look for these:

- **Mortise and tenon.** A tongue on one piece fits a slot cut in the other. It is strong and has held chairs together for centuries.
- **Dowels.** Round wooden pegs glued into holes in both pieces. Good when done well, but weaker than a tenon.
- **Through wedges.** The leg passes right through the seat, and a wedge splits its top to lock it. You see these on Windsor chairs.

Metal brackets and screws can hold a chair too, but they work loose as the wood moves with the seasons.

## Choosing a wooden chair

Sit in it for five minutes, not five seconds. Your feet should rest flat, with your thighs level. The back should meet you below the shoulder blades. Then stand up and push the back from behind. A good chair barely moves.

Oak, ash and beech make the strongest chairs. Ash bends without breaking, which is why it was used for tool handles and the curved backs of Windsor chairs. The [Windsor chair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Windsor_chair) is still one of the best designs ever made.

## Made to be mended

A plain wooden chair can be taken apart, reglued and put back together. A worn seat can be planed and oiled. With care, it will outlast the person who bought it, and their children too.
