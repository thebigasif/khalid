Why the sea is salty is one of the oldest questions children ask. The answer starts on land, with rain falling on rocks, and it takes millions of years.

## Rain picks up salt from rocks

Rain is not pure water. It takes in a little carbon dioxide from the air, which makes it very slightly acid. When it falls on rocks, it slowly dissolves them and picks up tiny amounts of minerals. Sodium and chloride, the two parts of table salt, are among them.

Rivers carry this water to the sea. A river does not taste salty because the salt in it is so dilute: about one part in ten thousand.

## The sun takes back only the water

At sea, the sun warms the surface and water evaporates. The salt stays behind. The water falls again as rain, runs over rocks and brings a little more salt to the sea. Over hundreds of millions of years, the salt has built up.

Vents on the sea floor add more. Hot water seeps through cracks near the edges of the plates that make up the Earth's crust, and it carries minerals with it.

## Why the sea is salty but not saltier

If rivers keep bringing salt, why does the sea not get saltier each year? Because salt also leaves. Some settles into mud on the sea floor and becomes rock. Some is taken up by living things. The [salinity of seawater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seawater) has stayed close to the same level for a very long time.

| Water | Salt in each litre |
|---|---|
| Rain | Almost none |
| A typical river | About 0.1 g |
| The open ocean | About 35 g |
| The Baltic Sea | About 7 g |
| The Dead Sea | About 340 g |

## Where it is saltier

The Baltic is less salty because many rivers pour into it and little water leaves. The Dead Sea is far saltier because water flows in, but none flows out: the sun takes it all. Seas in hot, dry places, such as the Red Sea, are saltier than the open ocean for the same reason.
