Reading the clouds is a skill sailors, farmers and walkers have used for centuries. You do not need any tools. You need to look up, and know what you are looking at.

## How clouds form

Clouds form when air rises and cools. Cool air holds less water vapour, so some of it turns into tiny drops or ice crystals around specks of dust. Billions of them together make a cloud. How the air rises decides the shape of the cloud.

## The three main shapes

In 1802 a chemist named Luke Howard gave clouds Latin names that we still use. There are three basic shapes:

- **Cumulus**, meaning heap: puffy clouds with flat bases, made by bubbles of warm air rising.
- **Stratus**, meaning layer: flat grey sheets, made when a wide layer of air rises gently.
- **Cirrus**, meaning curl: thin wisps high up, made of ice.

The [list of cloud types](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_cloud_types) combines these, such as cirrostratus or stratocumulus.

## Reading the clouds for the day ahead

| Cloud | What it often means |
|---|---|
| Small, scattered cumulus | Fair weather for the rest of the day |
| Cumulus growing tall by midday | Showers or a storm by the afternoon |
| Cirrus thickening into a milky sheet | Rain within a day |
| Low, grey stratus | Drizzle, but little change |
| A halo round the sun or moon | A warm front, and rain on the way |

## Watch for change

The single best clue is change. A sky that fills from high wisps to a lower, thicker sheet over a few hours is telling you a front is coming. Heaps of cumulus that grow taller and darker through the day can become thunderstorms. Clouds that stay small and fade by evening mean a settled night.
