The neighbourhood library was supposed to be dying. Everyone had a phone, books were cheap online, and councils closed branches to save money. Yet in many towns the library is now one of the busiest rooms on the street.

## More than books

Libraries still lend books, and more than ever lend e-books and audiobooks too. But walk into a busy branch today and you may find a seed library, a shelf of tools to borrow, a sewing machine, or a room of children learning to code. Some lend musical instruments. Some lend fishing rods.

The idea is the same as it always was: people share the cost of things that each of them needs only now and then.

## A warm room that asks for nothing

The library is one of the few indoor places where you can stay all day without buying anything. That matters more than it used to. Students come to work. Older people come for company and the newspapers. Parents come for story time, and stay for the chat afterwards.

## What makes a neighbourhood library work

The libraries that thrive tend to share a few habits:

1. **Long hours**, including evenings and weekends, when people are free.
2. **Staff who know the area**, and run events for the people who live there.
3. **Space to sit**, with tables, power sockets and good light.
4. **A welcome for noise** in one part, and quiet in another.

## Old idea, new life

Public libraries spread in the 1800s, often paid for by local gifts, so that working people could read and learn. The [public library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_library) is still built on that promise: knowledge open to anyone who walks in.

Ask at your own branch what it lends besides books. You may be surprised. And if you are after records, read [why vinyl records came back](/blog/why-vinyl-came-back/).
