The slow train over the Bernina Pass takes four hours to go 144 kilometres. A car would do it in less than three. I have taken it six times, and I would not swap a minute of it.

## A railway that climbs without help

The line runs from St. Moritz in Switzerland over the pass to Tirano in Italy. At its top, by the lake at Ospizio Bernina, it is 2,253 metres above the sea. That makes it one of the highest railways in Europe that climbs on its wheels alone, with no toothed rack between the rails.

To manage it, the engineers who built it before 1910 let the track twist. It loops round on itself at Brusio on a round stone bridge, so the train passes under its own track. The [Bernina railway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernina_railway) has been a World Heritage Site since 2008 because of curves like these.

## What you see from the slow train

The first hour is pine forest and wide valleys. Then the trees stop, and the train runs past glaciers and two lakes, one dark and one milky white with ground stone. In winter the snow beside the track is often higher than the windows.

After the pass the line drops almost 1,800 metres in 38 kilometres. You see vineyards and chestnut trees by Poschiavo, and palm trees at Tirano. You start the day in snow and end it in a café in the sun.

## How to ride it well

- **Take the ordinary train.** The panorama train has bigger windows, but you cannot open them. The red regional trains have windows that open, and they cost less.
- **Sit on the right going south.** The best views of the glaciers are on that side.
- **Get off halfway.** Alp Grüm has a small hotel and a terrace over the Palü glacier. The next train comes within the hour.

## Why slow travel still matters

On a slow train you look out of the window because there is nothing else to do. You notice how the forest thins and the air changes. You arrive somewhere new knowing how you got there. That is the whole point of [walking the Amsterdam canals](/blog/amsterdam-canals-walk/) too: the journey is the part you remember.
