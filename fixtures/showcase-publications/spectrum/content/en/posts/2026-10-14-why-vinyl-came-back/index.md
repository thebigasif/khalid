Vinyl records nearly vanished in the early 1990s. Shops cleared their racks for compact discs, and most pressing plants closed. Today vinyl outsells the CD again, year after year. It is the most surprising comeback in music.

## A slow fall and a slow return

The long-playing record arrived in 1948 and ruled music for forty years. Then the CD promised perfect sound that never wore out. By 1993, vinyl was a tiny part of sales, kept alive by DJs and collectors.

The return began quietly in the late 2000s. Record Store Day started in 2008 to help small shops. Each year since, sales of new [phonograph records](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phonograph_record) have grown, and in many countries they now earn more than CDs.

## Why vinyl records came back

Streaming is part of the answer. Once all music was free to play on a phone, owning a piece of it became special again. A record is something to hold, to give, and to put on a shelf where friends can see it.

Then there is the ritual. You take the record from its sleeve, set it down, lower the needle and listen to one side. It is hard to skip a track, so people listen to a whole album, as the musicians meant.

## Does it sound better?

Not always. A CD can hold a wider range of loud and soft than a record can. But many listeners like the warmth of vinyl, and many new records are mastered with care for the format. The sound is different, and different is enough for most fans.

## Who is buying them

Not only older listeners. Many buyers are under thirty, and some do not own a record player at all: they buy the record to support the artist and stream it to listen. Big new albums often sell more on vinyl than on CD in their first week.

## The record shop is back too

The comeback has saved many small shops, and it has given towns a place to meet over a shared interest. The same is happening with another old institution, as Ada writes in [the return of the neighbourhood library](/blog/the-neighbourhood-library/).
