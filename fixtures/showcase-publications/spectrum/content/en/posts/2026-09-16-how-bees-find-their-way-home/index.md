How bees find their way home is a small wonder. A honey bee may fly three kilometres to a field of flowers, zigzag from bloom to bloom, and then fly back to a hole in a tree no wider than a thumb.

## The sun as a compass

Bees use the sun to steer. A bee notes the angle between the sun and the line it flies. Because the sun moves across the sky during the day, the bee also keeps track of time and corrects for it. Bees can do this even when clouds hide the sun, because they see the pattern of polarised light in the blue sky, which we cannot.

## Counting the distance

A bee also measures how far it has flown. It does not count wing beats. Instead it notices how fast the ground seems to slide past its eyes. Scientists tested this by training bees to fly down narrow tunnels with busy patterns on the walls. The bees acted as if they had flown much further than they had.

## How bees find their way with landmarks

Near home, bees switch to landmarks. Young bees take learning flights before they ever collect food. They fly out in growing loops, facing the hive, and remember trees, walls and the shape of the horizon. Move a hive a few metres while the bees are out, and they will hover, lost, at the empty spot.

## The dance that shares the map

When a bee finds good food, it tells the others. On the comb in the dark hive, it runs a figure of eight and waggles its body along the straight part. The angle of the waggle run, compared with straight up, shows the angle to fly compared with the sun. The length of the waggle shows the distance. Karl von Frisch, who worked this out, won a Nobel Prize for it in 1973. The [waggle dance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waggle_dance) is still one of the best known findings in animal behaviour.

## Why it matters to gardeners

Bees remember where food was and return to it. So a garden with flowers from spring to autumn, planted in clumps, keeps them coming back. Asters and ivy are good for late bees, when there is little else in bloom.
