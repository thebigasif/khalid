Efua Asante weaves kente cloth on a narrow loom in Kumasi. Each strip is about ten centimetres wide, and a full cloth is sewn from twenty or more. In this episode she tells Nadia about weaving cloth whose patterns each carry a name, and about the young weavers she now teaches. Kente weaving, she says, is a way of telling stories in thread.

## Show notes: kente weaving

What we talk about:

- Why kente is woven in narrow strips and sewn together.
- How a pattern gets its name.
- Learning from her uncle, who learned from his.
- Teaching weaving to students who arrive with phones full of patterns.

> When someone wears a cloth, they are wearing the story it is named after.

## What we learned about kente weaving

Kente weaving is a language, Efua says, and every pattern is a word in it. Because each strip is narrow, a weaver can change the pattern every few hand widths, so a single cloth can hold several stories. For example, one pattern honours a wise elder and another marks a day of celebration. She learned from her uncle, who learned from his, so she now feels she owes the craft a student or two. As a result, her workshop takes four apprentices a year. They start with plain strips, then learn the named patterns one by one, and only then design their own.

## The transcript

:::toggle{summary="Read the full transcript"}
**Nadia:** Efua, why is the cloth so narrow?

**Efua:** The loom is narrow, so you can sit at it and reach every thread. Then we sew the strips side by side.

**Nadia:** Do the patterns mean something?

**Efua:** Every pattern has a name and a story. Some are proverbs, some are about a person, some are about a day.

**Nadia:** What do your students find hardest?

**Efua:** Slowing down. They want the whole cloth today. I tell them a strip a day is a good day.

**Nadia:** Do your students invent new patterns?

**Efua:** Yes, once they know the old ones. A new pattern needs a name and a story too.
:::
