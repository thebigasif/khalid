Sam Ortega runs a bike shop in Bristol and a free repair day once a month. He has spent fifteen years keeping bikes on the road, most of them old and cheap. In this episode he tells Ben which five fixes cover most broken bikes, and why he would rather teach a fix than do it. Bicycle repair, he says, is easier to learn than most riders think.

## Show notes: bicycle repair

What we talk about:

- The five fixes that cover most broken bikes.
- Why free Saturday fixing sessions are good for a paid shop.
- Truing a wheel by ear.
- Tools every rider should own, and the ones they can skip.

> People come in thinking the bike is finished. Usually it needs air, oil and a cable.

## What we learned about bicycle repair

Bicycle repair is mostly the same five jobs, Sam says: tyres, chains, brake pads, cables and gears. Because most broken bikes are only neglected, a short lesson often gets one back on the road for the cost of a tube. For example, at his last free session forty bikes came in and thirty-four went home the same afternoon. He believes the free sessions help his paid shop too, so he runs one every month. As a result, more people ride all year. His small tool kit for riders is a pump, tyre levers, a multi-tool and a rag.

## The transcript

:::toggle{summary="Read the full transcript"}
**Ben:** Sam, what do people bring on your free Saturdays?

**Sam:** Bikes from the back of the shed. Flat tyres, rusty chains, brakes that squeal.

**Ben:** Are they hard to fix?

**Sam:** Most broken bikes need ten minutes. Air, oil, a new cable, and a brake pad if we are unlucky.

**Ben:** Why teach people instead of just fixing it?

**Sam:** Because the tyre will go flat again next month, and I would like them to still be riding.

**Ben:** Do people come back to the shop afterwards?

**Sam:** Plenty do, for the bigger jobs. Teaching people is the best advert we have.
:::
