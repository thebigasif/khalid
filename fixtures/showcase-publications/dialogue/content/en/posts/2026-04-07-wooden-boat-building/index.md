Joel Farr builds one wooden boat a year in a shed by the water in Falmouth. Each takes about two thousand hours. In this episode he tells Ben how he draws a hull full size on the floor, bends oak frames with steam, and decides when a boat is finished enough to float. Wooden boat building, he says, is mostly patience and sharp tools.

## Show notes: wooden boat building

What we talk about:

- Lofting: drawing the boat full size before cutting any wood.
- Steaming oak so it bends without cracking.
- Why he still uses copper rivets.
- Launch day, and the leaks nobody talks about.

> Every plank has to agree with the one next to it, all the way along.

## What we learned about wooden boat building

Wooden boat building is slow on purpose, Joel says. Because every curve of the hull depends on the one before, he draws the whole boat full size on the floor first, so mistakes show up in pencil rather than in oak. For example, a plank that looks fine on paper may twist when it is steamed. He still fastens planks with copper rivets, because they last and can be replaced one at a time. As a result, a boat takes about two thousand hours. He finds his apprentices through the local sailing club and teaches them to sharpen tools before anything else.

## The transcript

:::toggle{summary="Read the full transcript"}
**Ben:** Joel, how do you start a boat?

**Joel:** On my knees, drawing. We draw the lines full size on the floor so every curve is real before any wood is cut.

**Ben:** And the frames?

**Joel:** Oak, steamed for an hour, then bent while it is hot. You have about two minutes.

**Ben:** How do you know it is finished?

**Joel:** You never quite do. A boat is a thousand small decisions, and at some point the owner needs to go sailing.

**Ben:** How long does it take to learn?

**Joel:** To build a simple dinghy, a year or two. To build a boat you would sail across a bay, about ten.
:::
