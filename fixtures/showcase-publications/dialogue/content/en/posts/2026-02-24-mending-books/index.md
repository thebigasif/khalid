Tomasz Wren spends most of his week mending books. Not rare books, he says, but loved ones: a grandmother's cookbook, a school atlas, a story read aloud a thousand times. In this episode he shows Nadia how he takes a book apart, sews it again and keeps every stain that matters.

## Show notes: mending books

What we talk about:

- Why a loved book always fails at the spine first.
- The difference between restoring a book and mending it.
- Wheat paste, linen thread and other plain materials.
- The book he refused to mend, and why.

> I never make an old book look new. The owner wants their book back, not a stranger.

## What we learned about mending books

Mending books, for Tomasz, starts with a question to the owner: what do you want to keep? Because a loved book carries its history in its marks, he often keeps a torn corner or a pencil note and repairs only what stops the book from opening. For example, a cookbook might get a new spine and new thread, while every stain stays. He uses plain, reversible materials, so a later binder can undo his work without harm. As a result, his mending is often invisible. He also runs a short evening course, because many small repairs are easy to learn with a bone folder, a needle and some patience.

## The transcript

:::toggle{summary="Read the full transcript"}
**Nadia:** Tomasz, what is the most common book people bring you?

**Tomasz:** Cookbooks. They are opened flat every day, near water and butter, for forty years.

**Nadia:** Where do they break?

**Tomasz:** A loved book wears out at the spine. The thread gives up first, then the cover.

**Nadia:** Do you clean the pages?

**Tomasz:** Only what harms the paper. A gravy stain on page ninety is part of the family. I leave it.

**Nadia:** What is the easiest repair to learn?

**Tomasz:** A loose page. Some wheat paste, a strip of thin paper and a heavy book on top overnight.
:::
