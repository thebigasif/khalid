Ruth Calloway builds walls without mortar across the Yorkshire Dales. A good waller lays about a metre a day, she says, and a good wall stands for two hundred years. In this episode she tells Ben how she reads a pile of stone, why she works in almost any weather, and what a wall gives back to a field. Dry stone walling, she says, is mostly looking before you lift.

## Show notes: dry stone walling

What we talk about:

- How a dry stone wall stands up with no mortar at all.

- What walls give to sheep, birds and small plants.

> A wall is two walls leaning on each other, tied together with long stones.

## What we learned about dry stone walling

Dry stone walling needs no mortar, because weight and friction hold the stones together. A wall is two faces that lean on each other, so long through stones tie the faces and small hearting stones fill the middle. For example, Ruth sorts a pile into sizes before she lays a single stone, which saves her back and her day. She builds about a metre a day and works in nearly any weather. As a result, she has rebuilt over a mile of wall in the Dales. A good wall, she says, gives back for two hundred years, because it shelters sheep, birds and small plants alike.

## The transcript

:::toggle{summary="Read the full transcript"}
**Ben:** Ruth, how does a wall stand up with no mortar?

**Ruth:** Weight and friction. It is really two walls leaning together, tied with long through stones.

**Ben:** How do you choose the next stone?

**Ruth:** You look before you lift. Pick up each stone only once. If you put one back on the pile, you have wasted your back.

**Ben:** Do you stop for rain?

**Ruth:** For lightning, yes. For rain, no. The Dales would never get a wall built.

**Ben:** How do you learn to read the pile?

**Ruth:** By getting it wrong for a few years. So I tell apprentices to sort before they build.
:::
