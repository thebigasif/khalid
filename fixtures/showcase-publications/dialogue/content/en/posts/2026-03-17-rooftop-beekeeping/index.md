Ada Mensah keeps bees on a city roof above a library in Leeds, and on four more roofs nearby. She started with one hive and a borrowed suit. In this episode she tells Nadia where city bees find their flowers, what she does in the cold months, and why her calendar now follows the hives. Rooftop beekeeping, she says, is easier than most people think.

## Show notes: rooftop beekeeping

What we talk about:

- Why city bees often do better than country bees.
- The weekly hive check, step by step.
- What a colony needs to get through winter.
- Selling honey to the neighbours who planted the flowers.

> The bees found lime trees I had walked past for ten years without noticing.

## What we learned about rooftop beekeeping

Rooftop beekeeping works, Ada says, because cities are full of flowers that nobody counts. Gardens, parks and street trees bloom one after another, so city bees often have food for longer than bees on farmland. Even so, a roof brings its own problems. For example, wind dries a hive out, and every box has to be carried up a ladder. She checks each hive once a week from April to August, and in winter she simply leaves the bees alone. As a result, her honey tastes different on each roof. She sells it to the neighbours, who planted many of the flowers in the first place.

## The transcript

:::toggle{summary="Read the full transcript"}
**Nadia:** Ada, is a roof a good place for bees?

**Ada:** A good roof is. It needs shelter from the wind, water nearby and a safe way up for me.

**Nadia:** Where do they find flowers in a city?

**Ada:** Gardens, parks, railway banks and street trees. In July it is almost all lime trees.

**Nadia:** How has it changed your year?

**Ada:** Bees have no interest in your diary. In May I check the hives every week, whatever else is happening.

**Nadia:** What do you do in winter?

**Ada:** Almost nothing. I check the hive is heavy enough, so I know they have food, and then I leave them be.
:::
