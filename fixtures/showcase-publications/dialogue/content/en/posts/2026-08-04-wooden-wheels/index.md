Marta Vidal makes wooden wheels by hand, about one a fortnight. Her customers are museums, farm parks and people restoring old carts. In this episode she walks Ben through the whole job: turning the hub, cutting the spokes, fitting the rim and shrinking a red-hot iron tyre onto it in one go.

## Show notes: how wooden wheels come together

What we talk about:

- Why the hub is elm, the spokes oak and the rim ash.
- The angle that makes a cart wheel dish outwards, and why it matters.
- Tyring day, when the whole village used to come and help.
- How she found the last wheelwright in her region and asked to learn.

> The tyre goes on hot and comes off the fire in seconds. If you hesitate, you start again.

## What we learned about wooden wheels

Wooden wheels use three woods, Marta says, because each part does a different job. The hub is elm, so it will not split when twelve spokes are driven into it. The spokes are oak for strength, and the rim is ash, because ash bends without breaking. For example, a farm cart wheel has a slight dish, so it leans outwards and stands up to the sideways push of a turning cart. As a result, a well made wheel can run for fifty years. Most of her work now comes from museums, so she spends as much time studying old wheels as making new ones.

## The transcript

:::toggle{summary="Read the full transcript"}
**Ben:** Marta, there are wheels hanging from every beam in here.

**Marta:** Those are the patterns. When a museum sends me a broken wheel, I hang it up until I understand it.

**Ben:** Where do you start?

**Marta:** With the hub. A wheel is only as true as its hub. If the mortises for the spokes are a degree out, you see it at the rim.

**Ben:** How long does a wheel take?

**Marta:** About sixty hours, so two weeks with everything else in the yard.

**Ben:** And the tyre itself?

**Marta:** We heat the iron ring until it glows, drop it over the rim and pour water on it. It shrinks and pulls every joint tight. It takes four people and about ninety seconds.
:::
