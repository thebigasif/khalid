Hello again. The last tomatoes are ripening on the windowsill, and before they all go into a sauce, keep one back. Saving tomato seed is the cheapest job in the garden. One good fruit gives you more seed than a packet, and it comes from a plant that you know grew well where you live.

## Pick the right tomato

Choose a fruit from your best plant, fully ripe and a little soft. Only save seed from open-pollinated kinds, the old ones with names such as Gardener's Delight or Moneymaker. Seed from an F1 hybrid, which the packet marks, will not grow true: the plants next year will be a mix of its parents.

## Saving tomato seed, step by step

1. Cut the tomato across its middle and squeeze the seeds and their jelly into a clean jam jar.
2. Add the same amount of water and write the name of the tomato on the lid.
3. Leave the jar on a warm windowsill for three to five days. A grey skin of mould forms on top. That is what you want: it eats the jelly around each seed.
4. Pour off the mould and the floating seeds, which are empty. Rinse the good seeds that sank in a sieve.
5. Spread them on a ceramic plate, not paper, and leave them to dry for a week.

When they rattle, tip them into a paper envelope and keep it somewhere cool and dry. Tomato seed keeps for four years or more.

## Why ferment the seed

The jelly around each seed holds back germination, so the seed does not sprout inside the fruit. Fermenting removes it and also kills some of the diseases that live on the seed coat. You can skip it and dry the seeds with their jelly on, but more of them will fail to come up.

## Sowing in spring

Sow in March on a warm windowsill, as Maren described in the issue on [windowsill growing](/blog/winter-windowsill-herbs/). If you have more seed than you need, swap it. The [Heritage Seed Library](https://www.gardenorganic.org.uk/hsl) keeps old kinds alive the same way, and many towns hold a seed swap in February.
