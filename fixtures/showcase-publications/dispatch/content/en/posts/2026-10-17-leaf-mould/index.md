Good morning. Every autumn the trees in my street drop enough leaves to fill my balcony pots twice over, and every autumn the council sweeps them up and takes them away. This year, take some for yourself. Leaf mould is crumbly, dark and sweet smelling, and it is one of the best things you can add to a pot.

## What leaf mould is

Leaf mould is what fallen leaves become after a year or two of slow rotting. A compost heap rots fast with the help of bacteria. Leaves rot slowly with the help of fungi, which is why they need time and damp, not heat. The result holds water like a sponge and opens up heavy compost, so it is ideal for seed sowing and for topping pots.

## How to make leaf mould in a bag

You do not need a bin or a garden. A strong bin bag on a balcony works.

1. Collect leaves after rain, from a quiet street or a park, away from busy roads.
2. Pack them into a bin bag. If they are dry, add a jug of water and shake.
3. Tie the bag and stab a dozen holes in it with a fork.
4. Stand it in a corner out of the way and forget it for a year.

Oak, beech and hornbeam leaves rot well. The leaves under the [late flowers](/blog/late-flowers/) pot can go in too. Leave out thick evergreen leaves, such as laurel, which take years.

## Using it

After one year the leaves are dark and crumbly, ready to spread on top of pots as a mulch. After two years they are fine enough to sieve and mix half and half with compost for sowing seed. I used last year's bags on the [tulips planted in layers](/blog/layered-bulbs/) last week.

## Is it safe to take leaves?

Yes, on a public pavement. The people sweeping them up are glad of the help. In a park, ask the gardeners first. The Royal Horticultural Society has a short [guide to leaf mould](https://www.rhs.org.uk/soil-composts-mulches/leafmould) if you want to read more.
