A good practice schedule for an adult learning to code is about thirty minutes on five days a week, stopping while you still want to carry on. Short, regular sessions help you remember far more than one long session, and they fit around a job and a family.

We have watched more than a thousand adults learn in our evening classes. The ones who reach the end of the path are not the ones with the most free time. They are the ones who keep a practice schedule of little and often, and who know what they will do before they sit down.

## Why short sessions work better

Learning a skill happens partly while you sleep, when your brain sorts out what you did that day. Psychologists call this the [spacing effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spacing_effect). Five short sessions give you five nights of sorting. One long Saturday gives you one. Short sessions also make starting easier: thirty minutes feels possible on a tired Tuesday, three hours does not.

:::callout{kind=stat}
**3 hours a week** was the median practice time of the students in our spring 2026 group who put a website online by the last class. Those who practised in one weekly block were half as likely to finish as those who spread the same time over the week.
:::

## A practice schedule that works

This is the practice schedule we suggest to new students. Move the days around to suit you, but keep the shape.

| Day | Time | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Monday | 30 min | Read the next guide on the path and try its first steps |
| Tuesday | 30 min | Finish the guide's steps and write down what confused you |
| Wednesday | Rest | Nothing. Let it settle. |
| Thursday | 30 min | Do the guide again from memory, without looking |
| Friday | 30 min | Change the example to make it your own |
| Saturday | 60 min | Work on your own project with what you learned |
| Sunday | Rest | Nothing, or read your learning log |

That adds up to three and a half hours. If your practice schedule can only hold three days, keep Monday, Thursday and Saturday.

## Plan each session before you sit down

Most of a wasted session is spent deciding what to do. Decide the day before.

::::howto{name="Run a thirty-minute practice session" time="0:30:00"}
:::step{name="Read yesterday's note"}
Open your learning log and read the last entry: what you did and what confused you. Two minutes.
:::
:::step{name="Do one small thing"}
Pick one step from the current guide, or one change to your project. Work on only that for twenty minutes.
:::
:::step{name="Stop on time"}
Set a timer and stop when it rings, even in the middle of something. Stopping mid-task makes the next session easier to start.
:::
:::step{name="Write two lines"}
Note what you did and what is still unclear. Write tomorrow's first task at the bottom.
:::
::::

:::callout{kind=tip}
Leave your laptop open on the file you were working on, or write the file's name in your log. Finding your place again is the hardest minute of any session.
:::

## Type the code, do not copy it

When a guide shows you code, type it out yourself rather than copying and pasting. Typing is slower, and that is the point. You notice each bracket and quote, you make small mistakes and you fix them. Students who type every example make fewer mistakes in their own projects a month later.

## What to do on a bad day

Some evenings nothing works. The example will not run, the words blur and you start to wonder whether coding is for you. Every student we have taught has had these evenings, including the ones who now work as developers.

On a bad day:

1. Shrink the task. Do one step, not the whole guide.
2. Go back to something you already know and do it again. It reminds you how far you have come.
3. Write down exactly what you were stuck on and stop. Fresh eyes the next day solve most problems in minutes.

:::callout{kind=note}
If you miss a week, do not try to catch up by doubling your practice schedule. Pick up where you stopped, with your usual thirty minutes. The habit matters more than the lost days.
:::

## Find someone to learn with

Learning alone is possible, but a friend makes it easier. Find one other person who wants to learn, and agree to show each other what you made at the end of every week. You do not need to learn at the same speed. You need someone who will ask, "What did you build this week?"
