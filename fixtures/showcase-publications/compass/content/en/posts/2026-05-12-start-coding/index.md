To start coding as an adult, pick one small thing you want to build, such as a one-page website about your hobby, and learn only what that thing needs, in a fixed order. Practise for half an hour most days rather than for a whole weekend once a month, and expect to feel lost for the first few weeks.

That is the whole method. The rest of this guide explains each part and shows you the route we teach at Northbound, stop by stop. You need no maths beyond adding up, no special computer and no money.

::toc

:::callout{kind=summary}
- **Start with a goal you can see.** A small website is the best first project, because you can open it and show it to someone.
- **Follow one route.** Jumping between languages and courses is the most common reason adults give up.
- **Little and often.** Thirty minutes on five days beats five hours on one.
- **Being confused is normal.** It means you are at the edge of what you know, which is where learning happens.
:::

## Why adults find coding hard at first

Adults are good learners. You already know how to plan, how to look things up and how to keep going when a task is dull. What makes coding hard at first is the words. Guides use terms like "server", "syntax" and "repository" as if everyone knew them, and a beginner cannot tell which ones matter yet.

The second problem is choice. There are hundreds of languages and thousands of courses. Most adults we meet have started three or four of them and finished none. That is not a lack of talent. It is a lack of a route.

## Start coding with one small goal

Before you start coding, write down one thing you want to make. Keep it small enough to finish in two months of evenings.

Good first goals:

- a one-page website about your allotment, your choir or your bike rides;
- a page that lists your favourite recipes with a photo of each;
- a simple online CV with your work history and a way to contact you.

A website is the best first goal for most people. It needs only a browser and a text editor, both free, and every step shows you a visible result. It also teaches you the ideas behind almost every other kind of programming.

:::callout{kind=tip}
Write your goal on a sticky note and put it where you study. When a guide wanders off into something your goal does not need, skip that part for now.
:::

## Follow the route, one stop at a time

This is the route we teach, in order. Each stop builds on the one before it, so try not to skip ahead.

| Stop | What you learn | Time it took our spring group |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Start here | Your first program, reading errors, a practice habit | 2 weeks |
| 2. The web | How pages travel, HTML, CSS, a little JavaScript | 4 weeks |
| 3. Tools | The terminal, a code editor, Git, developer tools | 2 weeks |
| 4. Your first project | Plan, build, adapt for phones, put it online | 3 weeks |

After the last stop, [what to learn next](/blog/learn-next/) helps you choose a route of your own.

The times are for students who practised about three hours a week. Take longer if you need to. Nobody is timing you.

::::howto{name="Start learning to code this week" time="2:30:00"}
:::step{name="Write down your goal"}
One sentence on a sticky note: what you want to build and who it is for.
:::
:::step{name="Book your practice times"}
Put five half-hour slots in your diary for the coming week, at times you can keep. Our [practice schedule](/blog/practice-schedule/) shows a week that works.
:::
:::step{name="Write your first program"}
Follow the first program guide linked at the end of this page. You need only the browser you are reading this in.
:::
:::step{name="Break it on purpose"}
Change one character in your program and read the error it gives. Then read [how to read error messages](/blog/read-error-messages/).
:::
:::step{name="Keep a learning log"}
After each session, write two lines in a notebook: what you did and what confused you. Next session, start with the confusing part.
:::

## What you do not need to start coding

Many adults put off the moment they start coding because they think they need something they do not have.

- **You do not need a new computer.** Any laptop or desktop from the last ten years will do. A tablet is harder, but it works for the first few stops.
- **You do not need to be good at maths.** Building websites uses less maths than splitting a restaurant bill.
- **You do not need to pick the "best" language.** For the web there is no choice to make: HTML, CSS and JavaScript are what browsers understand.
- **You do not need to pay.** Every tool on this path is free, and the [web docs from the Mozilla community](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Learn) are a free reference you can use alongside it.

## How to know you are making progress

Progress in coding is hard to feel, because each new thing makes you aware of three more you do not know. Look back instead of forward, to the week you decided to start coding. Once a fortnight, open your learning log and read your first entries. The things that confused you in week one will look simple.

:::callout{kind=stat}
**71%** of the 48 adults in our spring 2026 evening group had started at least one coding course before and stopped. Of those who followed the route in order, 39 put a website online by the last week.
:::

## Your next stop

Open [your first program](/blog/first-program/) now, while your browser is open. The best way to start coding is to type something and press Enter. It takes ten minutes, and by the end you will have told a computer to do something and watched it obey.
