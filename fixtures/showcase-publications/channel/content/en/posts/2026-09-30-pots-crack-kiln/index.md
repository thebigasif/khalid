When pots crack in the kiln, the crack itself tells you why. In this video, Tomas lays out five pots from our failed firings and reads each crack like a clue.

## Read the crack first

Look at the edges of the crack. Sharp, clean edges with glaze that has not run into them mean the crack happened as the kiln cooled. Soft, rounded edges with glaze flowed into them mean it happened before or early in the firing.

## Five reasons pots crack

1. **The pot was not dry.** Water turns to steam and forces the clay apart. Fire only bone dry pots, and hold the kiln below 100 degrees for the first two hours.
2. **The floor was too thick.** A thick floor dries slower than the walls and splits in an S shape. Press the floor down when you [open the clay](/blog/first-bowl-pulling-walls/).
3. **The walls were uneven.** Thick and thin parts shrink at different rates. Trim to an even wall, as Noor does in [trimming the foot](/blog/first-bowl-trimming-foot-ring/).
4. **Cooling was too fast.** Opening the kiln early cracks pots with a sharp sound. Wait until the kiln is below 100 degrees.
5. **The glaze did not fit.** A glaze that shrinks less than the clay can pull a thin pot apart. Test a new glaze on a tile first.

## What we changed

Most of our cracks were the first two. We now dry pots under plastic for two days and fire them only when they feel room warm, not cool. A pot that feels cool is still giving off water.

Since we made these changes, we lose about one pot in sixty, down from one in twelve. Tomas keeps a firing log of every load, and we will share it in a later video.

## When pots crack after the firing

Some cracks appear days later. A fine web of lines in the glaze is crazing: the glaze shrinks more than the clay as it cools. It is a glaze fit problem, not a firing one. A clean crack that rings when you tap the pot is often from a cold drink poured into a hot mug. [Thermal shock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_shock) is worse in thick bases, which is one more reason to trim the floor even.

If you keep a log like ours, write down where each cracked pot sat in the kiln. After a few firings, a pattern shows up, and the fix is often a shelf move, not a new clay.
