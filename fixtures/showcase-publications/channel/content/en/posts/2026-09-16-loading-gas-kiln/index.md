Loading a gas kiln well is half of a good firing. In this video, Tomas packs our gas kiln for a cone 10 glaze firing and explains each choice as he makes it.

## Check every pot

Before a pot goes in, Tomas turns it over. The foot must be clean, with no glaze on it. He runs a finger round the rim for chips and holds it to the light for thin spots. A pot with glaze on its foot goes back to Noor's bench.

## Setting the shelves

Our kiln holds four levels of shelves. Each shelf stands on three posts, never four, so it cannot rock. Posts on each level sit right above the posts below, so the weight goes straight down to the floor.

- Put tall pots on the bottom shelf, where the kiln is coolest.
- Put the celadon bowls in the middle, where the reduction is most even.
- Keep a shelf near the top for test tiles, so you learn something from every firing.

## Packing the gas kiln with room to breathe

A gas kiln heats with moving flame. If you pack it too tight, the flame cannot reach the back, and pots there come out dull. Tomas leaves a gap of about two fingers between pots and a wider gap at the burner ports. Pots must not touch, or the glaze will join them.

## Cones and the door

Three cones stand on a pad of clay in front of the spy hole: cone 9, cone 10 and cone 11. When cone 10 bends over, the firing is done. Mugs like those in [pulling handles](/blog/pulling-handles-mugs/) go on the top shelf. Tomas bricks up the door, checks the seal with a lighter flame and lights the burners in the evening.

The firing takes about eleven hours, and the kiln cools for two days. If a pot cracks, our video on [why pots crack in the kiln](/blog/pots-crack-kiln/) helps you find the cause. [Pyrometric cones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyrometric_cone) are explained well on Wikipedia.
