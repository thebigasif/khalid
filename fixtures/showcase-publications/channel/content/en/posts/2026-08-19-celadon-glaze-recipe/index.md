A celadon glaze is a clear glaze with a little iron in it. In a reduction firing, the iron turns a soft green that pools darker where the glaze runs thick. In this video, Tomas mixes our studio celadon from raw materials and tests it on a tile before it goes near a bowl.

## The celadon glaze recipe

Our celadon is a simple four-part base with a small iron addition. The table gives a 1,000 gram batch, enough to dip about twenty bowls.

| Material | Percent | Grams for a 1,000 g batch | What it does |
|---|---|---|---|
| Potash feldspar | 40 | 400 | Melts the glaze and gives it body |
| Silica | 30 | 300 | Forms the glass |
| Whiting | 20 | 200 | Helps the glaze melt at cone 10 |
| China clay | 10 | 100 | Keeps the glaze in suspension and on the pot |
| Red iron oxide | 1 | 10 | Gives the green in reduction |

Iron is the part to be careful with. At half a percent, the glaze is almost clear. At two percent, it turns olive. One percent gives the pale green you see in the video.

## Weighing and sieving

Wear a mask while you weigh dry materials. Weigh each one onto paper and tip it into a bucket with about 900 millilitres of water. Let it soak for ten minutes, then stir. Pass the glaze twice through an 80 mesh sieve, pushing it through with a stiff brush.

## Testing before you glaze

Never glaze a whole load with an untested batch. Tomas dips a test tile, scratches the glaze to check its thickness and fires it with the next kiln load. A good celadon is about as thick as a credit card on the pot.

When the test tile looks right, the glaze is ready. We use the same glaze on the mugs in [pulling handles for mugs](/blog/pulling-handles-mugs/).
