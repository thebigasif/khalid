For about ten minutes after sunrise, first light touches only the tops of the tallest dunes. The valley floor stays blue and cold, and every ridge turns into a line of warm colour. This picture was made in those ten minutes, from the far bank of Medano Creek.

## Be there before first light

The dunes at Great Sand Dunes sit at the foot of the Sangre de Cristo Mountains. The sun comes up over those mountains, so it reaches the dunes a little after the official sunrise time. Walk out in the dark with a head torch and a warm layer. Medano Creek is shallow in autumn, and the far bank is a good place to wait.

## One ridge, not the whole field

It is tempting to show the whole dune field. A single ridge works better. The lit side and the shaded side meet in one clean line, and that line carries the picture. Use a long lens, stand back, and let the ridge fill most of the frame.

## Shade is part of the picture

The shade is not empty. Blue light from the sky fills it, so the cold side of the dune keeps its ripples and texture. Expose for the bright sand and let the shade go a little dark. The warm and cool halves are what make the picture work.

## When to go

Autumn is the best season. The creek is low, the air is clear, and the mornings are quiet. Spring brings more water in the creek and more people on the sand. Summer mornings are short, because the sun climbs fast and the colour fades within minutes.

Read the park's own [page on visiting the dunes](https://www.nps.gov/grsa/planyourvisit/index.htm) before you go, and check the weather the night before: cloud over the mountains can block the sun for the whole first hour. The same sand looks very different two hours later, as [the dune ridgelines in the morning](/blog/dune-ridgelines-morning/) show.
