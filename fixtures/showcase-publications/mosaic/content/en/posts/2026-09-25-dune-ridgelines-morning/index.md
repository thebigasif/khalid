Two hours after sunrise, the light at Great Sand Dunes is still low. Each dune throws a long shadow down its far side, so the whole field turns into ridgelines. This is the time to show the shape of the dunes, not their colour.

## Ridgelines, not a view

From the road, the dune field looks flat and pale in the middle of the day. In the morning it fills with lines, one behind another. A longer lens stacks the ridgelines up, so the far dunes look close to the near ones and the field becomes a pattern.

## The mountain on top

The peak behind the dunes caught the sun first and still glows. Leaving a strip of mountain and cloud at the top tells you how big the dunes are. Without it, they could be ripples in a sandbox, with nothing to give them a size.

## Shoot from the road

You do not need to walk far for this picture. The road to the visitor centre has several places to stop. That makes it a good first morning, before you know your way around the sand and how long each walk takes.

## Coming back

The light changes fast in the first hours. Come back on another day and the same dunes will look different with a few clouds or a dusting of snow. Try the same spot at the same time on two mornings, and keep the picture where the lines are cleanest.

## What to bring

Bring a lens of at least 200 mm, a tripod for the slow start, and a cloth for the dust. Wind lifts fine sand by mid morning, and it gets into every gap of a camera.

The park's [photography tips](https://www.nps.gov/grsa/learn/photosmultimedia/index.htm) list good places to stand. The same dunes at the first minute of the day are in [first light on the dunes at sunrise](/blog/first-light-dunes/). Another field of lines is [the stripes of Blue Mesa](/blog/blue-mesa-stripes/).
