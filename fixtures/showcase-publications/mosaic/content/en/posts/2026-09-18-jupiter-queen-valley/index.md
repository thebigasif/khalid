Queen Valley is one of the darkest places in Joshua Tree. On a moonless night the sky is full of stars down to the horizon. The brightest point in this picture is not a star at all: it is Jupiter.

## Shapes against the Queen Valley sky

The Joshua trees are not lit. They are black shapes against the sky, and that is enough. Their spiky branches are easy to read, even with no detail inside them. Choose trees that stand apart, so their outlines do not merge into one dark mass.

## A wide view

A wide lens takes in both trees and a large part of the sky. The band of stars crosses the top of the frame. Keep the horizon low, so the stars get most of the picture, and keep it level, since a tilted horizon is the first thing people notice.

![The Milky Way rising over a sandy trail between low shrubs, seen in a tall frame](dune-trail.jpg)

## Find the planets

Planets do not twinkle like stars, and they move against the stars from month to month. A free sky chart shows where they are each night. Jupiter here gave the picture a bright point that pulls the eye to the middle, between the two trees.

## Getting to Queen Valley

The valley lies along the park's main road, with dirt pull-outs where you can leave the car. Arrive before dark so you can choose your trees in daylight and learn the ground. Mark the spot on your phone, since the trees all look alike once the sun is gone.

## Dark sky rules

Use a red light, keep it pointed down, and turn off car lights early. The park is an International Dark Sky Park, and its page on [night skies](https://www.nps.gov/jotr/learn/nature/nightskies.htm) has the dates of new moons.
