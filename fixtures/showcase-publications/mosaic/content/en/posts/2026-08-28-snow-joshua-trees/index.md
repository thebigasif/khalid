Snow falls at Joshua Tree perhaps once or twice a winter, and it rarely lasts the morning. On this day it fell steadily, and the trees near Keys View faded into the grey one row at a time. Joshua trees in snow look like a different plant: softer, darker and very still.

## Let the weather do the work

Falling snow works like fog. The nearest tree is dark and sharp. The ones behind it get paler with every step back. That fading gives a flat place a feeling of depth that a clear day never shows. Expose a little brighter than the camera suggests, or the snow turns grey, and check the screen often as the snow gets heavier or lighter.

## Joshua trees in snow need a simple frame

One strong tree in front is enough. Put it a little to one side and leave the grey around it. A busy frame loses the quiet that makes snow special, so leave out the road, the signs and the other people.

## Protect the camera

Snow melts on a warm lens. Keep a cloth in your pocket and a hood on the lens, and put the camera in a bag before you go back into a warm car. The bag catches the water that forms as the camera warms up.

## Be quick

Desert snow melts fast once the cloud thins. The best pictures come while it is still falling, often in the first hour of light. By late morning the ground is wet sand again and the trees are bare.

## Roads may close

The road to Keys View climbs to more than fifteen hundred metres and can close when it snows. Check the park's [current conditions](https://www.nps.gov/jotr/planyourvisit/conditions.htm) before you drive up.
