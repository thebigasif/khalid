Every month, Port City Wire sends a reporter to at least six council meetings, and every month a reader asks me how we decide what to write about them. This note sets out how our council reporting works, what we promise readers and how you can check whether we kept that promise. I have edited this paper since 2021, and before that I covered the council for twelve years.

The short answer is that good council reporting is slow work: we read the papers, we go to the meetings, and we tell you where every fact came from.

## Our council reporting, step by step

1. **We read the papers first.** Every committee publishes its agenda and reports a week before it meets. Owen Reyland, who covers the council, reads them all and marks anything that changes what people pay, what they can use or where they can go.
2. **We go to the meeting.** A report says what officers recommend. The meeting is where councillors change it. Last month, the transport committee threw out a plan to raise [parking charges](/blog/parking-charges-vote/), which the papers had made look settled.
3. **We ask both sides.** If a vote is split, we ask someone from each side why they voted as they did, and we print both answers.
4. **We name the source.** Every figure in a council story comes from a named report, meeting or person. If we worked a number out ourselves, such as the weekly cost of a council tax rise, we say so.
5. **A second person reads it.** Every story is checked by another member of the newsroom before it goes live. Their name appears on the story.

## What we do not do

- We do not take gifts, trips or meals from the council, councillors or companies bidding for council work.
- We do not let anyone we write about read a story before it runs. We will read a quote back to the person who said it, if they ask.
- We do not print claims we cannot check. If a councillor says a scheme "will save millions", we ask for the figures, and if they cannot give them, we say that.

:::callout{kind=note}
Councillors, officers and their families are not allowed to write for this paper. Our columnist Gil Penhale, who writes about everyday life such as [the ferry queue](/blog/ferry-queue-column/), was a harbour pilot and has never held office.
:::

## How to check our work

You do not have to trust us. Council papers are public, and most meetings are streamed online. When we write about the [council budget for 2027](/blog/council-budget/), every figure in our tables can be found in the draft budget the council published on 5 October. If you find one that does not match, tell us.

## When we get it wrong

Council reporting involves a great many names, dates and figures. We make mistakes, and when we do we fix them in the story itself, with a note at the foot that says what changed and when. We do not quietly edit a story and hope nobody noticed. In the past year, we corrected nine stories, most of them for a wrong date or a misspelled name. Our standards follow the common ideas of [journalism ethics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Journalism_ethics_and_standards): be accurate, be fair, be open about where things come from.

If you think we have been unfair to you or got something wrong, write to the newsdesk. I read every complaint myself, and I reply to each one within a week. You can find our address on the [about page](/blog/about/).
