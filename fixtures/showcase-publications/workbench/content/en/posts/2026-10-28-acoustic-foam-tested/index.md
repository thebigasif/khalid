Thin acoustic foam helps a small room less than most people expect: in our tests, 24 tiles of 2.5 cm foam cut the echo by about a quarter, while a rug, curtains and a full bookcase cut it by a third. Four thick mineral wool panels did best of all, cutting the echo by more than half.

Echo, or reverberation, is the sound that keeps bouncing around a room after you stop talking. A microphone hears it as a hollow, distant tone. We measured how long it lasted in our bare spare room, then tried four ways to shorten it, from acoustic foam tiles to thick panels.

## The room and how we measured

Our spare room is 3.2 by 3.6 metres, with a hard floor, plain plaster walls and one large window, much like a typical spare bedroom. We measured reverberation time, the time a loud sound takes to fade by 60 dB, with a calibrated microphone and a test signal. A shorter time means less echo. A good voice booth sits near 0.2 seconds, and a bare room like ours sits near 0.6.

We also recorded the same script through the [Larch 24, our favourite condenser](/blog/larch-24-review/), after each change, and our panel rated the room pickup from 1 to 10. [Our measuring kit](/blog/gear-testing/) is listed on our methods page.

## What the figures mean

**Thin acoustic foam only works on high sounds.** The 2.5 cm tiles soaked up the hiss and sparkle of the echo but did almost nothing below 250 Hz, where the boom of a low voice lives. Their reverberation time in that range barely moved, from 0.71 to 0.69 seconds. The room sounded less bright, but still boxy.

**Soft furnishing does more than you think.** A thick rug, heavy curtains over the window and a full bookcase on the wall behind the speaker shortened the echo by a third. A bookcase full of books of different depths scatters sound well. You may own most of this already.

**Thick panels are the real fix.** Four panels of 10 cm mineral wool in fabric covers, placed at the first reflection points either side of the speaker and behind them, cut the echo by more than half. They worked in the low range too, where the foam failed.

:::callout{kind=tip}
To find the first reflection points, sit in your recording seat and have a friend slide a mirror along the side wall. Wherever you can see the microphone in the mirror, a panel will do the most good.
:::

## Is acoustic foam worth buying?

For a voice, acoustic foam is rarely worth buying on its own. It is cheap and easy to fit, but it treats the high sounds of the echo and not the low boom of speech. A heavy rug and thick curtains do more for the money, and four thick panels change a room more than any microphone upgrade.

:::proscons
+ Acoustic foam is cheap, light and easy to fit
+ It takes the harsh edge off a bright room
- It does almost nothing for the low boom of a voice
- 24 tiles cut the echo by only about a quarter
- Thick panels did twice as much for a similar effort
:::

## What this means for your microphone

The room and the microphone work together. In the bare room, the [Wren D7, our dynamic pick](/blog/wren-d7-review/), sounded better than the Larch 24, because it hears less of the echo. With four panels, rug and curtains, the Larch 24 scored 8.9 for room pickup and our panel preferred it. If you plan to treat your room, a condenser becomes a good choice. If not, buy a dynamic microphone and skip the acoustic foam.
