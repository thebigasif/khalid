You can build a good home podcast studio for $342, $690 or $1,459 at 2026 prices, and every piece on this page passed our bench tests. The biggest jump in sound comes from the first step up, when you move from a USB microphone to a dynamic microphone and an interface; after that, money buys a better room more than better gear.

Each home podcast studio kit is for one host with a guest who joins over the internet. For two hosts in one room, double the microphone, arm and headphones, and see the note at the end.

## Starter kit: a home podcast studio on a laptop

The Alder U1, from our [round-up of podcast microphones](/blog/best-podcast-microphones/), is a dynamic microphone with a USB socket, so it plugs straight into a laptop and you need no interface. Being dynamic, it hears little of the room, which matters more than anything in an untreated space. Put it on a desk boom arm, not the stand in the box: our tests showed the stand passes every desk tap into the recording.

The [Kestrel M50 closed-back headphones](/blog/kestrel-m50-review/) are in every kit. They leak nothing into the microphone and play voices evenly enough to edit on, so you will not need to replace them as you grow.

For video, the Gorse B30 is a silent, battery-powered panel. Set it at 45 degrees to your face, slightly above eye level.

:::proscons
+ One cable to a laptop; set up in ten minutes
+ Dynamic microphone ignores an untreated room
+ The microphone also has XLR, so it moves with you to the next kit
- Thinner voice sound over USB
- A single panel is dim beyond arm's length
:::

## Step up: the home podcast studio most people need

This is the home podcast studio we recommend to most people, and the step that changes the sound most. The Wren D7, which won our [dynamic microphone review](/blog/wren-d7-review/), is warm, clear and deaf to the room. It needs plenty of gain, and the [Osprey Two two-input interface](/blog/osprey-two-review/) gives it 58 dB with no audible hiss.

The cable is the Teal Standard. Our [XLR cable test](/blog/xlr-cables-tested/) found that dearer cables sound no different.

The [Plover L60 key light](/blog/plover-l60-review/) with a 60 cm softbox lights a face softly at one metre and stays quiet enough to sit beside the microphone in its quiet mode. If you film yourself, a [fluid video head](/blog/video-tripod-heads/) on a tripod keeps the camera steady.

:::figure{src="softbox-ring.jpg" alt="A hand holding the white mounting ring of a softbox for a home podcast studio light, with the steel rods of the frame fixed into its slots and the silver lining below"}
The softbox's rods slot into a mounting ring, which then twists onto the light. Assembly takes about five minutes the first time.
:::

:::proscons
+ Biggest jump in voice quality, from 6.8 to 8.2
+ Clean preamps with room to grow to two microphones
+ A real key light with a softbox
- More cables and a bigger desk footprint
- The light needs mains power and an extension lead
:::

## Full studio: spend on the room

At this level, most of the extra money goes into the room, not the gear. Four thick mineral wool panels, a rug and heavy curtains cut the echo in our spare room by two thirds, as our [measurements of foam and panels](/blog/acoustic-foam-tested/) showed. In a treated room, a condenser microphone stops being a risk and becomes the best choice.

The [Larch 24 large-diaphragm condenser](/blog/larch-24-review/) gives a richer, more detailed voice than any dynamic microphone we have tested, with very low noise. It is heavy, so it needs a heavy-duty boom arm.

For video, the Moth 100 is our pick in [the best video lights for small rooms](/blog/video-lights-small-rooms/). It is bright and accurate and can match any room's colour, with the Gorse B30 from the starter kit moving to the other side as a fill light.

:::proscons
+ Best voice quality in our tests, 9.0 out of 10
+ A treated room makes every future recording better
+ Two-light setup for video
- Most of the cost is room treatment, which takes an afternoon to fit
- The condenser exposes any noise outside the room, such as traffic
:::

## Which home podcast studio should you build?

Build the step-up kit if you publish every week, because it gives the biggest jump in sound for the money. Start with the starter kit if you are still testing whether podcasting is for you, since every part except the light carries on to the next kit. Build the full studio if you record for clients or narrate audiobooks.

- **You are testing whether podcasting is for you:** the starter kit.
- **You publish every week:** the step up.
- **You record for clients, or narrate audiobooks:** the full studio.

:::callout{kind=note}
For two hosts in one room, use two Rook 7 microphones in place of one Wren D7, because the Rook 7 rejects the other host's voice best. For a guest on camera, clip on one of the [lavalier microphones we compared](/blog/lavalier-microphones-compared/). For three or four hosts, the [Heron R4 multitrack mixer](/blog/heron-r4-review/) records each person to their own track without a computer.
:::

::::faq
:::question{text="What should I buy first for a home podcast studio?"}
A dynamic microphone on a boom arm, and closed-back headphones. Those two decide how your voice sounds and whether you can hear problems while you record. Lights and room treatment can come later.
:::
:::question{text="Is a USB microphone good enough for a podcast?"}
Yes, to start. The Alder U1 sounded clear in our tests. Moving to an XLR microphone and an interface raised our panel's rating from 6.8 to 8.2, so it is the first upgrade worth making.
:::
:::question{text="Do I need to treat my room?"}
Not with a dynamic microphone. With a condenser, yes. A rug, curtains and a full bookcase help a lot for little money, and thick panels help most.
:::
