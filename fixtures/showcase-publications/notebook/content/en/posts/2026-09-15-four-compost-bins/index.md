For years I had one compost heap, and it never made compost. It was always half fresh and half rotten, and digging out the good stuff at the bottom meant moving everything on top. This spring I changed to four compost bins in a row, and in six months I have made more compost than in the three years before.

## How four compost bins work

Each bin has one job, and the material moves along the row.

1. **The filling bin.** Everything new goes in here: weeds without seeds, peelings, spent plants, grass, torn cardboard. When it is full, it is closed.
2. **The rotting bin.** The full bin's contents are turned into the second bin. Turning mixes in air, and the heap heats up again for a few weeks.
3. **The finishing bin.** After about two months, the heap is turned once more and left to finish. Worms move in and it cools down.
4. **The bin you use.** After another two or three months, it is dark and crumbly, and it goes on the beds.

## Getting the mix right

A heap needs about as much brown, dry material as green, wet material. Green means grass, peelings and soft leaves. Brown means cardboard, straw, woody stems and dry leaves. Too much green and it turns slimy and smells. Too much brown and nothing happens.

My fix for the slimy grass of summer is a stack of torn cardboard boxes in the shed. A layer of cardboard on every layer of grass keeps it right.

## What does not go in

Cooked food, meat and dairy bring rats. Weeds with seeds and roots of bindweed survive the heap and come back. Diseased plants, such as blighted tomatoes, go to the council green waste instead.

## What I have learned since spring

The bins made in August will feed next year's beds in the [crop rotation](/blog/planning-next-years-beds/). The autumn leaves go in a separate cage for [leaf mould](/blog/leaf-mould-in-two-winters/), since they rot so slowly. If you want to build bins from old pallets, the [allotment society's build guide](https://www.example.com/allotment-society/pallet-compost-bins) shows how in an afternoon.
