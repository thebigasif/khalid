A seed library is a shelf of seed packets that anyone can borrow from, on the promise to bring back seed from the plants they grow. The Northern Growers' Journal has a short write-up of one that runs from a public library in a small town in Northumberland, and it is the model for what we want to do with our spring seed swap.

## How their seed library works

The shelf is a set of old card index drawers by the library door. Each drawer holds one kind of crop: beans, peas, tomatoes, squash, flowers. Anyone can take three packets a month. In the autumn, people bring back seed from what they grew, in paper envelopes with the variety, the year and where it grew.

The write-up is honest about what goes wrong. About a third of the borrowers never bring seed back. Some returned seed is crossed, so a squash comes up as something strange. They fixed the second problem by keeping squash and sweetcorn, which cross easily, out of the returns, and only lending those from bought packets.

## What we will copy

Our seed swap has run for four years as a table in the pavilion on one Saturday in March. It works, but it is over in an afternoon. From next spring we will keep a seed library going all year as well:

- **A tin of drawers in the pavilion,** open whenever the pavilion is.
- **Beans, peas, tomatoes and lettuce first.** They are easy to save true, as in my note on [saving broad bean seed](/blog/saving-broad-bean-seed/).
- **A sheet for every packet,** so we know whose seed grew well where.

## The swap itself

The spring swap is on Saturday 7 March at the pavilion, from ten until one. Bring seed if you have it, and come anyway if you do not. There will be tea, and Sami will bring his swift box plans from [the swifts note](/blog/the-swifts-have-gone/).

[The write-up](https://www.example.com/ng/seed-shelf) is short and practical. If you run an allotment, a school garden or a library, it is worth ten minutes.
