The first fieldfares of the winter landed in the hawthorn hedge on the east side of the allotments on Monday morning. I counted fourteen, all stripping berries as fast as they could swallow them, with a loud chattering call that sounds like someone shaking a box of stones.

## Where fieldfares come from

Fieldfares are thrushes that breed in Scandinavia and Russia and spend the winter here. They arrive from the middle of October, often on the same clear nights that bring the [first frost](/blog/first-frost-on-the-plot/), and stay until March or April. The picture here is from a trip to Finland in February, where they stand out against the snow. Here they are harder to spot in the brown hedges.

## How to tell fieldfares from mistle thrushes

At a distance, fieldfares and mistle thrushes look alike: both are big, speckled thrushes. Three things tell them apart:

- **The grey head and rump.** A fieldfare has a blue-grey head and a grey patch above the tail. A mistle thrush is brown all over its back.
- **The call.** The fieldfare chatters, "chack chack chack". The mistle thrush rattles like a football rattle.
- **The company.** Fieldfares come in flocks, often mixed with redwings. Mistle thrushes are usually alone or in pairs, and guard one tree.

## When to look

Fieldfares feed early. The best time is the first hour after sunrise, when they come down to the hedge before anyone is on the plots. Once the hawthorn is stripped, which takes a week or two, they move to the fields and to fallen apples. Maren leaves the bruised windfalls from [apple day](/blog/apple-day-at-the-orchard/) under the hedge for them, and that keeps them close for another month.

Like the summer [swift counts](/blog/the-swifts-have-gone/), I send every count to the local wildlife group's [winter bird survey](https://www.example.com/wildlife-group/winter-birds), which maps the flocks across Leeds. If you see fieldfares this week, your count helps too.
