I have been saving broad bean seed from the same line of plants since 2017, and this year's beans are dry and in their jar. It is the easiest seed to save, and after a few years the plants suit your soil better than anything from a packet.

## Choosing the pods

In June, when the first beans are ready to eat, I tie a piece of wool round the best three plants. These are the strongest and earliest, with the most pods and no sign of rust. I leave every pod on those plants and pick the rest to eat.

The pods turn black and leathery by August. That looks like a disaster, but it is what you want. The beans inside are drying.

## Drying the beans

I pick the black pods on a dry day in September, before the autumn rain, and lay them on a tray in the shed for two more weeks. Then I shell them. A bean is dry enough when you bite it and it does not dent. If it dents, it goes back on the tray.

This year I saved about two hundred beans, from three plants. That is enough for my two rows and plenty for the [spring seed swap](/blog/a-seed-library-worth-copying/).

## Storing them

The beans go in a glass jar with a label: the variety, the year and the plot. I add a spoonful of dry rice in a twist of paper to soak up damp, and keep the jar in a cool cupboard in the house, not in the shed, where mice find everything.

Stored like this, broad bean seed grows well for three or four years. I still test a few each February: ten beans on damp kitchen paper in a tub. If eight or more sprout within ten days, the jar is good.

## Why it is worth it

Seed you save yourself is free, but that is not the main reason. Each year you keep the plants that did best on your own ground. The [Northern Seed Circle](https://www.example.com/northern-seed-circle/broad-beans) explains this well, and it is how most old varieties came about.
