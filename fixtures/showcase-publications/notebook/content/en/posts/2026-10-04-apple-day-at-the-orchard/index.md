Apple day at the community orchard was last Saturday, and it was the best one yet. Forty people came, we picked twelve varieties from thirty trees, and by four o'clock we had pressed ninety litres of juice with a press borrowed from the next village.

## How apple day works

The orchard is a strip of old trees behind the allotments, planted in the 1950s and nearly lost before the society took it on. Once a year we pick everything that is ripe in one day. Children pick the low branches, adults use the long pickers, and anyone tall stands under the trees with a basket.

The apples are sorted into three heaps. Good ones go home with whoever picked them. Bruised ones go in the press. Badly damaged ones go under the hedge for the birds, which keeps the [fieldfares](/blog/fieldfares-in-the-hawthorn/) around for weeks.

## What we picked

Most trees are cooking apples, with a few eaters. The labels on half the trees are long gone, so each year someone brings a book and we argue. This year we agreed on Bramley, Lord Derby, Grenadier, Egremont Russet, Discovery and James Grieve. The other six are still a mystery.

## The press and the juice

The press takes about ten kilos of chopped apple at a time and gives about six litres of juice. It is slow, sticky and the best job of the day. We pasteurise the juice in a big pan at 72 degrees for twenty minutes and pour it into bottles while it is hot. Sealed like that, it keeps for a year.

The pulp goes on the [compost bins](/blog/four-compost-bins/), and the hens next door get some too.

## Notes for next year

- Book the press by July. Three other orchards wanted it this year.
- Bring more bottles. We ran out at seventy litres.
- Ask someone from the [Leeds fruit group](https://www.example.com/leeds-fruit-group/apple-names) to name the six mystery trees.

The cooking apples I brought home went into a crumble and into this year's [green tomato chutney](/blog/green-tomato-chutney/).
