Leaf mould is the cheapest soil improver there is, and this week the Northern Growers' Journal published the clearest guide to making it that I have read. It is short, it has good pictures, and it tells you what to expect in each season.

## What the guide gets right

Most guides tell you to pile up leaves and wait. This one explains why it takes so long. Leaves break down through fungi, not bacteria, so a pile of leaves never heats up the way a compost heap does. That is why leaf mould needs a year for a rough mulch and two years for a fine, crumbly mix you can use for seed sowing.

It also says plainly which leaves to use. Oak, beech and hornbeam make the best leaf mould. Plane and sweet chestnut take three years. Leaves from roadsides pick up litter, so leave those where they fall.

## How I make leaf mould on the plot

I use the method in the guide with one change. I make a cage of chicken wire on four stakes in the corner behind the [compost bins](/blog/four-compost-bins/), and fill it each November. Before the leaves go in, I run the mower over them on the path. Chopped leaves rot in about half the time, so I get usable leaf mould after one winter, not two.

## Where to use it

One year old, the leaf mould goes on the beds as a mulch in spring. It keeps the soil damp and the worms busy. Two years old, it is fine enough to mix with sand for sowing carrots and parsnips. It is low in food for plants, so it improves the soil rather than feeding the crop, which is why it works so well in the beds I plan in my [crop rotation](/blog/planning-next-years-beds/).

[The guide itself](https://www.example.com/ng/leaf-mould) is free to read, and worth bookmarking before the leaves come down.
