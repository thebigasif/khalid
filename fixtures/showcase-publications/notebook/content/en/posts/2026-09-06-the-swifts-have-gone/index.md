The swifts have gone. The last ones screamed over the allotments on the evening of 14 August, and since then the sky has been quiet. Every year it catches me out: one week there are thirty swifts racing round the chimneys, and the next there are none. Here is what our swift counts showed this year.

## What our swift counts showed

From May to August I counted the swifts over the plots every Sunday evening, between seven and eight. The highest count was 34, on 21 June. The average across the summer was 19. That is lower than in 2020, when the average was 26, but a little higher than last year.

Our swift counts go to the [wildlife group's swift survey](https://www.example.com/wildlife-group/swift-survey), which maps where swifts nest across Leeds. Most of ours nest under the roof tiles of the old terraces on Harehills Lane.

## Where they go

Swifts spend the winter over central and southern Africa, south of the equator. They do almost everything in the air: they feed, drink and even sleep on the wing. A young swift that leaves Leeds this August may not land again until it comes back to nest in two or three years. It is hard to watch one and not think about that.

## Nest boxes for next May

Swifts lose nest sites when old roofs are repaired and the gaps are sealed. Over the winter the allotment society will put up six swift boxes under the eaves of the pavilion. They need to face north or east, be at least five metres up and have a clear flight path in front. Swifts are faithful to their nests, so boxes can take a few years to be found. Playing a recording of swift calls near the boxes in May helps them find the site.

## When to look again

The first swifts usually arrive in the first week of May. Maren has written the date on the shed calendar, under the [first frost](/blog/first-frost-on-the-plot/) list. Until then, the [fieldfares](/blog/fieldfares-in-the-hawthorn/) and the redwings will keep us busy.
