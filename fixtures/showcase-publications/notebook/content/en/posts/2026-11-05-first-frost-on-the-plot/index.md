The first frost on the plot came on the night of 4 November, two weeks later than last year. By eight in the morning every leaf had a white edge, and the water butt had a skin of ice you could tap with a finger.

## What the first frost finished off

The nasturtiums went first. By noon they hung like wet paper, and the courgette leaves had turned dark and soft. The last runner beans are done too. That is fine: I had already picked the pods I wanted for seed, as I wrote in [saving broad bean seed](/blog/saving-broad-bean-seed/), and the rest can go on the heap.

The dahlias are black at the top but still firm at the base. I will cut them down this weekend and lift the tubers to dry in the shed.

## What it made better

Frost is good news for some crops. The parsnips and the kale both taste sweeter after a cold night, because the plants turn some of their starch into sugar to protect themselves. I pulled two parsnips to test this, and they were sweeter than the ones I lifted in October.

## Three jobs for the morning after a first frost

1. **Clear the soft plants.** Anything that collapsed goes on the [compost bins](/blog/four-compost-bins/) before it rots where it stands and feeds the slugs.
2. **Check the fleece.** The young spring cabbages need a layer of fleece on clear nights from now on. I keep it pegged at one end so it is quick to pull over.
3. **Write it down.** I keep a list of first frost dates on the plot. This year is 4 November, last year was 21 October, and the year before was 9 November. Over ten years, the average is 28 October.

The forecast says more clear nights this week. The fieldfares should like it: Sami saw the [first ones in the hawthorn](/blog/fieldfares-in-the-hawthorn/) last week, and frost brings them down to the berries.

If you keep your own frost dates, compare them with the [Leeds weather station's frost record](https://www.example.com/leeds-weather/frost-dates), which goes back to 1960. It is a good check on your own notes.
