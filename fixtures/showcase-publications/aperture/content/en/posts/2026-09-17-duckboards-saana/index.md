At the foot of Saana the path crosses a wet bog on duckboards, planks laid side by side on short logs. In early August the bog is white with cotton grass, and the mountain birches around it are in full leaf. This picture was made at half past five, on the way down from the fell.

## A path into the picture

A path is the oldest trick in landscape pictures, because it works. The boards start at the bottom edge, run away from you and bend to the right. The eye follows them into the picture and out to the birches. The bend matters: a straight path pulls the eye out of the frame too fast.

The camera was low, about the height of a knee, so the boards start large and narrow quickly. That makes the bog feel deep.

## Depth from front to back

At f/16 everything is sharp, from the nearest plank to the far trees. A small aperture lets in little light, so the shutter slowed to 1/20 of a second. The camera rested on a pack on the boards, which kept it still enough.

## Why the duckboards are there

Bogs are soft, and a path walked by thousands of feet becomes a muddy trench. Duckboards spread the weight and keep walkers off the plants. In the north they are part of nearly every marked trail. You can read about [cotton grass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eriophorum), which grows in wet ground across the Arctic.

For the view from the top of the same fell, see [the lakes below Saana](/blog/lakes-below-saana/). More pictures from the far north are on the [Lapland page](/blog/category/lapland/).

Duckboards also make a good subject on a dull day. When the sky is grey and the fell top is in cloud, the boards and the cotton grass still give a picture with depth and a clear path through it. Look for a place where the duckboards turn, and get low. The wooden stairs of [the midnight sun stairs](/blog/midnight-sun-stairs/) work in the same way, leading the eye up instead of along.
