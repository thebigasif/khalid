Loading the kiln is the job I like best in the studio. A good load fits more pots, fires evenly and leaves no glaze stuck to the shelves. A bad load wastes a whole day of electricity. This is how I fit forty glazed mugs on three shelves.

## Check every pot first

Before a glazed pot goes in, I check its foot. Any glaze on the foot or within half a centimetre of it gets wiped off with a damp sponge. Glaze on a foot melts onto the shelf and glues the pot down. I also check for thin spots and runs from the [glaze bucket](/blog/mixing-my-own-glazes/).

## Loading the kiln: shelves and posts

Our kiln takes three round shelves. The bottom shelf sits on short posts, so heat reaches the underside of the floor. I use three posts for each shelf, never four, because three posts always sit flat even when a shelf has warped a little.

Tall pots go at the bottom, where the kiln is a little cooler. Mugs go in the middle. Small cups and test tiles go at the top, the hottest part of our kiln.

## Spacing

The most important rule when loading the kiln is that glazed pots must not touch. I leave a finger's width between each mug, and a finger's width from the walls and the elements. Forty mugs is the most that fits with that spacing. Forty-one is always a mistake.

## The firing schedule

We fire glaze loads to cone 6, about 1,220 °C, over about nine hours. The kiln climbs slowly for the first two hours to drive off any water, then faster to the top. A cone pack on the middle shelf tells us what the pots really felt. The [Orton cone chart](https://www.ortonceramic.com/) explains what each cone means.

## Unloading

The kiln cools for a full day with the door shut. Opening it early can crack the glaze. When it reads under 60 °C, Tamsin and I unload together, and any pot with a small fault goes to the [seconds sale](/blog/seconds-sale-green-bowls/). The rest go to the shop.

Raku is the exception to every rule here. Tamsin's [first raku firing](/blog/first-raku-firing/) pulls pots out while they glow.
