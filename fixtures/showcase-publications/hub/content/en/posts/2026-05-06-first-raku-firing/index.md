This week's video is my first raku firing. Raku means taking a pot out of the kiln while it is still glowing, then dropping it into sawdust that bursts into flame. The smoke turns the bare clay black and makes the glaze crackle. It is loud, quick and very different from the slow electric kiln we use every week.

## The kit

We borrowed a small gas raku kiln from a friend and set it up in the yard behind the studio. You also need long metal tongs, a metal bin with a lid, a bag of sawdust, thick gloves and a fire blanket. Dev kept a bucket of water and a fire extinguisher beside us the whole time.

## The pots

Raku is hard on pots. They go from about 1,000 °C to the open air in seconds, so they need a clay with plenty of grog that copes with the shock. I threw six small bowls from a raku clay and bisque fired them in our usual [kiln load](/blog/loading-the-kiln/) first. Then I glazed them with a white crackle glaze and left the outsides bare.

## The raku firing itself

Each load took about forty minutes to reach temperature. When the glaze looked wet and glossy through the spy hole, Dev opened the lid and I lifted a bowl out with the tongs. It went straight into the bin of sawdust, the sawdust caught fire, and we closed the lid to starve it of air.

After fifteen minutes in the smoke, each bowl came out black and sooty. We scrubbed it in water, and the crackle lines appeared where smoke had crept into the glaze.

## What worked and what cracked

Four of the six bowls survived. One cracked in the kiln because its wall was too thin at the rim. One cracked in the sawdust, probably because I held it too long in the air before dropping it. The four survivors are the best pots I have made this year, and two are in the [shop](https://www.example.com/tamsin-hale/shop).

## Next time

For my next raku firing I will throw the walls thicker and move faster from kiln to bin. If you want to start with something calmer, our post on [mixing glazes](/blog/mixing-my-own-glazes/) covers the glazes we fire every week.
