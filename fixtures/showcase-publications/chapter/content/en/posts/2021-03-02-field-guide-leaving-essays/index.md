This field guide collects twelve essays about the countryside Hanna Lowe grew up in, and about the people who leave it. Some essays are about her own family. Others follow a bus route, a closed school, a village shop and a footpath that nobody walks any more.

## What A Field Guide to Leaving is about

Each essay starts with one thing you can stand in front of: a gate, a phone box, a field that used to be an orchard. From there it asks who used it, who left, and what changed for the people who stayed. The book is gentle but clear about the costs of a countryside where young people cannot afford to live.

The essays were written over ten years, many of them for magazines and radio. Hanna rewrote each one for the book, and three are new.

## A guide you can use

The title is half a joke. Each essay ends with a short, practical note, such as where to find the old parish maps or how to read a hedge for its age. Readers have used it as a walking guide. If you want to try the hedge trick yourself, read about [hedgerow dating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hooper%27s_rule) and its limits.

## Its place among the books

A Field Guide to Leaving sits between the two novels. Many of its places come back in [the orchard novel](/blog/orchard-year-novel/), and its last essay was the seed of that book.

## What readers say

The book was a bookshop favourite in the border towns, where readers recognised the bus routes and the shops. It is still in print in paperback and as an ebook.

## Reading it with the novels

Many readers come to the essays after the novels, looking for the real places behind them. The bus route in the third essay is the one Cass takes home in The Orchard Year, and the closed school in the sixth essay is where Nell went in [Hanna's first novel](/blog/salt-road/). Hanna has said the essays are the map, and the novels are the journeys across it.
