Small Hours was Hanna Lowe's first book. It holds fourteen short stories, each set between midnight and dawn in the same small market town. A baker starts her ovens. A taxi driver takes the same fare home every Friday. A night nurse counts the hours until her shift ends.

## What the stories are about

The stories are short, most of them under ten pages, and they share streets and characters. Read in order, they make one long night in the town, from the pub closing to the first bus. Read alone, each is a small portrait of someone awake when everyone else is asleep.

Hanna wrote them while she worked night shifts at a county archive, cataloguing old parish records. Several stories borrow names and details from those records.

## How Small Hours began

The first story, about the baker, won a regional short story prize. Tern Editions asked if there were more, and there were. The book took two more years. Hanna still reads the baker's story at events, since it is short and it ends with bread.

## Where to start with Small Hours

If you have read the novels, start with the last story, which is set in the valley of [the first novel](/blog/salt-road/). If you are new to Hanna's work, start at the beginning and read one story a night. You can learn more about the [short story](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Short_story) as a form and its long history.

## What readers say

Small Hours is now in its fourth printing. Teachers use the shortest stories in writing classes, since each one shows a whole life in a few pages.

## Hear the stories read

Hanna reads two stories from Small Hours at the [winter reading in Ludlow](/blog/winter-reading-ludlow/), in the library where she wrote most of the book. The stories were written to be read aloud, late, by a lamp, and many readers say they work best that way: one a night, just before sleep.

## Small Hours in the classroom

Teachers often write to ask which stories from Small Hours work best for a class. Hanna suggests the baker's story for younger readers and the night nurse's story for older ones. Both are under six pages, both have a clear turn in the middle, and both end on an image that a class can talk about for a whole lesson.
