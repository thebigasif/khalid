Our apple day comes round on Saturday 18 October this year. It is the one day we close the till early, set out trestle tables in the orchard and let the apples do the talking. Everyone is welcome, and it is free.

## What is on at apple day

- **Tastings** from ten o'clock: twelve old apples, cut and labelled, so you can find one you love before you buy a tree.
- **The press** runs all day. Bring a bag of your own apples and take the juice home.
- **Grafting talks** at eleven and two, with Tom, for anyone who wants to grow a tree from a cutting.
- **Soup and cake** in the potting shed, made by my sister from our own apples and squash.

## The old apples we still grow

My father planted the orchard in 1971 with apples he could not find anywhere else. Some came from old gardens in the village, as cuttings given over the fence. Fifty years on, we still graft new trees from them every winter.

| Apple | Season | What it is like |
|---|---|---|
| Larkfield Pippin | October | Sweet and crisp, a little nutty |
| Mill Lane Russet | November | Rough skin, tastes of pear |
| Hollins Seedling | September | Sharp, best for cooking |

These names are our own. Each of these trees came from a garden near here, and we named them after where we found them.

## Why old apples matter

Most shops sell the same few apples. Old ones taste different, keep well in a cool shed and often stand up to disease without spraying. Growing them keeps them alive, and every tree we sell is one more place they grow.

## Getting here on apple day

Park in the field by the gate on Mill Lane. The orchard is flat, with grass paths, and there are chairs in the shed. Dogs on leads are welcome.
