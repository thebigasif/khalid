Courtyard blocks are back because they fit as many homes on a plot as a tower does, give every flat a quiet side away from the street, and leave the streets around them lined with front doors and windows. Planners turned away from them for most of the twentieth century, and many cities are now returning to them for new housing.

A courtyard block, also called a perimeter block, is a ring of buildings around the edge of a plot, with a shared garden or yard inside. Most of the old centres of Europe are made of them. This article explains why the form fell out of favour, why it is returning, and what makes a good one.

## How courtyard blocks fell out of fashion

In the early twentieth century, many old courtyard blocks were overcrowded and dark. Their yards had been filled with workshops and back houses, and some flats never saw the sun. Reformers blamed the form itself.

After the Second World War, many planners chose a different model: towers and long slabs standing in open green space, with light and air on every side. Some of these estates worked well. Many did not. The green space between the buildings belonged to nobody in particular, and the streets around them lost their shops and their life.

## How courtyard blocks compare with other forms

| Form | Typical height | Homes per hectare | Street life | Private outdoor space |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Detached houses | 1 to 2 storeys | Low | Little | Large gardens |
| Terraced houses | 2 to 3 storeys | Middle | Good | Small gardens |
| Courtyard blocks | 4 to 7 storeys | High | Very good | Shared courtyard |
| Slab blocks in open space | 6 to 12 storeys | High | Poor | Open lawns, rarely used |
| Towers | 15 storeys and more | High to very high | Poor at the base unless designed for it | Balconies |

The numbers vary with each scheme, so the table gives ranges, not figures. The pattern is clear, though. Courtyard blocks reach high densities without great height, and they keep the street as a real street.

## Why courtyard blocks work

- **A quiet side for every home.** Flats can face both the street and the courtyard, so bedrooms can face the quiet side.
- **Clear ownership.** The street is public, the courtyard is shared by the residents, and the flats are private. Nobody wonders whose space a corner is.
- **Eyes on the street.** Front doors and windows face the pavement, so people walking past are seen. Streets with many windows feel safer and usually are.
- **Room for shops.** Ground floors on the street side can hold shops and workshops, and that is how many old courtyard blocks paid for themselves.

:::callout{kind=tip}
When you judge a courtyard block, look at its corners. Corners are where flats get the least light and where courtyards feel cramped. A good scheme opens the corners, lowers them or gives them the largest windows.
:::

## What makes a good courtyard

The old reformers were right about one thing: a courtyard that is too small is a light well, not a garden. A good courtyard is at least as wide as the buildings around it are tall, so the sun reaches the ground for part of the day.

It also needs things to do. The best courtyards in Vienna council housing hold play spaces, benches, drying lines and trees large enough to give shade. Our article on [Vienna council housing](/blog/vienna-council-housing/) shows how the city has used the form for a century, including estates where most of the plot is garden.

## Courtyard blocks in new housing

Cities from Copenhagen to Barcelona now plan new districts as courtyard blocks, often with several architects designing different parts of each block so it does not look like one building. The form is also a good fit for conversions. An old office block around a yard can often become flats more easily than a deep tower; we look at that in [when offices into homes works](/blog/offices-into-homes/).
