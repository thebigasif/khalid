HAMBURG: Brick buildings still make good cities because brick lasts for centuries, looks better as it weathers, and can be repaired one brick at a time by ordinary builders. A street of brick ages together, so it keeps its character long after the people who built it are gone.

Hamburg's Kontorhaus district shows this better than almost anywhere. Its office blocks were built in dark brick in the 1920s and 1930s, and a century later they are still in daily use. This article looks at why brick works so well in a city and what designers today can learn from it.

## The Chilehaus and its district

The Chilehaus was finished in 1924, for a merchant who had made his fortune in trade with Chile. Its architect built it in dark clinker brick, a hard brick fired at a high heat until its surface almost turns to glass. The building fills an awkward wedge of land, and its east end narrows to a sharp edge that people often compare to the bow of a ship.

Around it, the other office houses of the district use the same dark brick in different ways. Together with the warehouse district of the Speicherstadt nearby, the area became a UNESCO World Heritage site in 2015. A visitor sees one material used for a whole neighbourhood, and that consistency is much of its power.

## Why brick buildings last

- **Brick does not rot or rust.** A well-fired brick is a small piece of baked clay. Water, sun and frost wear its surface very slowly.
- **The mortar is the weak point, on purpose.** Lime mortar is softer than the brick. When a wall moves or gets wet, the mortar cracks and wears first, and mortar is cheap to replace.
- **Small units are easy to repair.** A damaged brick can be cut out and replaced with one that matches. A damaged concrete panel usually has to be patched or replaced whole.
- **It weathers well.** Brick gets darker and softer in colour over time. Few people find an old brick wall ugly.

## Bonds: how bricks are laid

The pattern in which bricks are laid is called the bond. Each bond ties the wall together in a different way, and each gives brick buildings a different look.

| Bond | How the bricks are laid | Where you see it |
|---|---|---|
| Stretcher bond | Long sides only, each row shifted by half a brick | Most modern walls, where brick is a thin outer skin |
| Flemish bond | Long and short ends alternate in each row | Older solid walls in Britain and the Netherlands |
| English bond | A row of long sides, then a row of short ends | Bridges, warehouses and heavy walls |
| Header bond | Short ends only | Curved walls and some decorative facades |

A wall in Flemish or English bond is solid: the short ends of the bricks reach into the wall and tie its layers together. A modern wall in stretcher bond is usually a single skin of brick tied to a hidden structure behind it.

:::callout{kind=warning}
Never repoint an old brick wall with hard cement mortar. Cement is harder than old brick, so the bricks crack and wear instead of the mortar. Use a lime mortar that matches the old one.
:::

## What modern brick buildings can learn

Today, most new brick buildings use brick as a thin outer layer over a frame of concrete or steel. That can still last well, but only if the designer treats brick as brick: with deep window reveals, a proper bond, and details that shed water rather than trap it.

Firing brick takes a lot of energy, which is a real cost. The answer is to make brick buildings last as long as their material can, and to reuse old bricks where possible. A brick wall that stands for three hundred years spreads that energy over a very long life.
