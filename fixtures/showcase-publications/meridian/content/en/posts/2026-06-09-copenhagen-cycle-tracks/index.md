COPENHAGEN: Copenhagen cycle tracks work because each one sits between the pavement and the road, raised above the road by a kerb and set a step below the pavement, so people on bikes have a space of their own that drivers and walkers can both see. The kerb is the whole idea: it is cheap, it lasts for decades, and it tells everyone where they belong without a single sign.

Many cities paint a cycle lane on the road and wonder why people do not use it. Copenhagen built a kerb instead, and a large share of its residents ride to work or school every day. This article looks at what makes the design work and what other cities get wrong when they copy it.

## How Copenhagen cycle tracks are built

A typical track on a main street has the same parts, in the same order, from the buildings to the middle of the road.

1. **The pavement**, at the highest level, for people on foot.
2. **A low kerb**, a step down to the cycle track.
3. **The cycle track**, one way only, in the same direction as the traffic beside it.
4. **A second kerb**, a step down to the road.
5. **Parking or a bus stop**, on many streets, between the track and the moving cars.
6. **The road** for cars, buses and lorries.

Every street with a track has one on each side, so nobody rides against the traffic. On the busiest routes, the tracks are wide enough for people to overtake each other safely.

## Paint, kerb or wall

| Design | What separates bikes from cars | How safe it feels | Cost to build |
|---|---|---|---|
| Painted lane | A white line | Low: cars drift into it and park on it | Very low |
| Lane with posts | Plastic posts on a line | Better, but posts get knocked over | Low |
| Raised track with kerbs | A kerb on each side | High: cars cannot drift into it | Middle |
| Fully separate path | A verge or a wall | Highest, but junctions get harder | High |

Copenhagen cycle tracks sit in the third row, which is the reason they work so widely. They are far safer than paint, yet they stay part of the street. People on bikes stay close to the shops and front doors, so riding is as convenient as driving.

:::callout{kind=note}
A kerb also helps people who never ride. It keeps parked cars and delivery vans off the track, and it gives people on foot a clear edge, so they do not step into the path of a bike without looking.
:::

## The junctions are where it matters

Most crashes happen at junctions, not along the street. Copenhagen cycle tracks usually end a short way before each junction, and a band of blue paint carries the route across. Drivers turning right see a block of colour exactly where a bike might be. At many junctions, the lights let people on bikes start a few seconds before the cars, so they are already in view when the cars move.

On some main routes, the traffic lights are timed so that someone riding at a steady pace meets a run of green lights. It is a small change that tells riders they are traffic too.

## What other cities get wrong

Many cities have tried to copy Copenhagen cycle tracks. These are the most common mistakes.

- **They paint instead of building.** Paint is quick, but it does not change how safe people feel, and people who do not feel safe do not ride.
- **They stop at the hard parts.** A track that disappears at the busiest junction makes the whole route useless for anyone nervous.
- **They make tracks two-way on one side.** Two-way tracks surprise drivers turning in from the other direction.
- **They forget the network.** One good street is a showpiece. A network of good streets is a transport system.
