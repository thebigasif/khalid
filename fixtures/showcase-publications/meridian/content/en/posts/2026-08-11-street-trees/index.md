Street trees need four things to grow old: enough soil that their roots can reach, water in their first few summers, room to grow above and below the ground, and someone whose job it is to look after them. Most young street trees that die in a city die because one of those four was missing, not because the tree was the wrong kind.

Trees give a street shade, shelter and a sense of age. They cool the pavement on hot days and soften the noise of traffic. Yet many young street trees never reach the size that gives those benefits. This article explains what a tree needs and what a good scheme does differently.

## Why so many street trees die young

A tree planted in a street lives in a hard place. Its roots sit under paving, its trunk stands next to traffic, and the water that falls on the street runs into drains instead of into the soil. The tree pit, the opening in the pavement where it stands, is often barely larger than the root ball it arrived with.

For the first two or three years, the tree survives on the soil it was planted in. Once its roots reach the compacted ground under the pavement, they stop. The tree stays small, grows weak and dies within ten or fifteen years. The street is left with an empty pit or a stunted tree that never gives shade.

## What street trees need, by size

Street design guides give different figures, so the table below shows a common pattern rather than exact numbers. The point is the difference between sizes.

| Tree size when grown | Space above the pavement | Soil the roots need | Where it fits |
|---|---|---|---|
| Small | A few metres of crown | A large tree pit, extended under the pavement | Narrow streets, near buildings |
| Medium | Clear of first-floor windows | Several times a standard pit | Most residential streets |
| Large | Clear of the buildings on both sides | A long trench of soil shared with other trees | Wide streets, avenues and squares |

Large trees give far more shade than small ones, and they live longer. That is why avenues like the one in our photograph above are planted as a line, with the trees sharing a long strip of soil instead of standing in separate pits.

## How a good scheme plants street trees

- **Soil under the pavement.** Special cells or structural soil carry the weight of the paving while leaving loose soil for the roots. This is the biggest single difference between trees that thrive and trees that fail.
- **Water from the street.** Rain from the pavement can be led into the tree pit instead of the drain. It waters the tree and takes pressure off the sewers.
- **Watering for three summers.** A young tree needs regular watering until its roots spread. Many schemes plant the trees and then forget them.
- **Protection from cars and bikes.** A low guard or a kerb stops vehicles damaging the trunk and parked cars crushing the soil.
- **One owner for the job.** A tree that belongs to everyone belongs to no one. Someone must be paid to water, prune and replace it.

:::callout{kind=tip}
If your street has young trees, a bucket of water once a week in a dry summer makes a real difference in their first years. Some cities run schemes where residents adopt a tree and get a reminder when it needs water.
:::

## Choosing the right tree

The kind of tree matters less than the soil, but it still matters. A good street tree tolerates drought, pollution and heavy pruning, and does not drop heavy fruit on cars or people. Planting many different kinds also matters. A street, or a whole city, planted with one kind of tree can lose all of them to a single disease.

## Street trees and calmer streets

Trees work best on streets where people linger. A street with slow traffic and wide pavements gives trees space, and the trees in turn make the street more pleasant to walk and sit in.
