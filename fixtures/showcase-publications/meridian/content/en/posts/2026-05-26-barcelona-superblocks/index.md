BARCELONA: Barcelona superblocks give streets back to people by moving through traffic to the edge of a group of city blocks and slowing the streets inside to walking pace. Cars can still reach every door, but they can no longer use the inner streets as a short cut, so those streets fill with benches, trees and play space.

The idea is simple and cheap to start. It needs paint, planters and new signs before it needs any building work. That is why so many other cities have studied it. This article explains how a superblock works, what Barcelona learned from its first ones, and where the idea fits elsewhere.

## The grid that made Barcelona superblocks possible

Much of central Barcelona is the Eixample, a grid of square blocks planned in the 1850s. Each block has its corners cut off at an angle, which gives every junction a small open space. The grid is regular, so the same plan can be repeated block after block.

That regularity is what makes Barcelona superblocks work. Take a square of three blocks by three. The streets around the outside carry buses and through traffic as before. The streets inside the square become local streets, where cars may only go slowly and must turn out again at the next junction.

## How a superblock changes a street

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Through traffic on every street | Through traffic on the outer streets only |
| Speed set for cars | Speed kept to about walking pace inside |
| Junctions given to cars and parking | Junctions turned into small squares |
| Pavements as the only space for people | The whole street shared, with people first |
| Little shade | Planters, new trees and benches |

The first superblock opened in Poblenou in 2016. It was put in quickly, mostly with paint and moveable furniture, and some neighbours were angry at the change. Over the following months, the street filled with children's games, tables and plants, and the opposition faded. The next, around the market in Sant Antoni, was planned with residents from the start.

:::callout{kind=tip}
Start with paint and planters, not paving. A cheap first version can be changed after a few months of use, and it lets neighbours see the street before they are asked to judge it.
:::

## What the first Barcelona superblocks taught

- **Talk first.** Poblenou showed that a change made overnight makes enemies, even when the result is good. Later schemes held meetings and street trials before any paint went down.
- **Keep deliveries and emergencies in.** Every door still needs a van and an ambulance. Slow is fine; closed is not.
- **Watch the edges.** Traffic moved to the outer streets has to go somewhere. The edge streets need good crossings and bus priority, or they become worse places to live.
- **Plant early.** Trees take years to give shade, so they should go in with the first paint, not the final scheme.

## From superblocks to green axes

More recently, the city has gone a step further and turned whole lengths of streets in the Eixample into green axes, with new paving, trees and squares at every junction. These cost far more than paint, and they are permanent. They were only possible because the first superblocks had shown people what a calm street feels like.

## Will it work where you live

Barcelona superblocks rely on a grid, and most cities are not grids. But the principle travels: separate the streets that carry traffic from the streets where people live, and make it impossible to cut through the second kind. In older cities, that is often done with a few bollards or one-way turns, sometimes called a low-traffic neighbourhood.

Two more details make a calmer street last. The first is trees, which need more care than most schemes give them; read [what a street tree needs to grow old](/blog/street-trees/). The second is a good place to sit, which we look at in [what makes small squares work](/blog/small-squares/).
