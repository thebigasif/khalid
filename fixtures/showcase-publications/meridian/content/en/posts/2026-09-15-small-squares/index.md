PARIS: Small squares work when they have a clear edge of buildings around them, places to sit in sun and shade, something to watch, and a reason for people to pass through. Place Dauphine, a small triangle of gravel and trees on the Île de la Cité, has had all of these for four hundred years.

Big squares get the attention, but most of the public space people use every day is small: a widened corner, a space in front of a church, a patch of trees between two streets. In my work for city councils, I have counted people in dozens of them. The same few things decide whether a small square fills up or stays empty.

## A square with an edge

Place Dauphine was laid out in the early seventeenth century, at the western end of the island. Tall houses of pale stone and brick line it on all three sides, with cafés and shops on the ground floor. Two narrow openings lead in. Step through, and the noise of the city drops away.

That sense of enclosure is the first thing small squares need. People sit where their backs are covered and where they can see who is coming. A square open to traffic on every side feels like a traffic island, however well it is paved.

## What makes small squares work

| Feature | Why it matters | What we count in a survey |
|---|---|---|
| A clear edge of buildings | People feel sheltered and the space has a shape | How much of the edge is building, not road |
| Seats in sun and shade | People stay longer when they can choose | Seats per 100 square metres, and how many are used |
| Movable chairs | People like to set their own distance from others | Whether chairs are moved during the day |
| Shops and cafés on the edge | They bring people and keep watch | Doors that open onto the square |
| Something to watch | A fountain, a game, a market, other people | Where people look while they sit |
| Paths through it | People cross the square on their way somewhere | Crossings per hour |
| Trees | Shade in summer, a roof overhead | Share of the square under the canopy |

The last two rows surprise people. A square that nobody crosses rarely feels busy, even with good seats, because sitting alone in an empty square feels odd. And trees do more than give shade. Their canopy works like a ceiling and makes a large space feel like a room.

## Common mistakes in small squares

- **Too much open paving.** Large empty stretches feel exposed. Break them up with trees, seats or level changes.
- **Seats facing nothing.** A bench facing a wall stays empty. Face seats towards paths, doors and views.
- **Hostile benches.** Armrests every half metre and sloping seats keep everyone away, not only the people they target.
- **A blank edge.** One side of offices that close at five o'clock leaves a square dead every evening.
- **No one in charge.** Small squares need cleaning, repairs and someone who notices when a seat breaks.

:::callout{kind=tip}
To test a square, visit it at lunchtime on a sunny weekday and count how many people sit down. Then visit again in the early evening. A square that fills twice a day is doing well.
:::

## Making more small squares

Many cities have more potential squares than they think. The cut corners of the Barcelona grid became small squares when traffic was calmed; read [how Barcelona superblocks gave streets back](/blog/barcelona-superblocks/). A widened pavement, a closed side street or a courtyard opened to the public can do the same. The ingredients are always the edge, the seats, the shade and the reason to pass through.
