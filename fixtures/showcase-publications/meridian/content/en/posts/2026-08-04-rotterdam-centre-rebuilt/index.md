ROTTERDAM: The Rotterdam centre was rebuilt as a modern city because the people who planned it after 1940 chose not to copy what had been lost. Bombing on 14 May 1940 destroyed most of the old centre, and the city decided to start again with new streets, new buildings and new ideas.

Most European cities damaged in the war rebuilt something close to their old streets. Rotterdam went the other way, and for eighty years it has kept experimenting. This article follows the main steps and asks what the experiment has taught.

## A clean slate

The old Rotterdam centre was dense and crowded, with narrow streets and canals. After the bombing, and after the fires that followed, little of it could be saved. Within a few years, the city adopted a plan for a new centre: wider streets, separate areas for shopping, work and housing, and room for traffic.

Many people at the time thought this was a loss. Others saw a chance to build a centre for a modern port city. That argument has never quite ended, and it is part of what makes the Rotterdam centre interesting to walk through.

## The Rotterdam centre decade by decade

| Period | What was built | What it tried |
|---|---|---|
| Late 1940s and 1950s | The Lijnbaan, a shopping street for people on foot only | Separating shoppers from traffic |
| 1960s and 1970s | Offices, wide roads and car parks | Making room for the car |
| 1980s | The cube houses and new housing in the centre | Bringing residents back |
| 1990s and 2000s | Towers along the river and a new bridge | A skyline for a modern port |
| 2010s | The market hall, with flats in its arch | Mixing homes, food and public space |

The Lijnbaan opened in 1953, and it was one of the first streets in Europe built from the start for shoppers on foot. Low shops line it on both sides, with flats in taller blocks behind. Planners from many countries came to see it.

## Bringing people back to live in the centre

By the 1970s, the new Rotterdam centre had a problem. It worked by day and emptied at night, because few people lived there. The city began to add housing, and some of it was boldly experimental.

The cube houses are the best known. Each house is a cube tipped on one corner and set on a concrete column, and the houses sit in a row above a busy road, forming a kind of bridge. The space beneath them is a sheltered courtyard. They are odd to live in, as some residents freely admit, but they gave the centre a landmark and a reason to visit.

## The market hall

The market hall, opened in 2014, takes the idea further. It is an arch of flats and offices wrapped around a covered food market, with the end walls made of glass. People live in the arch, shoppers fill the hall, and the building gives the old market square a roof.

## What the Rotterdam centre teaches

- **Rebuilding as new can work, but it takes decades.** The first plan made a centre for shopping and traffic. It took forty years and many changes to make it a place where people live.
- **Housing is what keeps a centre alive.** Shops and offices fill a centre by day. Homes fill it at night and at weekends.
- **Experiments age at different speeds.** Some of the 1960s roads feel dated now, while the Lijnbaan, a simpler idea, still works.

The same lesson appears in other cities that try to bring life back to their centres. Read [when offices into homes works](/blog/offices-into-homes/) for one way to add residents without new building.
