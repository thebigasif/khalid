To reuse old buildings well, start by surveying the structure, then match the building to a use that suits its floors, its light and its location, and only then design the changes. Done in that order, reuse is usually quicker, cheaper in carbon and often cheaper in money than demolishing and building new.

This guide is for owners, councillors and residents' groups facing an empty building and a choice. It sets out what to check, which new uses suit which kinds of building, and how a typical project runs. It draws on fifteen years of my own work on conversions of warehouses, mills, schools and offices.

::toc

:::callout{kind=summary}
- **Survey before you design.** The structure decides what is possible.
- **Match the use to the building.** Deep floors suit offices and labs; narrow ones suit homes.
- **Keep the big moves simple.** Spend money on the structure, the services and the light, not on a new face.
- **Count the carbon already in the building.** Demolition throws it away.
:::

## Why reuse old buildings at all

The first reason to reuse old buildings is carbon. Every building holds the energy and materials that went into making it: the cement, the steel, the bricks, the glass and the transport that brought them. When a building is demolished, all of that is lost, and a new building must spend it again. For many building types, this embodied carbon is a large share of the carbon a building causes over its whole life.

There are other reasons too. Old buildings give a street its character, and people grow attached to them. They are often well built, with thick walls, high ceilings and generous windows. And reuse avoids months of demolition noise, dust and lorries.

Not every building can or should be kept. But the choice to reuse old buildings should be the starting point, and demolition the option that has to make its case.

## Step 1: survey what you have

To reuse old buildings safely, you need facts before ideas. Before anyone draws a plan, find out what the building is made of and what state it is in. These surveys cost a small share of the project and save far more.

| Survey | What it tells you | Who does it |
|---|---|---|
| Measured survey | The exact size and shape of every floor and wall | A surveyor, often with a laser scanner |
| Structural survey | What the frame, floors and foundations can carry | A structural engineer |
| Condition survey | Damp, decay, cracks and failing materials | A building surveyor or conservation architect |
| Hazardous materials survey | Asbestos, lead paint and other harmful materials | A licensed specialist |
| Services survey | The state of the wiring, plumbing, heating and drains | A building services engineer |
| History and significance | Which parts matter most and why | A historian or conservation architect |

:::callout{kind=warning}
Do not strip out any part of an old building before the surveys are done. Early stripping often destroys details that would have been cheap to keep, and it can expose hazards before anyone knows they are there.
:::

## Step 2: match the building to a use

The most common mistake when people reuse old buildings is to choose a use first and force the building to fit. It is far better to ask what the building is good at.

| Kind of building | What it offers | Uses that often suit it |
|---|---|---|
| Warehouse | Strong floors, deep plans, large windows | Offices, studios, flats with a central light well, storage |
| Mill or factory | Long open floors, high ceilings, regular columns | Offices, workshops, flats, schools |
| Power station or gasworks | One or two huge spaces | Galleries, markets, sports halls, event spaces |
| School | Classrooms with good light, a hall, a yard | Flats, community centres, studios |
| Office block | Regular floors, lifts and stairs, often deep plans | Flats if the floors are shallow, hotels, labs |
| Church | One tall space, strong character | Concert halls, libraries, community use |
| Railway arch or viaduct | Rows of strong vaults | Workshops, shops, cafés, storage |

Depth is the key measure for homes. A flat needs daylight in every bedroom and living room, and daylight reaches only a few metres into a room. Deep buildings, such as many warehouses and office blocks, may need a courtyard or light well cut through the middle before they can hold flats. We look at that problem in [when offices into homes works](/blog/offices-into-homes/).

## Step 3: plan the changes

Once the use is clear, plan the work in order of importance. The structure and the building's skin come first, because everything else depends on them. Then come the routes through the building, the services and the light. The finishes come last.

1. **Repair the structure and the roof.** A dry building is the first goal.
2. **Improve the walls and windows.** Insulate where it is safe, and repair or replace windows to keep heat in.
3. **Add stairs, lifts and fire escapes.** New uses usually need more of them than the old one did.
4. **Install new services.** Heating, ventilation, water and power, ideally in routes that can be reached and changed later.
5. **Let in light.** Rooflights, light wells and new openings where the survey allows them.
6. **Fit out.** Partitions, kitchens, bathrooms and finishes.

## Step 4: keep the old and the new distinct

The best conversions make it easy to tell what is old and what is new. New stairs, floors and windows look like what they are, and old walls, beams and roofs are repaired with matching materials. This approach is honest, and it makes later changes easier, because each layer can be read and, if needed, removed.

Our article on [how a power station became a gallery](/blog/power-station-gallery/) shows this approach at the largest scale. The arches of the old railway in Paris show it at a small one, in [what Paris built on an old railway viaduct](/blog/railway-viaduct-parks/).

## Costs, time and risk

Plans to reuse old buildings have a reputation for surprises, and they deserve it. Old buildings hide problems until they are opened up. But the surveys described above remove most of the risk, and a sensible reserve in the budget covers the rest.

| Factor | Reuse | Demolish and rebuild |
|---|---|---|
| Embodied carbon | Most of the existing structure kept | Lost, then spent again on the new structure |
| Time on site | Often shorter, with no demolition and fewer foundations | Longer, starting with demolition |
| Risk of surprises | Higher at first, lower after full surveys | Lower in the building, but ground problems can appear |
| Planning | Often easier, especially for listed or valued buildings | Can be harder where the old building is valued |
| Final result | Character, high ceilings, some limits on layout | Freedom of layout, no character to start with |

## A checklist before you decide

Before you decide whether to reuse old buildings or replace them, answer these questions.

- Have the surveys been done, and have you read them?
- Does the building suit the proposed use, or are you forcing it?
- Has someone counted the embodied carbon of both options?
- Have neighbours been asked what they value in the building?
- Is there a plan for the parts that will not be needed, such as old machinery or fittings, so they can be reused or recorded?
