LONDON: Bankside Power Station became Tate Modern by keeping almost everything that made it a power station, its brick shell, its chimney and its vast turbine hall, and fitting galleries into the spaces around them. The building opened as a gallery in 2000, nearly twenty years after it stopped making electricity.

It is the best-known conversion of an industrial building in Europe, and it is worth looking at closely, because the choices that made it work apply to far smaller buildings too. This article looks at what the architects kept, what they changed and what other cities took from it.

## A power station nobody wanted

Bankside was built in two stages between 1947 and 1963, on the south bank of the Thames opposite St Paul's Cathedral. It burned oil, and when oil prices rose in the 1970s, it became too expensive to run. It closed in 1981.

For more than a decade, the building stood empty. A power station is a hard building to reuse. Its main rooms are enormous, its walls hold almost no windows, and the ground under it is often polluted. Several plans to demolish it came and went. In the 1990s, the Tate gallery chose it as the home for its collection of modern art, and an architectural competition followed.

## What the conversion kept

The winning design did something that seemed modest at the time: it left the power station looking like a power station. The brick walls stayed. The central chimney stayed, though it no longer carries smoke. The turbine hall, where the machines that made electricity once stood, was emptied and left as one huge room.

| Part of the power station | What it became | What changed |
|---|---|---|
| Turbine hall | Entrance and space for large works | Machines removed, floor ramped down from the west door |
| Boiler house | Several floors of galleries | New floors and stairs built inside the shell |
| Chimney | A landmark, lit at night | Kept as it was, no longer in use |
| Roof | A two-storey glass box with a restaurant | Added on top, the main new element |
| Oil tanks below ground | Spaces for film and live art | Opened in 2012, before a new wing was added in 2016 |

The turbine hall is the reason most visitors remember the building. It is about the length of a city street, and it is open to everyone without a ticket. That one decision turned an industrial room into a covered public square.

## Why keeping the power station worked

Three things made the conversion succeed, and none of them was cheap or obvious.

- **The big room was left big.** It would have been easy to fill the turbine hall with floors and sell more space. Leaving it empty gave the gallery a room no new building could afford.
- **New work was easy to read.** The glass roof box and the new floors inside look new. Nobody mistakes them for the old building, so the power station's own story stays clear.
- **The site was already a landmark.** People in London knew the chimney. Keeping it meant the new gallery started life as a building people already recognised.

:::callout{kind=note}
The architects did not invent a new face for the building. Most of the money went into the inside: floors, stairs, lifts, light and air. That is the usual pattern in a good conversion, and the reason our [step-by-step reuse guide](/blog/reuse-old-buildings/) starts with the structure, not the facade.
:::

## What it did to the riverside

Before the gallery opened, the stretch of riverbank around Bankside was quiet and hard to reach. The gallery, together with a new footbridge across the river opened at about the same time, made it one of the busiest walks in London. New flats, offices and cafés followed within a few years.

That change brought problems as well as visitors. Rents in the streets around the gallery rose, and some older businesses moved out. Our article on [how to read the signs of neighbourhood change](/blog/read-neighbourhood-change/) looks at that pattern, which follows many large cultural projects.

## What other cities took from it

After 2000, many cities looked at their own empty power stations, gasworks and warehouses with new eyes. The lessons they drew were mostly practical:

1. Start with the biggest space and decide what it is for before anything else.
2. Keep the parts people recognise from the street.
3. Make the new parts plainly new.
4. Open some of the building to the public without a ticket.

A power station is an extreme case. Most old buildings are far smaller, but the same order of thinking works for a mill, a school or a railway arch. Paris used it on a disused railway line, which we describe in [what Paris built on an old railway viaduct](/blog/railway-viaduct-parks/), and Hamburg used it on its brick warehouses.
