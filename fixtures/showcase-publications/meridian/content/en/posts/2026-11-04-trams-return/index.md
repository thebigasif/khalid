FREIBURG: Trams are coming back to mid-sized cities because they carry far more people than buses on the busiest routes, cost a fraction of a metro, and change the streets they run on for the better. A city of a few hundred thousand people rarely needs a metro, but its main bus routes are often full, slow and unreliable, and trams fill that gap.

Freiburg kept its trams through the decades when many cities tore theirs up, and it has since built new districts around new lines from the start. In France, Spain and elsewhere, cities that scrapped their old networks after the war have built new ones. This article explains why, what a tram scheme involves, and what makes one succeed.

:::callout{kind=summary}
- **Capacity.** One vehicle carries as many people as several buses, with one driver.
- **Cost.** A light rail line costs far less than a metro line of the same length.
- **Permanence.** Rails in the street tell people and businesses the route will last.
- **Streets.** A tram scheme is a chance to rebuild a whole street, front door to front door.
:::

## Why so many cities tore up their rails

In the first half of the twentieth century, most European cities had street railways. After the Second World War, many removed them. Buses were cheaper to buy, needed no rails or wires, and could change route overnight. Cars were growing fast, and the trams were in their way.

A few cities, mostly in Germany, Switzerland, Austria and the Netherlands, kept their networks and modernised them. Their experience is a large part of why other cities have returned to trams.

## Why trams are coming back

The argument rests on four points.

- **They carry more people.** A modern tram can carry several times as many passengers as a standard bus. On a route where buses already run every few minutes and are still full, more buses only add to the queue.
- **They are faster where it counts.** They usually run on their own track, away from traffic jams, and passengers board through many wide doors at once.
- **They are permanent.** A bus route can be cut with a timetable change. Rails cannot. That permanence encourages people to live, work and invest along the line.
- **People like them.** Many people who never take a bus will take a tram, because it feels smooth, clear and reliable.

## Bus, tram or metro

| Mode | Typical passengers per hour in each direction | Cost to build per kilometre | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ordinary bus | Low | Very low, uses existing roads | Light routes and local links |
| Bus on its own lanes | Low to middle | Low | Busy routes where space is tight |
| Tram | Middle to high | Middle | The busiest routes in mid-sized cities |
| Metro | Very high | Very high, often several times as much | The largest cities, under dense centres |

Costs and capacities vary widely from one city to another, so the table compares the modes rather than giving figures. The pattern holds, though. Light rail sits in the middle: much more capacity than buses, at much less cost than a metro. For a mid-sized city, that middle is often exactly what the busiest routes need.

## What a new line does to a street

A tram line is not only a transport project. To lay the track, the city has to dig up the street from one side to the other, and that is a chance to rebuild it. The best schemes treat the line as a street project with a tram in it.

They widen the pavements, plant trees, add cycle tracks and rebuild the squares at each stop. In many French cities, the new line arrived together with a new main street, and the change in the centre was as large as the change in travel.

Grass track, like the line in our photograph, is a common choice where the line runs on its own path. The grass softens the noise of the wheels, soaks up rain and makes the line look like part of a park rather than a railway.

:::callout{kind=note}
Building a tram line is disruptive. Streets close for months, and shops along the route lose trade while the work goes on. Good schemes build in short sections, keep every shop reachable on foot, and help the businesses that suffer most.
:::

## What makes a scheme succeed

1. **Choose the busiest corridor.** A tram on a quiet route is an expensive bus. The first line must go where people already travel in large numbers.
2. **Give the line its own space.** A vehicle stuck in car traffic loses most of its advantage. Separate track, and priority at traffic lights, are what make it fast.
3. **Link it with buses.** Buses should feed the tram at its stops, not compete with it along the same street.
4. **Plan homes and jobs along the line.** Freiburg built whole districts around new lines, so residents had a service from the day they moved in.
5. **Design the stops as places.** A stop is where people wait, meet and shop. It deserves shelter, seats, good lighting and a clear walk to the pavement.

## Where trams struggle

- **Narrow old streets** may have no room for track, a stop and a pavement at once.
- **Low demand** cannot justify the cost, however attractive the tram.
- **Long build times** test the patience of residents and politicians alike.
- **Poor links with buses** leave whole districts without good access to the line.

These are reasons to plan carefully, not reasons to give up. Most cities that opened new lines in recent decades extended their networks afterwards, which suggests the first lines did what was promised.
