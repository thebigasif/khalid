VIENNA: Vienna council housing works because the city has kept building it for a hundred years, pays for it with steady money that does not depend on each year's budget, and builds homes that people from every income want to live in. Other cities can copy the method, even if they cannot copy the century.

Around half of the people in Vienna live in a flat owned by the city or built with its subsidy. In most European cities, the figure is far lower, and council housing is something people hope to leave. In Vienna, it is ordinary. This article looks at how that came about and which parts travel.

## A century of Vienna council housing

The city began building homes on a large scale in the 1920s, when its housing was among the worst in Europe. Between 1919 and 1934, a period remembered as Red Vienna, it built tens of thousands of flats, paid for by a new tax on housing and on luxuries.

The most famous estate of that time is the Karl-Marx-Hof, finished in 1930. It is more than a kilometre long and holds around 1,400 flats. Only a small part of its site is covered by buildings. The rest is courtyards and gardens, with laundries, kindergartens and a library built in.

## How Vienna pays for it

The method matters more than the buildings. Vienna council housing rests on money that arrives every year, whatever the politics.

- **A charge on wages.** Employers and employees each pay a small share of wages into a housing fund. The money is set aside for building and renovating homes.
- **Land bought early.** The city buys land long before it builds, so it does not pay the price of land that already has planning permission.
- **Limited-profit builders.** Housing associations build much of the newer stock. They may make a small profit, but they must put it back into housing.
- **Rents set by cost.** Rents cover the cost of building and running the homes, not what the market would pay.

:::callout{kind=summary}
- Steady money, not a one-off budget.
- Land bought before prices rise.
- Builders who reinvest their profits.
- Homes for middle incomes as well as low ones.
:::

## Who lives in Vienna council housing

In most cities, social housing is for people on the lowest incomes. In Vienna, the income limits are set high enough that most households qualify. That keeps the estates mixed. A nurse, a bus driver and a retired teacher can be neighbours, and nobody can tell from the street who earns what.

Mixing matters for the buildings too, because Vienna council housing gets the attention of everyone who lives in it. When people with a voice at the town hall live in council flats, the flats get repaired.

| Private rented flats in old buildings | Private landlords | Anyone | Partly controlled by law |
| Private rented flats in new buildings | Private landlords | Anyone | Market rent |
| Owner-occupied flats | Owners | Anyone who can buy | Not rented |

## What other cities can learn

Few cities can build at Vienna's scale, so they cannot simply copy Vienna council housing. But three parts of the method travel well.

1. **Separate the money from the yearly budget.** A fund with its own income keeps building through bad years.
2. **Buy land ahead of need.** Land is often the largest cost. A city that owns it can build cheaper homes for decades.
3. **Design for people who have a choice.** When council homes are good enough for the middle class, they stay good for everyone.
