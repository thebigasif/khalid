Timber buildings can now go to around twenty storeys and more, using engineered wood panels and beams that are far stronger and more predictable than ordinary sawn timber. The limit today is set less by the strength of wood than by fire rules, the movement of a light building in the wind, and the cost of the engineering.

For most of the twentieth century, nobody built tall in wood. Fire codes limited timber buildings to a few storeys, and steel and concrete did everything else. That has changed in the last twenty years, and engineered timber is now a serious choice for flats, offices and schools. This article explains the materials, the limits and where wood makes sense.

## The materials that changed timber buildings

The change came from new ways of gluing wood together. Instead of using a single piece cut from a tree, factories now press layers of wood into panels and beams of almost any size.

| Product | How it is made | What it is used for |
|---|---|---|
| Cross-laminated timber (CLT) | Layers of boards glued with the grain of each layer at right angles to the last | Walls, floors and roofs, as large flat panels |
| Glued laminated timber (glulam) | Layers of boards glued with the grain all in one direction | Beams and columns, straight or curved |
| Laminated veneer lumber (LVL) | Thin sheets of wood glued together, like a very thick plywood | Beams and frames where high strength is needed |
| Sawn timber | Cut directly from a log | Small houses, roofs and frames |

Cross-laminated timber is the material most people mean when they talk about tall timber buildings. Because the layers cross, a panel is strong in both directions and hardly swells or shrinks. Panels are cut to size in a factory, with openings for doors and windows already made, and they arrive on site ready to lift into place.

## How tall timber buildings have grown

In 2019, an 18-storey building of flats, a hotel and offices opened in Brumunddal, Norway. Called Mjøstårnet, it was among the tallest timber buildings in the world when it opened, and taller ones have followed since in other countries. Most such towers use timber for the main structure and concrete for a few parts, such as the foundations, or the top floors, which add weight to steady the building against the wind.

## What limits the height

- **Fire.** Wood burns, but a thick timber beam burns slowly and predictably. The outside chars, and the char protects the core. Engineers size the timber so enough remains after a fire of a set length. Many codes still require timber to be covered with plasterboard above a certain height.
- **Wind and movement.** Timber is light, and a light, tall building sways more in the wind. People notice movement long before the structure is at risk, so comfort sets the limit, not safety.
- **Sound.** Timber floors carry footsteps more easily than concrete. Good schemes add a layer of screed and a separate ceiling.
- **Cost and skill.** Few builders have built tall in timber, and insurers and lenders are still learning to price the risk.

:::callout{kind=note}
Timber stores the carbon the tree took in as it grew, for as long as the building stands. That benefit holds only if the forest is replanted, and if the building lasts. A timber building demolished after thirty years loses most of the advantage.
:::

## Where timber buildings make the most sense

The strongest case for timber is not the tallest tower. It is the ordinary building of four to ten storeys: flats, schools and offices, where timber is quick to build, light on its foundations, and pleasant inside. Building on top of existing buildings is another good use, because a light timber extension can add floors that a concrete one could not.

Timber is also quiet and clean to build with. Panels arrive ready to fit, so the site has fewer deliveries, less noise and less dust, which matters in a crowded street.
