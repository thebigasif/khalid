LONDON: The Barbican concrete looks rough and stony because workers hammered most of its surfaces by hand after the concrete had set, chipping away the smooth outer skin to expose the crushed stone inside. The result is a surface that looks more like rock than like cast concrete, and it has weathered far better than the smooth concrete of many buildings from the same years.

The Barbican Estate, in the City of London, was built from the 1960s into the early 1980s on land left empty by wartime bombing. It holds about two thousand flats, a school, a library and a large arts centre, linked by raised walkways and a lake. People argue about whether they like it. Almost everyone who sees it up close agrees that its concrete is unusual.

## How the Barbican concrete was finished

Concrete is a mix of cement, sand, water and aggregate, the crushed stone or gravel that gives it bulk. When it is poured into a mould, the cement and fine sand rise to the surface and leave a smooth, grey skin. That skin is what most people picture when they think of concrete.

On the Barbican, the builders broke that skin. Working by hand with pointed hammers, they went over the columns, balconies and walls, chipping away the outer surface. Underneath was the aggregate, a mix of stone that gives the surface its rough texture and its varied, warm grey colour.

## Concrete finishes compared

| Finish | How it is made | How it looks | How it ages |
|---|---|---|---|
| Smooth cast | Left as it comes from a smooth mould | Flat, even grey | Shows every stain and streak |
| Board-marked | Cast against rough timber boards | The grain of the wood printed in the surface | Fairly well, the texture hides some stains |
| Exposed aggregate | Surface washed or brushed before it sets | Small stones visible in a cement ground | Well |
| Bush-hammered or pick-hammered | Surface hammered after it sets | Rough, stony, with the aggregate broken open | Very well, it weathers like stone |
| Polished | Ground and polished smooth | Glossy, with the aggregate cut flat | Well indoors, slippery outdoors |

The Barbican concrete belongs in the fourth row. The rough surface catches the light, so rain streaks and dirt show far less than on a smooth wall. Water runs off unevenly, so no single stripe of staining forms. The building looks older and more settled each decade, which is not what most people expect of concrete.

## Why the hammering mattered

- **It hid the joins.** The marks of the moulds, which show on smooth concrete as lines and patches, were hammered away.
- **It made the colour warmer.** The stone inside the mix is warmer and more varied than the grey cement skin.
- **It made the surface age well.** A rough surface wears evenly, like a cliff, rather than staining like a painted wall.
- **It made the scale human.** Up close, the rough texture gives the eye something to rest on, which large smooth walls do not.

:::callout{kind=note}
Hammering concrete is slow, noisy and costly, and it was heavy work for the people who did it. Few modern buildings use it on such a scale. But the Barbican concrete shows that how a material is finished can matter as much as the material itself.
:::

## A listed estate

The estate is now listed, which protects it from major changes. The flats are much sought after, the arts centre is busy, and the lake and gardens are among the calmest places in the City. Its raised walkways, meant to separate people from traffic, are harder to find your way around than the planners hoped, and visitors still get lost.
