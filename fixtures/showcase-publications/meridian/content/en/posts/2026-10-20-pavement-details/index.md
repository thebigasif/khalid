You can read a pavement by looking at four things: the kerb, the surface, the textured paving at crossings and the covers set into the ground. Each one records a decision about who the street is for, and many of them are older than the buildings beside them.

Most people never look down except to avoid a puddle. But the ground of a city is one of its most lasting records. Kerbs outlive shops, setts outlive cars, and drain covers often carry the date they were made. This article is a short field guide to what you are walking on.

## The kerb

The kerb is the edge between the pavement and the road. Its height tells you who the street was built for. A high kerb, of the kind found on many busy roads, marks a clear border: cars on one side, people on the other. A low kerb, or none at all, says the street is shared.

Old kerbs are often granite, cut in long blocks that have lasted a century or more. Newer ones are usually concrete. Where a kerb drops to the level of the road at a crossing, it was lowered for people in wheelchairs and with pushchairs, a change many cities made only in the last few decades.

## Setts, cobbles and slabs

| What you see | What it is | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|
| Rounded stones, set loosely | Cobbles, gathered from rivers or fields | An old street, often from before modern roads |
| Squared stone blocks in rows | Setts, cut from granite or other hard stone | A street built for carts and heavy traffic |
| Large flat stone slabs | Flagstones | An old pavement for people on foot, often in a town centre |
| Small concrete blocks in patterns | Block paving | A street rebuilt in the last fifty years |
| Smooth asphalt | Tarmac | A street resurfaced for cars, or repaired in a hurry |
| Textured slabs with raised bumps | Tactile paving | A crossing or a platform edge, for people who cannot see |

People often call setts cobbles, but the two are different. Cobbles are rounded and uneven, and they were laid where the stone was found. Setts are cut to a square shape and laid in tight rows. They are flatter and easier to walk on, though still hard work for bikes and wheelchairs.

True cobbles: rounded river stones set loosely in the ground. They are older than setts and far harder to walk on.
:::

## Tactile paving

The slabs with raised bumps at crossings are tactile paving. They tell people who cannot see that they are at the edge of the pavement. The pattern matters. Round bumps in a grid mark a crossing. Long raised bars mark a platform edge or the top of steps. When the paving is laid in the wrong place, or worn smooth, it misleads the very people it is meant to guide.

## Covers and grates

The iron and concrete covers set into a pavement lead to drains, water pipes, gas valves and cables. Old iron covers are often cast with patterns and sometimes with the name of the foundry and a date, which makes them small historical records. Grates at the kerb take rainwater from the street. Their spacing tells you how much rain the engineers expected and how steep the street is.

:::callout{kind=tip}
On your next walk, look for the oldest piece of pavement you can find. It is often at the foot of a church, a bank or a town hall, where the stone was paid for by an owner who expected it to last.
:::

## What a good pavement does

- **It is wide enough.** Two people should be able to pass a pushchair without stepping into the road.
- **It is level.** Uneven slabs trip people, and older people fall on them.
- **It drains.** Puddles at crossings push people into traffic.
- **It is clear.** Signs, bins and street furniture belong on one edge, not scattered across the middle.
- **It is continuous.** Where a side street meets it, the pavement should carry on across at the same level, so drivers turn in slowly and give way.
