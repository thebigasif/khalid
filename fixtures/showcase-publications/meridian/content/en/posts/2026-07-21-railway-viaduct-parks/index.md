PARIS: Paris turned a disused railway viaduct in the east of the city into a long planted walk on top and a row of workshops in the arches below. The Promenade Plantée opened in 1993, and it is often described as the first park built on an elevated railway line.

A railway viaduct is one of the hardest structures in a city to reuse. It is long, narrow and too strong to knock down cheaply. Paris found a use for both its top and its underside, and the idea has since spread to cities on several continents. This article looks at how the Paris scheme works and what other cities took from it.

## A line that closed

The viaduct once carried trains from the Bastille towards the eastern suburbs. The line closed in 1969, and for years the viaduct stood empty, with the arches bricked up or used for storage. The city could have demolished it, but demolition would have been costly and would have left a long scar through the neighbourhood.

Instead, the city planted the top as a garden walk and restored the arches below as shops and workshops. The walk continues beyond the viaduct on embankments and through cuttings, and it runs for more than four kilometres in all, almost to the edge of the Bois de Vincennes.

## Two uses for one railway viaduct

| Level | Before | After |
|---|---|---|
| The top of the viaduct | Tracks and ballast | A planted walk with trees, roses and benches |
| The arches below | Bricked up or used for storage | Glazed fronts with craft workshops, galleries and shops |
| The street beside it | A blank wall | A row of shop windows, open in the daytime |
| The neighbourhood | Cut in two by an empty structure | Joined by a walk that people use every day |

The arches are as important as the garden. Each one was cleaned, repaired and given a glazed front. The section beside the Avenue Daumesnil, known as the Viaduc des Arts, now holds furniture makers, instrument makers and other craft workshops. The street, once lined with a blank wall, now has shop windows for several hundred metres.

## Why the Paris railway viaduct works

- **It goes somewhere.** The walk links real places: a busy junction at one end and a large park at the other. People use it to get somewhere, not only to stroll.
- **It is narrow and planted.** Trees and hedges break up the long straight line, so the walk never feels like a corridor.
- **It has a life below.** The workshops bring people to the street at all hours of the working day, so the viaduct is never a dead wall.
- **It is quiet.** Raised above the traffic, the walk is calmer than any street around it.

:::callout{kind=note}
Many cities have copied the garden on top and forgotten the arches below. A planted walk on a railway viaduct with blank walls underneath brings visitors to the top, but it does little for the street.
:::

## What other cities copied

The best-known copy is the High Line in New York, which opened on an old freight line in 2009. It became one of the most visited places in the city, and the streets beside it changed quickly. Rents and land values rose sharply, which brought new buildings and pushed out some older businesses. Our article on [reading neighbourhood change](/blog/read-neighbourhood-change/) looks at that pattern.

Smaller cities have done more modest versions: short walks on old bridges and viaducts, often with a café or a market in the arches. The lesson in every case is the same. A railway viaduct is valuable because it is long, strong and already there.
