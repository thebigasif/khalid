Turning offices into homes works when the building is shallow enough for daylight to reach every room, has windows that can open, has stairs and pipes in the right places, and is converted under rules that set a decent minimum for space and light. When those conditions are missing, the result is often small, dark flats that nobody would choose.

Since more people began working from home, many cities have more office space than they need, and less housing. Turning offices into homes sounds like an easy answer. Some conversions are excellent. Others have given the idea a bad name. This article explains the difference.

## Why offices into homes is harder than it looks

An office and a flat ask for different things from a building. An office wants deep, open floors, lots of power, and air conditioning. A flat wants daylight in every main room, windows that open, a kitchen and a bathroom with water and drains, and quiet.

The hardest of these is daylight. Daylight reaches only a few metres into a room from a window. An office floor may be twenty metres or more from one window wall to the next, with the middle lit by lamps all day. That middle cannot become bedrooms.

## How to tell if offices into homes will work

| Feature | Good for conversion | Hard to convert |
|---|---|---|
| Depth of floor from window to window | Shallow, so every room can reach a window | Deep, with a dark core |
| Windows | Many, regular, able to open or be replaced | Sealed glass walls |
| Floor-to-ceiling height | Generous, room for new pipes and ducts | Low, with services already in the ceiling |
| Stairs and lifts | Two or more stair cores, spread out | One central core |
| Structure | Regular columns, floors that can take new walls | Long spans with little spare strength |
| Location | Near shops, schools, parks and transport | In an office park far from daily life |

A building that scores well on most rows can make good flats. A building that fails on depth and windows usually cannot, unless the owner cuts a courtyard or light well into the middle. That is costly, but it can turn a deep block into a ring of good homes around a garden, much like the [courtyard blocks](/blog/courtyard-blocks/) of older cities.

Stairs decide a great deal. Homes need safe ways out from every floor, and a building with a single stair often needs a second before anyone can live there.
:::

## The rules that make the difference

Some places have allowed offices to become homes without full planning permission. That was meant to speed up housing, and it did produce many flats quickly. But where the rules did not set space and daylight standards, some of those flats were very small, and some had no proper windows. Many cities have since tightened their rules for offices into homes.

Good rules for offices into homes set:

- **A minimum floor area for each flat**, the same as for new homes.
- **Daylight in every main room**, with a window that opens.
- **Fire safety for homes**, which is stricter than for offices, especially for escape stairs.
- **Somewhere to put bins and bikes**, which offices never needed.
- **A share of affordable homes**, where the local rules ask the same of new buildings.

:::callout{kind=tip}
If you are looking at a flat in a converted office, ask to see it on a dull afternoon and stand at the back of each room. If you need the lights on to read, the conversion was too deep.
:::

## What a good conversion looks like

A good conversion of offices into homes keeps the best parts of the office: the high ceilings, the large windows, the strong floors. It adds what homes need, and it does not try to squeeze in more flats than the plan allows. Shops or a café on the ground floor help the building join the street, which helps the whole area come back to life.
