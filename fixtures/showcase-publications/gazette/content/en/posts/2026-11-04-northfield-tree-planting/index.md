More than 90 volunteers turned out for the Northfield tree planting on Sunday and put in 600 young trees, the first part of a plan to line every street on the estate with trees. The trees went in along the edge of the playing field and on the green by the shops.

The planting was organised by the Northfield residents' group with the town council and a woodland charity, which gave the trees for free. Group chair Femi Adeyemi said people kept arriving all morning. "We had spades for 50," he said. "By ten o'clock we were sharing them in turns."

## What was planted on the Northfield estate

The trees are mostly native kinds: oak, rowan, field maple, hazel and wild cherry. Each one is about a metre tall and has a guard to keep rabbits off. The rowans and cherries were planted close to the paths, so people on the estate will see them flower in spring.

The woodland charity says the trees should take about 20 years to grow into a small wood along the field. Until then, the residents' group will water them in dry summers, with a rota of volunteers.

## Why the estate wanted trees

The Northfield estate was built in the 1970s with wide grass verges and almost no trees. A survey by the residents' group last year found that the estate had fewer trees per home than any other part of Marlbeck. Children from the estate's primary school helped plant the trees nearest the school gate.

## The next tree planting

The second planting, of about 400 trees, is planned for February along Northfield Road and the new bus stops on the [Marlbeck High route](/blog/high-school-bus-route/). Volunteers can sign up with the residents' group. No experience is needed, and the group lends spades and gloves.

Research gathered by the [Woodland Trust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woodland_Trust) shows that street trees cool streets in summer and slow rainwater, which matters in a town that floods.
