The debt avalanche pays off the debt with the highest interest rate first, and the debt snowball pays off the smallest balance first. On our three made-up debts, with the same 500 a month, the avalanche cost 70 less in interest and the snowball cleared its first debt twelve months sooner. Both finished in the same month.

That result surprises people who expect a large gap. This study sets out the debts, the monthly sums and the order each method follows, so you can see where the gap comes from and judge which matters more to you: the lower total or the early win.

::toc

:::callout{kind=note}
**This is not financial advice.** The debts, rates and budget are made up. If your debts feel unmanageable, a free debt advice service can look at your whole situation.
:::

## The three debts in this study

The household owes three debts and can put 500 a month towards them in total. It always pays every minimum first, then puts whatever is left on one target debt.

| Debt | Balance | Interest a year | Minimum payment |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Credit card | 4,000 | 24 in 100 | 3 in 100 of the balance, at least 25 |
| Store card | 900 | 18 in 100 | 3 in 100 of the balance, at least 25 |
| Car loan | 6,000 | 7 in 100 | 180 fixed |
| **Total** | **10,900** | | about 330 in the first month |

The minimums add up to about 330 in the first month, so about 170 is left for the target debt. As each debt is cleared, its minimum is freed up and added to the next target. That rolling of payments is the same in both methods. Only the order changes.

## The debt avalanche: highest rate first

The debt avalanche lists debts by interest rate, highest first. Every spare unit goes to the credit card until it is gone, then to the store card, then to the car loan.

The logic is pure arithmetic. Each unit paid off the credit card stops 24 in 100 of interest a year, which is more than any other debt charges. Paying the most expensive debt first means less interest builds up overall.

## The debt snowball: smallest balance first

The debt snowball lists debts by balance, smallest first. Every spare unit goes to the store card, which is only 900, then to the credit card, then to the car loan.

The [debt snowball method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Debt-snowball_method) rests on behaviour, not arithmetic. Clearing a whole debt in a few months gives a visible win and one fewer bill to think about. Many people find that keeps them going.

## The two methods month by month

We ran both methods through a spreadsheet one month at a time, adding each month's interest before the payment, as lenders do.

| Milestone | Debt avalanche | Debt snowball |
|---|---:|---:|
| First debt cleared | Month 17 (credit card) | Month 5 (store card) |
| Second debt cleared | Month 19 (store card) | Month 19 (credit card) |
| All debts cleared | Month 25 | Month 25 |
| Total interest paid | 1,496 | 1,566 |
| Total paid | 12,396 | 12,466 |

The debt avalanche saved 70 in interest over two years. That gap is small here because the store card is small and its rate is not far below the credit card's. The gap grows when the smallest debt is also the cheapest and the largest is the most expensive.

## When the gap grows

We changed one thing at a time to see what widens the gap between the debt avalanche and the snowball.

| Change to the example | Interest saved by the avalanche |
|---|---:|
| None, the example above | 70 |
| Store card balance 2,500 instead of 900 | about 190 |
| Credit card rate 34 in 100 instead of 24 | about 220 |
| Budget 400 a month instead of 500 | about 100 |

The pattern is clear. The debt avalanche saves the most when your expensive debt is large and your monthly budget is tight. With small differences in rate, the snowball costs little extra.

## Which one to choose

Both methods only work if you keep paying every month and stop adding new debt. So the best choice is the one you will stick with.

- **Choose the debt avalanche** if you are steady with plans and want the lowest total cost.
- **Choose the snowball** if you have tried before and given up, or if fewer bills would make life calmer.
- **Mix them** if one small debt is close to cleared: finish it first, then switch to the highest rate.

:::callout{kind=warning}
Before either method, check that you are paying at least the minimum on every debt. A missed minimum brings fees and can raise the rate, which costs more than either method saves.
:::

Two things make either method work better. First, a small emergency fund stops the next repair going back on the card; our post on [working out your emergency fund size](/blog/emergency-fund-size/) suggests starting with 1,000. Second, keep paying more than the minimum on cards: our study of [credit card minimum payments](/blog/credit-card-minimum-payments/) shows how long a balance lasts when you pay only that.

If your rates are high everywhere, a single cheaper loan may help, but only if the sums work. We set them out in [how to tell if a debt consolidation loan is cheaper](/blog/debt-consolidation-loan/). And to compare rates fairly, read [what the APR on a loan tells you](/blog/apr-interest-rate/).
