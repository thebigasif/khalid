A yearly budget check takes one evening and seven steps: start from your current pay, compare your plan with what you really spent, and reset each line for the year ahead. Done once a year, it catches the slow changes a monthly glance misses, such as a bill that crept up or a subscription nobody uses. In our worked example, one household raised its planned saving by 110 a month in a single evening.

Most budgets are set once and then followed, or not, for years. Pay rises, prices rise, children grow, and the plan stays where it was. A budget check fixes that. We suggest the same evening every year, such as the first quiet evening in November, with the past twelve months of statements, this month's payslip and a pot of tea.

::toc

## What you need on the table

- This month's payslip, or your last few months of income if it varies.
- Twelve months of bank and card statements.
- The budget you used, if you had one.
- Your list of yearly bills and the balance of each savings pot.
- A pencil. Changes are easier to make on paper first.

:::figure{src="kitchen-table.jpg" alt="A round wooden kitchen table with upholstered chairs in a bright kitchen and dining room, the kind of table where a yearly budget check gets done"}
A kitchen table, a year of statements and a pencil are enough. Most households finish in about two and a half hours.
:::

## A budget check in seven parts

| Step | What you do | Time |
|---:|---|---:|
| 1 | Find your steady monthly pay | 10 minutes |
| 2 | Add up twelve months of spending by category | 45 minutes |
| 3 | Compare spending with the plan | 20 minutes |
| 4 | Check every regular bill and subscription | 20 minutes |
| 5 | Update the yearly bills list | 15 minutes |
| 6 | Check each savings pot against its target | 15 minutes |
| 7 | Write the new plan | 30 minutes |

### Step 1: find your steady monthly pay

Start the budget check from what actually reaches your account, without overtime or bonuses you cannot count on. Our guide to [reading every line of a payslip](/blog/read-payslip/) shows where to find it. If your pay rose this year, this is where the extra money first appears.

### Step 2: add up a year of spending

Go through the statements and total each category for the year, then divide by twelve. If this feels like too much, track one month in detail instead. Our [thirty-day tracking exercise with eleven households](/blog/track-spending-thirty-days/) showed that a single month reveals most of the pattern.

### Step 3: compare spending with the plan

Set the real monthly figures beside the old plan. Mark each line that was more than a tenth over or under. Those lines are where the plan and your life have drifted apart.

### Step 4: check every regular bill

List each direct debit and subscription. For each one ask: do we still use it, and has the price changed? Prices often rise once a year with little notice.

### Step 5: update the yearly bills list

Add any new yearly cost from the past twelve months, and update each amount to today's price. Then reset the monthly sum for your [sinking funds for once-a-year bills](/blog/sinking-funds-yearly-bills/).

### Step 6: check each savings pot

Compare your emergency fund with your essential costs, which may have risen. Our guide to [how many months an emergency fund should cover](/blog/emergency-fund-size/) gives the method. Then check any goal pots, such as a deposit, against their targets.

### Step 7: write the new plan

Write the new plan with the method that suits you. If the old method did not stick, the evening is a good moment to try another; we set three side by side in [budgeting methods compared](/blog/budgeting-methods-compared/).

## A worked example: one household's evening

A made-up household of two adults ran this budget check in November. Here is what changed.

| Line | Old plan | Real spending | New plan | Change |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Steady pay | 3,000 | 3,090 | 3,090 | +90 |
| Energy | 170 | 200 | 200 | +30 |
| Subscriptions | 40 | 72 | 15 | -25 |
| Phone and internet | 80 | 80 | 45 | -35 |
| Car insurance fund | 40 | 40 | 50 | +10 |
| Eating out | 200 | 260 | 200 | 0 |
| Saving | 300 | 250 | 410 | +110 |

The pay rise of 90 a month had been absorbed into everyday spending without anyone noticing. Cancelling two unused subscriptions and moving to a cheaper phone deal at the end of a contract cut 60 from the old plan. Energy and the car insurance fund went up to their real levels, and eating out was held at 200. The household can now plan to save 410 a month: 110 more than the old plan, and 160 more than it actually saved.

## Keep the budget check short

:::callout{kind=tip}
Do not try to fix everything in one evening. Choose the three changes that free the most money and make those. Leave the rest for next year.
:::

Small fees deserve a look in every budget check too. A monthly charge of a few units feels like nothing, but our note on [account fees over ten years](/blog/account-fees-ten-years/) shows how much they add up to. For more on the method behind any [personal budget](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personal_budget), the reference pages are a good start.

:::callout{kind=summary}
- **Once a year,** one evening, seven steps.
- **Start from steady pay,** then compare plan with real spending.
- **Check every bill,** update yearly costs and savings pots.
- **Make three changes,** not twenty.
:::
