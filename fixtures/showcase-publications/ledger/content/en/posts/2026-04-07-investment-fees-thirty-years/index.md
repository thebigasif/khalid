A yearly investment fee of 1 in 100 looks small, but over thirty years it takes about a third of the growth on your savings. In our worked example, 10,000 left to grow for thirty years ends at 43,219 with no fee and at 32,434 with that fee, so the fee costs 10,785, more than the sum first put in. The reason is that investment fees compound in the same way growth does.

This research note shows the arithmetic step by step, so you can run it on any fee you are quoted. It does not say where to invest or which products to buy. It shows what a fee costs over time, which is the one part of investing you can know in advance.

::toc

:::callout{kind=note}
**This is not financial advice.** The growth rate below is an assumption chosen to make the arithmetic clear, not a forecast. Real investments rise and fall, and past growth does not promise future growth.
:::

## The assumptions in this note

Every worked example rests on assumptions. Here are ours, stated once so you can change them.

- **Starting sum:** 10,000, put in once at the start and never added to.
- **Growth before fees:** 5 in 100 a year, the same every year. Real growth is never this smooth.
- **Fees:** taken once a year as a share of the whole pot, which is how most ongoing investment fees work.
- **Nothing else:** no tax, no inflation, no withdrawals.

With a fee of 1 in 100, growth of 5 becomes growth of 4. That one unit is the whole story of this note.

## How investment fees compound

A fee charged as a share of the pot is not a fixed cost. It grows as the pot grows, and every unit taken out is a unit that never grows again. So the cost of the fee compounds, just as growth does.

| Year | Pot with no fee | Pot with 1 in 100 fee | Fee's total cost so far |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 10,500 | 10,400 | 100 |
| 5 | 12,763 | 12,167 | 596 |
| 10 | 16,289 | 14,802 | 1,487 |
| 20 | 26,533 | 21,911 | 4,622 |
| 30 | 43,219 | 32,434 | 10,785 |

In year one the fee costs 100. By year thirty it has cost 10,785. The gap widens every year because the fee is taken from a larger pot each time, and because the money it took is no longer there to grow.

## Five fee levels side by side

The same 10,000 at the same growth, with five different yearly fees.

| Yearly fee | After 10 years | After 20 years | After 30 years | Share of 30 years' growth lost |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| None | 16,289 | 26,533 | 43,219 | 0 in 100 |
| 0.25 in 100 | 15,905 | 25,298 | 40,237 | 9 in 100 |
| 0.5 in 100 | 15,530 | 24,117 | 37,453 | 17 in 100 |
| 1 in 100 | 14,802 | 21,911 | 32,434 | 32 in 100 |
| 1.5 in 100 | 14,106 | 19,898 | 28,068 | 46 in 100 |

The last column is the useful one. Without fees, the 10,000 grows by 33,219 in thirty years. A fee of 1 in 100 takes 10,785 of that, or about 32 in every 100 units of growth. A fee of 1.5 takes almost half.

## With regular saving instead of one sum

Most people save a little each month rather than once. We ran the same sums for 200 a month over thirty years, 72,000 put in altogether.

| Yearly fee | Pot after 30 years | Cost of the fee |
|---|---:|---:|
| None | 163,075 | 0 |
| 0.5 in 100 | 149,413 | 13,662 |
| 1 in 100 | 137,054 | 26,021 |
| 1.5 in 100 | 125,870 | 37,205 |

The pattern holds. Money saved in the early years is exposed to the fee for longest, so it carries most of the cost.

## The fees to look for

Investment fees go by many names. When you compare two options, add up all the yearly ones, because they compound together.

| Fee | When it is charged | Compounds over time |
|---|---|---|
| Ongoing charge or fund fee | Every year, as a share of the pot | Yes |
| Platform or account fee | Every year, as a share or a fixed sum | Yes, if a share |
| Adviser fee | Every year, as a share, or once as a fixed sum | Yes, if a yearly share |
| Dealing or transaction cost | Each time you buy or sell | Only if you trade often |
| Entry or exit fee | Once, when you join or leave | No |

A fixed yearly fee, such as 30 a year, does not grow with the pot. On a small pot it can still cost more than a share would, so work it out both ways.

## How to check a fee you are quoted

You need three numbers: the yearly fee as a share of the pot, your starting sum or monthly saving, and how many years you will leave it. Then run the sum both with and without the fee.

1. Take your assumed growth rate and subtract the yearly fee.
2. Work out the pot at both rates, using any compound interest calculator.
3. Subtract one from the other. That difference is what the fee costs you.

If compounding is new to you, start with [compound interest explained with one jar](/blog/compound-interest-explained/), which builds the idea from a single year. And the same arithmetic applies to small fixed charges on everyday accounts: our note on [what small account fees add up to](/blog/account-fees-ten-years/) runs it on monthly fees.

- **Investment fees compound,** because they are a share of a growing pot.
- **A yearly fee of 1 in 100** took about a third of thirty years' growth in our example.
- **Add up every yearly fee** before you compare two options.
- **Run the sum yourself:** growth with the fee, growth without, and the difference.
