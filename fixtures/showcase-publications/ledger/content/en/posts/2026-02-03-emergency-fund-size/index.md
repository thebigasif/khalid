An emergency fund should cover your essential costs for three to six months, and the right point in that range depends on how steady your income is and how many people rely on it. For the made-up household in this post, essential costs are 1,800 a month, so the fund should hold between 5,400 and 10,800. The rest of this post shows how to find your own number and how long it takes to save.

An emergency fund, which economists call [precautionary saving](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Precautionary_savings), is money kept for things that are both necessary and unplanned: a lost job, a broken boiler, a car repair you need to get to work. It is not for holidays or yearly bills, which you can see coming.

:::callout{kind=note}
**This is not financial advice.** The household and its costs are made up, with round numbers. Use the method with your own figures.
:::

## Start with essential costs, not total spending

The size of an emergency fund is measured in months of essential costs. Essential costs are what you would still have to pay if your income stopped tomorrow and you cut everything else.

| Essential cost | Per month |
|---|---:|
| Rent or mortgage | 900 |
| Food and household goods | 400 |
| Energy and water | 180 |
| Transport to work | 150 |
| Insurance | 60 |
| Minimum debt payments | 110 |
| **Essential total** | **1,800** |

This household's total spending is about 2,400 a month. The other 600 covers eating out, hobbies, subscriptions and clothes. In an emergency those stop, so they do not count towards the fund.

## Three months or six: what decides it

The usual range is three to six months. Where you sit in it depends on how quickly your income could come back and how many people depend on it.

| Your situation | Months to aim for | For this household |
|---|---:|---:|
| Two steady incomes, either could cover essentials | 3 | 5,400 |
| One steady income | 4 | 7,200 |
| One income and children | 5 | 9,000 |
| Self-employed or irregular income | 6 | 10,800 |

Lean towards the higher end if your work is seasonal, if it would take a long time to find a similar job, or if your home or car is old enough that large repairs are likely.

## How long an emergency fund takes to build

Few households can put aside thousands at once. The table below shows how many months it takes to reach each target at three saving rates, ignoring interest, which makes little difference over these periods.

| Target | 150 a month | 250 a month | 400 a month |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 1,000 starter fund | 7 | 4 | 3 |
| 3 months: 5,400 | 36 | 22 | 14 |
| 6 months: 10,800 | 72 | 44 | 27 |

Thirty-six months is a long time. That is why we suggest a starter fund of 1,000 first. It covers most single repairs, and reaching it in a few months shows the habit works. Then build towards the full target.

If you are paying off expensive debt at the same time, the starter fund matters even more. Without it, the next repair goes back on the card. Our comparison of [paying off debt with the avalanche or snowball method](/blog/debt-avalanche-snowball/) assumes a starter fund is already in place.

## Where to keep it

An emergency fund has three jobs: be there when you need it, be hard to spend by accident, and lose as little value as possible while it waits.

:::figure{src="jars-by-window.jpg" alt="Three glass jars wrapped in knitted covers on a table by a window, kept apart from everyday things the way an emergency fund should be"}
Keep the fund apart from everyday money, in a separate account with its own name, so it is never spent by accident.
:::

- **Keep it separate.** A separate savings account, with a name such as "Emergency only", stops it being spent on ordinary things.
- **Keep it reachable.** You should be able to move it to your current account within a day or two.
- **Expect it to shrink slowly.** Cash loses value when prices rise faster than its interest. Our study of [what inflation does to cash savings](/blog/inflation-cash-savings/) shows by how much over five years.

## When to use it, and how to refill it

Before you spend from the fund, ask two questions: was this unplanned, and was it necessary? If both answers are yes, use it without guilt. That is what it is for.

Then refill it as a fixed line in your budget, the same way you built it. A [zero-based budget](/blog/budgeting-methods-compared/) makes this easy, because the refill is a planned line like rent.

Costs you can see coming, such as car insurance or birthdays, belong in a separate pot. Our guide to [saving for bills that come once a year](/blog/sinking-funds-yearly-bills/) explains how. Keeping the two apart stops the emergency fund being emptied by things that were never emergencies.

If you are saving for something large as well, such as [a deposit on a first home](/blog/saving-home-deposit/), finish the starter fund first. A deposit fund that pays for a car repair is no longer a deposit fund.

:::callout{kind=summary}
- **Count essential costs only,** not total spending.
- **Aim for three to six months,** higher when income is less steady.
- **Start with 1,000,** then build to the full target.
- **Keep it separate and reachable,** and refill it after every use.
:::
