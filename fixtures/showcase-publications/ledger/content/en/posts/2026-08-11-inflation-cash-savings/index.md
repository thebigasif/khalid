Inflation lowers what cash savings can buy whenever prices rise faster than the interest the cash earns. In our worked example, 10,000 earning 1 in 100 a year while prices rise by 3 in 100 a year grows to 10,510 in five years, but buys only what 9,066 bought at the start. The number in the account went up; what it can buy went down.

This research note works through that gap year by year and then across six pairs of interest and price rises. It explains one idea, the real return, which is the only measure of savings that tells you what your money can buy.

::toc

:::callout{kind=note}
**This is not financial advice.** The rates are round examples, not forecasts. Real inflation and real savings rates change from year to year and from country to country.
:::

## What inflation means for a basket of shopping

Inflation is the rise in prices across the things households buy, measured as a share each year. National statistics offices track it with a fixed "basket" of goods and services; the [general idea of inflation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inflation) is the same everywhere.

Here is one basket of shopping that costs 100 today, with prices rising at that pace each year.

| Year | Cost of the same basket |
|---:|---:|
| 0 | 100.0 |
| 1 | 103.0 |
| 2 | 106.1 |
| 3 | 109.3 |
| 4 | 112.6 |
| 5 | 115.9 |

After five years the same basket costs nearly 116. Like interest, inflation compounds: each year's rise is worked out on last year's higher price. The arithmetic is the same one we use in [compound interest explained](/blog/compound-interest-explained/).

## Inflation and cash savings, year by year

Now put 10,000 in a savings account paying 1 in 100 a year, and let prices rise at the same pace as the basket.

| Year | In the account | What it buys, in today's prices |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 0 | 10,000 | 10,000 |
| 1 | 10,100 | 9,806 |
| 2 | 10,201 | 9,615 |
| 3 | 10,303 | 9,429 |
| 4 | 10,406 | 9,246 |
| 5 | 10,510 | 9,066 |

The second column is what the statement shows. The third column is what matters: the money in the account, divided by how much prices have risen. After five years the saver has 510 more on paper and about 934 less in buying power.

## The real return, in one step

The real return is roughly the interest rate minus the inflation rate. Here, 1 minus 3 gives a real return of about minus 2 in 100 a year. When it is below zero, cash loses buying power. When it is above zero, cash gains it.

The exact sum divides rather than subtracts, but for small rates the shortcut is close enough. Economists call this the [real interest rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_interest_rate).

## Six pairs of interest and inflation

The same 10,000, left for five years, under six pairs of rates. The last column is its buying power at the end, in today's prices.

| Interest a year | Prices rise a year | Real return, roughly | Buying power after 5 years |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| 0 in 100 | 3 in 100 | -3 | 8,626 |
| 1 in 100 | 3 in 100 | -2 | 9,066 |
| 2 in 100 | 3 in 100 | -1 | 9,524 |
| 3 in 100 | 3 in 100 | 0 | 10,000 |
| 4 in 100 | 2 in 100 | +2 | 11,020 |
| 1 in 100 | 5 in 100 | -4 | 8,235 |

Cash in an account that pays no interest, the first row, loses almost a seventh of its buying power in five years at modest inflation. When prices rise fast, the last row, the loss is close to a fifth.

## What this means for a household

None of this means cash is a bad place for savings. It means cash has a job, and the job is not growth.

- **An emergency fund belongs in cash.** Its job is to be there on a bad day, not to grow. Losing a little buying power is the price of that. Our post on [emergency fund size](/blog/emergency-fund-size/) explains how much to keep.
- **Short-term goals belong in cash.** Money for a car next year or a deposit in three years needs to be there when you need it.
- **Shop around for interest.** On 10,000, the gap between earning 1 and earning 3 is about 930 of buying power over five years in our example.
- **Watch the fees.** A small monthly fee on a savings account cuts the real return further. Our note on [small account fees over ten years](/blog/account-fees-ten-years/) shows by how much.

For long-term goals, people often look beyond cash, which brings risk and its own costs. We do not recommend investments, but we do show what [yearly investment fees cost over thirty years](/blog/investment-fees-thirty-years/), so you can judge one part of that choice.

- **Inflation** raises prices, so the same money buys less each year.
- **Real return** is roughly interest minus inflation.
- **With interest below inflation,** 10,000 lost about 934 of buying power in five years.
- **Cash still has a job:** emergencies and short-term goals.
