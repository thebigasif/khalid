A payslip shows three things: what you earned before anything was taken off, what was taken off and why, and what reached your account. Reading it line by line takes about five minutes a month, and it is the only way to catch a wrong tax code, missed overtime or a pension contribution that never started.

I ran payroll for small employers for eight years. The most common question I heard was "why is my pay lower this month?", and the answer was nearly always printed on the payslip already. This guide walks through a made-up payslip with round numbers. Names and rates differ from country to country, but almost every payslip has the same four parts.

## The four parts of a payslip

Every payslip, whatever its layout, holds these parts:

1. **Who and when:** your name, your employer, your employee number and the pay period.
2. **Payments:** everything you earned before deductions, called gross pay.
3. **Deductions:** everything taken off, such as income tax, social insurance and your pension.
4. **Net pay:** what is paid into your account, sometimes called take-home pay.

How [payroll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Payroll) is run differs by country, but most payslips also show a year-to-date column, which adds up each line since the start of the tax year.

## A worked example payslip

Here is the payslip of a made-up employee, Ana Torres, for one month. Her basic pay is 36,000 a year, so 3,000 a month, and she worked ten hours of overtime.

| Line | This month | Year to date |
|---|---:|---:|
| Basic pay | 3,000 | 9,000 |
| Overtime, 10 hours | 200 | 350 |
| **Gross pay** | **3,200** | **9,350** |
| Income tax | -420 | -1,215 |
| Social insurance | -256 | -748 |
| Pension, employee share | -150 | -450 |
| **Total deductions** | **-826** | **-2,413** |
| **Net pay** | **2,374** | **6,937** |
| Pension, employer share (not deducted) | 90 | 270 |

The year-to-date column covers three months. It shows overtime in two of them, 150 last month and 200 this month.

## Payments: what you earned

**Basic pay** is your salary divided by the number of pay periods in a year, or your hourly rate times your hours. Check it against your contract once, and again after every pay rise.

**Overtime, bonuses and allowances** each get their own line. Ana's overtime is 10 hours at 20 an hour. Check the hours against your own record, because overtime is the line most often missed when a timesheet arrives late.

**Gross pay** is the total of all payments. Tax and social insurance are worked out from this figure, not from basic pay alone.

## Deductions: what was taken off

**Income tax** depends on how much you earn and on your tax code or allowance, which tells your employer how much of your pay is tax-free. A wrong code is the most expensive payslip mistake, because it repeats every month. If your code changes without a reason you know, ask your employer or tax office why.

**Social insurance** pays for public pensions, health care or unemployment benefits, depending on the country. It is usually a fixed share of earnings above a threshold. In Ana's example it is 8 in every 100 of gross pay.

**Pension, employee share** is your own contribution to a workplace pension. Ana pays 5 in every 100 of her basic pay, 150 a month. Some schemes take it before tax, which lowers the income tax line. Check that the share matches what you signed up for.

**Pension, employer share** is what your employer adds. It is not deducted from your pay, but many payslips print it so you can see it. Ana's employer adds 90 a month. That is part of what she earns, even though it never reaches her current account.

## Net pay: what reached your account

Net pay is gross pay minus total deductions: 3,200 minus 826 is 2,374. This is the number to budget with. A household that plans with gross pay, or with last month's net pay after overtime, plans with money it does not have.

| Figure | Amount | Use it for |
|---|---:|---|
| Gross pay | 3,200 | Checking tax and pension |
| Net pay with overtime | 2,374 | This month only |
| Net pay without overtime | 2,230 | Planning a normal month |

If overtime comes and goes, plan with net pay without it, and treat overtime as a bonus for saving or yearly bills. Our [comparison of three budgeting methods](/blog/budgeting-methods-compared/) starts every plan from this kind of steady figure.

## Four checks to make every month

:::callout{kind=tip}
Keep each month's payslip in one folder, paper or digital. Comparing this month with last month is how most mistakes are found.
:::

1. **Hours and rate.** Basic pay and overtime match your contract and your own record of hours.
2. **Tax code.** The code or allowance is the same as last month, or changed for a reason you know.
3. **Pension.** Your share and your employer's share are both there, at the agreed rate.
4. **Net pay.** The net pay on the payslip matches what arrived in your account.

If something looks wrong, ask your payroll team in writing and keep a copy of the payslip. Most mistakes are put right in the next pay run once someone points them out.

## What a payslip tells you about your budget

Reading your payslip each month also shows where your budget really starts. In our study of [eleven households that tracked spending for a month](/blog/track-spending-thirty-days/), four planned with a figure that was higher than their real net pay. Once a year, set your payslip beside your budget during [a yearly budget check](/blog/yearly-budget-check/) and update the starting figure.

:::callout{kind=summary}
- **Gross pay** is what you earned; **net pay** is what you can spend.
- **Check four things monthly:** hours, tax code, pension and net pay.
- **Budget with steady net pay,** without overtime that may not repeat.
:::
