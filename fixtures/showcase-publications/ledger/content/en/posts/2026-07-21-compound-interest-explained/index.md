Compound interest is interest paid on interest: each year's interest is added to the pot, so next year's interest is worked out on a larger sum. In our worked example, 1,000 left in a jar at 5 in 100 a year grows to 1,629 after ten years with compound interest, against 1,500 with simple interest. The gap is small at first and grows every year.

This note builds the idea one year at a time, with one imaginary jar and no formulas until the end. The same arithmetic explains why savings grow faster over time, why fees cost more than they seem, and why a debt left alone keeps getting larger.

## One jar, one year

Imagine a jar that pays you 5 in 100 a year on whatever is inside. You put in 1,000 and leave it.

At the end of year one, the jar adds 50, because 5 in 100 of 1,000 is 50. You now have 1,050. So far, simple and compound interest give the same answer.

## The second year is where it changes

With **simple interest**, the jar pays 5 in 100 on your original 1,000 every year. So it adds 50 again, and you have 1,100.

With **compound interest**, the jar pays 5 in 100 on everything inside, including last year's interest. So it adds 52.50, and you have 1,102.50. The extra 2.50 is interest earned on the first year's interest.

That 2.50 looks like nothing. Watch what it becomes.

## Ten years and more, side by side

| Years in the jar | Simple interest | Compound interest | Extra from compounding |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 1,050 | 1,050 | 0 |
| 2 | 1,100 | 1,103 | 3 |
| 3 | 1,150 | 1,158 | 8 |
| 5 | 1,250 | 1,276 | 26 |
| 10 | 1,500 | 1,629 | 129 |
| 20 | 2,000 | 2,653 | 653 |

After ten years, compounding has added 129 more than simple interest. After twenty, it has added 653, and the compound jar holds more than two and a half times what went in. Simple interest grows in a straight line; compound interest grows in a curve that gets steeper.

## Adding a little each month

Most people do not leave one sum alone. They add to it. Here is the same idea with 100 added every month, earning compound interest of 4 in 100 a year, added monthly.

| Years | Paid in | Value of the jar | Interest earned |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 1,200 | 1,222 | 22 |
| 5 | 6,000 | 6,630 | 630 |
| 10 | 12,000 | 14,725 | 2,725 |
| 20 | 24,000 | 36,677 | 12,677 |

In the first year interest adds 22. By year twenty it has added more than half as much again as everything paid in. The early years matter most, because money paid in early has the longest to compound. That is the main reason a [home deposit saved slowly](/blog/saving-home-deposit/) gets more help from interest than one saved fast.

:::callout{kind=note}
**This is not financial advice.** The rates are round examples. Savings accounts pay what they pay, and investments can fall as well as rise.
:::

## The rule of 72

To estimate how long it takes money to double, divide 72 by the yearly rate. At 5 in 100 a year, 72 divided by 5 is about 14.4, so the jar doubles in about fourteen years. At 4 in 100 it takes about 18 years. At 8 in 100, about 9.

| Yearly rate | Rule of 72 estimate | Exact years to double |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 2 in 100 | 36 | 35.0 |
| 4 in 100 | 18 | 17.7 |
| 5 in 100 | 14.4 | 14.2 |
| 8 in 100 | 9 | 9.0 |

The [rule of 72](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rule_of_72) is close enough for planning in your head, and it works the other way too. A debt at 24 in 100 a year doubles in about three years if nothing is paid.

## Compound interest works against you too

The same curve applies to everything worked out as a share of a growing sum.

- **Fees** taken as a share of your savings compound, because each unit taken stops growing. Our study of [investment fees over thirty years](/blog/investment-fees-thirty-years/) shows a small yearly fee taking a third of the growth.
- **Debts** compound when interest is added to an unpaid balance, which is why card balances can last for years. We show this in [why minimum payments take so long](/blog/credit-card-minimum-payments/).
- **Rising prices** compound too, so cash in a jar buys a little less each year. Our study of [inflation and cash savings](/blog/inflation-cash-savings/) puts numbers on it.

## The formula, for those who want it

The value after a number of years is the starting sum times one plus the rate, multiplied by itself once for each year. For 1,000 at 5 in 100 for ten years, that is 1,000 times 1.05 ten times over, which comes to 1,628.89. You can check it on any calculator with a power key.

- **Compound interest** is interest on interest.

- **Early money matters most,** because it compounds for longest.
- **Divide 72 by the rate** to estimate how long money takes to double.
:::
