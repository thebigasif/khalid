Account fees of a few units a month add up to hundreds over ten years, and to more once you count the interest the money could have earned. In our worked example, a monthly fee of 5 costs 600 in ten years, and about 700 when that money would otherwise have sat in savings earning 3 in 100 a year. A household paying two or three such fees can lose the price of a holiday every decade.

This research note looks at the small, regular charges that come with everyday accounts: monthly account fees, fees per transaction, and charges for going overdrawn. None of them looks worth worrying about on its own. Added up over ten years, most of them are.

:::callout{kind=note}
**This is not financial advice.** The fees and rates are made up for the arithmetic. Your own account's fees are in its terms and on your statements.
:::

## The fees in this note

Everyday accounts can carry three kinds of charge.

- **Monthly account fees,** a fixed sum each month, sometimes in return for extras such as insurance.
- **Fees per transaction,** such as a charge for each cash withdrawal from another provider's machine or each payment in another currency.
- **Overdraft charges,** interest or a daily fee when the account goes below zero.

The reference page on [bank charges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bank_charge) lists more, but these three cover most of what households pay.

## Monthly account fees over ten years

The first column is what you pay. The second adds what that money would have earned if you had put it into savings at 3 in 100 a year instead, which is the true cost over time.

| Monthly fee | Paid in 10 years | Including interest it could have earned |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 3 | 360 | 419 |
| 5 | 600 | 699 |
| 8 | 960 | 1,118 |
| 12 | 1,440 | 1,677 |

The interest column grows faster than the fee, for the same reason [investment fees cost more than they seem](/blog/investment-fees-thirty-years/): money taken early is money that never grows. Over twenty or thirty years the gap widens further.

## Is a packaged account worth its fee

Some accounts charge a monthly fee and include extras, such as travel insurance or phone insurance. Whether that is good value depends only on whether you would otherwise pay for those extras.

| Extra included | Would you buy it anyway | Its value to you a year |
|---|---|---:|
| Travel insurance | Yes, two trips a year | 120 |
| Phone insurance | No, phone is old | 0 |
| Breakdown cover | Already have it with the car | 0 |
| **Value to this household** | | **120** |

At 12 a month, the account costs 144 a year. For this made-up household, the extras are worth 120, so the account costs 24 a year more than it gives back. For a household that would not use the travel insurance, the whole 144 is lost each year. Write your own list before deciding; a packaged account is only good value when you would pay for every included extra anyway.

## Fees per transaction

Per-transaction fees are small and easy to forget, which makes them hard to see on a statement.

| Habit | Fee each time | Times a month | Cost in 10 years |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Cash from another provider's machine | 1.50 | 4 | 720 |
| Card payment in another currency | 0.75 | 6 | 540 |
| Paper statement by post | 2 | 1 | 240 |

None of these is large. All three together cost 1,500 in ten years, or 12.50 a month, before counting lost interest.

## Overdraft charges

An overdraft lets the balance go below zero, at a cost. Many accounts charge interest at a high yearly rate; some add a daily or monthly fee. A household that dips below zero by 200 for the last week of every month pays for those seven days twelve times a year.

| Overdraft habit | Cost each month | Cost in 10 years |
|---|---:|---:|
| 200 below zero for 7 days, fee of 1 a day | 7 | 840 |
| 200 below zero for 7 days, interest of 40 in 100 a year | about 1.50 | about 180 |

The second row looks cheap. But a habit of ending each month below zero usually means the budget is short by a little every month. A small buffer of one week's spending ends the habit, and the fee with it. Our [budget check in one evening](/blog/yearly-budget-check/) is a good moment to set that buffer.

## How to find your own account fees

1. Search the last twelve months of statements for the words "fee", "charge" and "interest".
2. Add up each kind for the year.
3. Multiply by ten to see the decade's cost.
4. Ask your provider whether a cheaper account or a change in habit would remove each fee.
