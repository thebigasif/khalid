When eleven households agreed to track spending for thirty days, their real flexible spending came out about 390 a month higher than their own guesses, and the largest misses were small purchases and eating out. Fixed bills were guessed almost exactly. The lesson is simple: people remember bills and forget coffees.

Our call to track spending was a small reader exercise, not a scientific survey. Eleven households answered a call in our spring issue, wrote down their guesses, then recorded every purchase for a month. We share it because the pattern was so consistent, and because you can repeat it at home with a notebook.

::toc

## How the households tracked spending

To track spending the same way, each household did four things.

1. **Guessed first.** Before starting, each household wrote down what it thought it spent in a month on seven flexible categories.
2. **Recorded everything.** For thirty days they wrote down every purchase, however small, on paper or in a phone note, on the day it happened.
3. **Checked against statements.** At the end they matched their notes to bank and card statements, to catch anything missed.
4. **Added up by category.** They sent us their guesses and their totals, with no names, and we averaged them.

Fixed bills such as rent, energy and insurance were left out of the comparison. Every household guessed those within a few units, because the same amount leaves the account every month.

All figures in this post are averages for the eleven households, rounded to the nearest five. Their incomes and sizes differed, so look at the gaps rather than the totals.

## Guesses against real spending

| Category | Average guess | Average tracked | Gap | Households that guessed low |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Food shopping | 450 | 560 | +110 | 9 of 11 |
| Eating out and takeaways | 120 | 210 | +90 | 10 of 11 |
| Small purchases under 10 | 60 | 175 | +115 | 11 of 11 |
| Subscriptions | 35 | 60 | +25 | 8 of 11 |
| Transport top-ups and parking | 180 | 195 | +15 | 6 of 11 |
| Gifts | 40 | 55 | +15 | 5 of 11 |
| Household goods | 70 | 85 | +15 | 7 of 11 |
| **Total** | **955** | **1,340** | **+385** | |

Every household guessed low on small purchases. These were coffees, snacks, a magazine, a phone charger, a parking meter: each one too small to remember, but together worth more than any other gap.

## Three patterns in the results

**Small things add up fastest.** The average household made about 58 purchases under 10 in the month, nearly two a day. Each was easy to forget. Together they came to 175.

**Subscriptions hide.** Eight households had at least one subscription they had forgotten about. Two were paying for the same kind of service twice. Cancelling them saved an average of 20 a month at once.

**Food shopping runs over in small trips.** The weekly shop was close to the guess. The gap came from top-up trips for bread, milk and "something for tonight", which averaged six a month.

:::callout{kind=tip}
If thirty days of notes sounds like too much, try one week. The small purchases pattern usually shows within the first five days.
:::

## What the households changed afterwards

We asked each household what it changed once it had stopped to track spending. Their answers fell into three groups.

| Change | Households | Average saving a month |
|---|---:|---:|
| Cancelled forgotten subscriptions | 8 | 20 |
| Set a weekly limit for small purchases | 6 | 60 |
| Planned top-up shopping into the weekly shop | 5 | 40 |

Four households also found that they had been planning their budget with the wrong starting figure: their pay after overtime, not their steady pay. Our guide to [reading a payslip](/blog/read-payslip/) shows how to find the right one.

## How to track spending yourself

To track spending you need nothing more than a notebook, a pen and thirty days.

1. Write down your guesses for each flexible category before you start. Do not skip this; the gap is the lesson.
2. Record every purchase on the day you make it. Include cash.
3. At the end of each week, match your notes against your statements.
4. After thirty days, add up each category and compare it with your guess.
5. Choose one gap to close. Just one.

Once you know where the money really goes, you can choose a plan that fits. Our [three budgeting methods, compared on one household](/blog/budgeting-methods-compared/), suggests which method fixes which kind of leak. Track spending for thirty days once a year as part of [a yearly budget check in one evening](/blog/yearly-budget-check/).

We checked every average in this post the same way we check our worked examples, as set out in [how we test a study before it runs](/blog/check-every-number/). The gap between what people believe they spend and what they do spend is well known in [behavioural economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Behavioral_economics), and our eleven households fit it closely.
