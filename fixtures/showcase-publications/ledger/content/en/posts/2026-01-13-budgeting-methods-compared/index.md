The three budgeting methods most households try are the 50/30/20 split, the zero-based budget and the envelope method, and on the same month of income each one leaves a different amount saved. In our worked example the zero-based budget saved the most, the envelope method kept spending closest to plan, and the 50/30/20 split took the least time. Which one suits you depends less on the arithmetic than on where your money tends to leak.

We tested all three budgeting methods on one made-up household with round numbers, so you can follow every sum. The household is two adults with one child and a take-home pay of 3,000 a month. We write sums without a currency sign, so they read the same wherever you live.

:::callout{kind=note}
**This is not financial advice.** The household, its income and its bills are made up. The study shows how each method works on paper, not which one you should choose.
:::

## The household in this study

Before comparing budgeting methods, we wrote down what the household actually spent in an ordinary month. These are the fixed and regular costs, rounded to the nearest ten.

| Cost | Per month |
|---|---:|
| Rent | 1,000 |
| Food and household goods | 520 |
| Energy and water | 190 |
| Transport | 210 |
| Phones and internet | 70 |
| Insurance | 60 |
| Childcare | 240 |
| Eating out, outings and hobbies | 330 |
| Clothes and shoes | 90 |
| Subscriptions | 40 |
| **Total spent** | **2,750** |
| **Left over** | **250** |

The household had no plan for the 250 left over. In most months it disappeared into small purchases nobody could name a week later. That gap between what is left on paper and what is left in the account is what a budget is meant to close.

## Method one: the 50/30/20 split

The 50/30/20 split divides take-home pay into three parts: half for needs, three tenths for wants and one fifth for saving or paying off debt. It needs only one calculation a month, which is its main strength.

| Part | Share of pay | Amount | What the household spent |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Needs | 50/100 | 1,500 | 2,290 |
| Wants | 30/100 | 900 | 460 |
| Saving and debt | 20/100 | 600 | 0 |

The split showed the problem at once. Needs took 2,290, well over the 1,500 the rule allows, mostly because rent alone was a third of pay. The household could not cut rent this month, so the rule could not be followed as written. Instead it moved the target: wants were cut to 360 and saving was set at 350. By the end of the month wants had reached 420, and the extra 60 came out of saving, which ended at 290.

That flexibility is fair, but it shows the method's weak point. When needs are high, the 50/30/20 split gives a target and no route to it.

## Method two: the zero-based budget

A [zero-based budget](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero-based_budgeting) gives every unit of income a job before the month starts, until income minus the plan equals zero. Saving is a line like rent, not whatever is left.

| Line | Planned | Spent | Difference |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Rent | 1,000 | 1,000 | 0 |
| Food and household goods | 480 | 495 | -15 |
| Energy and water | 190 | 190 | 0 |
| Transport | 200 | 185 | 15 |
| Phones and internet | 70 | 70 | 0 |
| Insurance | 60 | 60 | 0 |
| Childcare | 240 | 240 | 0 |
| Eating out, outings and hobbies | 250 | 270 | -20 |
| Clothes and shoes | 60 | 40 | 20 |
| Subscriptions | 25 | 25 | 0 |
| Yearly bills fund | 75 | 75 | 0 |
| Emergency fund | 350 | 350 | 0 |
| **Total** | **3,000** | **3,000** | **0** |

The household saved 350 into its emergency fund and 75 towards yearly bills, so 425 in all. The small overspends on food and outings were covered by underspends elsewhere, because the plan was checked once a week.

The cost is time. Writing the plan took about forty minutes the first month and fifteen after that. The "yearly bills fund" line comes from our guide to [sinking funds for once-a-year bills](/blog/sinking-funds-yearly-bills/), and the emergency fund line from [how big an emergency fund should be](/blog/emergency-fund-size/).

## Method three: the envelope method

The envelope method puts a fixed sum for each spending category into its own envelope at the start of the month. When an envelope is empty, spending in that category stops. Today most households use separate accounts or labelled pots instead of paper envelopes, but the rule is the same.

:::figure{src="envelopes.jpg" alt="A neat stack of plain white window envelopes on a dark grey table, the kind of envelopes the envelope budgeting method is named after"}
The envelope method works with paper envelopes or with separate pots in a bank account. The rule is the same: when one is empty, that spending stops.
:::

The household used envelopes only for the flexible costs, where money leaks. Fixed bills stayed on direct debit.

| Envelope | Filled with | Left at month end |
|---|---:|---:|
| Food and household goods | 480 | 12 |
| Eating out, outings and hobbies | 250 | 0 |
| Clothes and shoes | 60 | 35 |
| Transport top-ups | 60 | 18 |

The envelope method kept flexible spending closest to plan. Eating out stopped exactly at 250, and the household felt the stop was easier to accept from an empty envelope than from a spreadsheet. It saved 350, plus the 65 left in envelopes, which it moved to saving.

## The budgeting methods side by side

| | 50/30/20 split | Zero-based budget | Envelope method |
|---|---|---|---|
| Saved this month | 290 | 425 | 415 |
| Time each month | 5 minutes | 15 to 40 minutes | 20 minutes |
| Overspend on flexible costs | 60 | 35 | 0 |
| Works when needs are high | Poorly | Well | Well |
| Hardest part | Needs over the limit | Planning every line | Refilling envelopes |

The differences between the budgeting methods look small in one month. Over a year they are not: the 135 a month between the zero-based budget and the 50/30/20 split adds up to 1,620 a year.

## How to choose between budgeting methods

Of the three budgeting methods, pick the one that fixes your own leak.

- **If you overspend on small flexible things,** try the envelope method. A hard stop works better than a reminder.
- **If you never know where the money went,** try the zero-based budget. Planning every line makes the month visible.
- **If you have little time and your needs fit in half your pay,** the 50/30/20 split is enough.

Whichever of the budgeting methods you choose, start from your real spending, not a guess. Our study of [households that tracked their spending for thirty days](/blog/track-spending-thirty-days/) found that most people guess low on food and outings. And before you set any target, check what actually reaches your account each month: [reading your payslip line by line](/blog/read-payslip/) shows where gross pay goes.

- **50/30/20** is fast but gives no route when needs are high.
- **Zero-based** saved the most in our example, at the cost of planning time.
- **Envelopes** kept flexible spending exactly on plan.
- **Choose the method that fixes your leak,** and start from real numbers.
