The interest rate on a loan tells you what you pay for borrowing the money, while the APR, the annual percentage rate, tells you the yearly cost of the whole loan including most fees. When two loans have fees, compare that yearly cost, not the interest rate. In our worked example, a loan at 7 in 100 interest has an APR of 13.5 because of its fee, while a loan at 9 in 100 with no fee comes to 9.4, and the second loan is cheaper.

Most people look at the interest rate first, because it is printed largest. This note works through three made-up loans to show what each number includes, where it can still mislead you, and the one figure that settles any comparison.

::toc

## What the interest rate includes

The interest rate is the price of borrowing the money itself, as a share of what you owe, per year. It does not include arrangement fees, account fees or anything else you pay to get the loan. On a loan with no fees, the two numbers are close. On a loan with fees, they can be far apart.

## What the APR adds

It spreads the loan's required fees across its term and turns everything into one yearly rate. It also allows for how often interest is charged. A rate of 9 in 100 charged monthly costs a little more than 9 in 100 over a full year, because each month's interest is added before the next is worked out. That is why a loan at 9 in 100 with no fees still shows a yearly cost of 9.4.

The rules for working it out differ between countries, and some leave out certain fees. The [general idea of the annual percentage rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annual_percentage_rate) is the same everywhere: one number that puts loans with different fees on a level footing.

## Three loans of 5,000 over three years

| | Loan A | Loan B | Loan C |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Interest rate a year | 8 in 100 | 9 in 100 | 7 in 100 |
| Arrangement fee | 250 | 0 | 400 |
| Monthly payment | 156.68 | 159.00 | 154.39 |
| Interest over 3 years | 641 | 724 | 558 |
| Total cost of borrowing | 891 | 724 | 958 |
| APR | 12.1 | 9.4 | 13.5 |

Loan C has the lowest interest rate and the lowest monthly payment. It is also the most expensive, because its fee is large. Loan B has the highest interest rate and no fee, and it costs the least. The yearly cost puts them in the right order; the interest rate puts them in the wrong one.

## Where the APR can mislead

It is a good guide when the loans you compare have the same amount and the same term. When the term changes, it can point the wrong way, because a fixed fee spread over fewer months looks larger as a yearly rate.

| Loan A over | Monthly payment | Total cost of borrowing | APR |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 year | 434.94 | 469 | 19.3 |
| 3 years | 156.68 | 891 | 12.1 |
| 5 years | 101.38 | 1,333 | 10.7 |

Over one year the rate looks high, at 19.3, yet the total cost is the lowest by far. Over five years it looks low, at 10.7, yet the total cost is the highest. A borrower who picked the lowest APR here would pay the most.

:::callout{kind=warning}
Compare APRs only between loans of the same amount and the same term. When the terms differ, compare the total amount repayable instead.
:::

## The one figure that settles it

The total amount repayable is the sum of every payment you will make, including fees. For loans of the same amount, the one with the lowest total costs you least. Lenders must usually show this figure before you sign. If they do not, ask for it.

Use the three numbers together:

- **Total amount repayable** to find the cheapest loan.
- **Monthly payment** to check you can afford it every month.
- **APR** to compare loans of the same amount and term quickly.

## APR on credit cards

Credit cards also show an APR, but most cards charge no arrangement fee, so it mostly reflects the interest rate charged monthly. A card charging 1.5 in 100 a month costs about 19.6 a year, not 18, because of monthly compounding. The cost of carrying a card balance for years is worked out in [why card minimum payments take so long](/blog/credit-card-minimum-payments/).

When you are weighing a loan to replace cards, all three numbers matter. Our worked example of [a consolidation loan against two cards](/blog/debt-consolidation-loan/) shows a lower monthly payment that saved almost nothing. And if you are deciding which debt to clear first, the yearly rate is the number the debt avalanche sorts by, as our [avalanche and snowball study](/blog/debt-avalanche-snowball/) explains.
