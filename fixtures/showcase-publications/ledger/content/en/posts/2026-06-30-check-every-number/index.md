We check every number in a study twice, by two people working apart, before an issue of The Ledger Review goes out. If their answers differ by even one unit, the table does not run until we know why. This note sets out the whole process, so you know what stands behind each table and how to tell us when we get one wrong.

Personal finance writing is full of numbers that cannot be traced. A rate appears with no source, a total that does not add up, an example that only works with one hidden assumption. We would rather publish fewer studies than publish one a reader cannot rebuild.

## Why we use made-up round numbers

Every worked example in the Review uses a made-up household and round numbers. We do this on purpose, for three reasons.

1. **You can follow the sums.** 3,000 a month is easier to check in your head than 2,847, and you can check every number by hand.
2. **Nobody's private finances are shown.** Our households are invented, even when their situations are common.
3. **The method matters more than the figure.** A round example shows how a sum works, and you then run it with your own numbers.

We also write sums without a currency sign, so a reader anywhere can follow them. Where a real rule or rate matters, we say that it differs by country and point you to where to check it.

## How we check every number in a table

| Check | Who does it | What it catches |
|---|---|---|
| 1. The author builds the table | Author | The first version |
| 2. A second editor rebuilds it from the stated assumptions alone | Second editor | Hidden assumptions and slips |
| 3. Every total and row is added again | Second editor | Rows that do not sum to the total |
| 4. Every figure in the text is matched to the table | Copy editor | A number that changed in the table but not in the text |
| 5. One sum is changed to see if the result moves as it should | Data editor | Errors in the method, not just the arithmetic |

The second step is the one that matters most. The second editor gets only the assumptions written in the post, never the author's spreadsheet. If they cannot rebuild the table from the post alone, the post does not say enough, and we add what is missing.

The fifth step is a sense check. If we raise an interest rate, the cost should go up. If we save more each month, the wait should get shorter. When a result moves the wrong way, something in the method is broken, even if the arithmetic is right.

## How we round

We round only at the end, using ordinary [rounding to the nearest unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rounding). Every sum is worked to the cent, and the result is rounded for the table. That is why some rows in our tables do not add up exactly to the total shown: each row is rounded on its own. When that happens, we say so under the table, so a reader who wants to check every number again knows why.

Rates are written as "in 100" in tables, such as 5 in 100 a year, so that the unit is never in doubt. Long-run studies, such as [what investment fees cost over thirty years](/blog/investment-fees-thirty-years/), state their assumed growth rate before the first table and say that it is not a forecast.

## What we never do

:::callout{kind=warning}
We never recommend a product, a provider or an investment, and we never take payment to mention one. Our studies explain how the arithmetic works. They are not financial advice, and we say so in each one where it matters.
:::

- **No real banks or brands.** If we need a product in an example, it is a type, such as "a credit card", never a name.
- **No predictions.** We do not say where rates, prices or markets will go.
- **No numbers we cannot rebuild.** We check every number in a table against the assumptions stated in the same post.

## When we get it wrong

We check every number we publish, and we will still make mistakes. When we do, we correct the post, add the date of the correction under the title, and say in one line what changed. We do not quietly edit a number.

In the last year we corrected two studies. One rounded a row before adding it, which moved a total by one unit. The other used last year's minimum payment rule for one row of a table. Both were found by readers, and both readers are thanked in the corrected posts. Our study of [why card minimum payments take years](/blog/credit-card-minimum-payments/) was rebuilt after the second.

If you find a number you cannot rebuild, write to editors@example.com with the post and the table. A data editor will work it through again and answer within a week.

:::callout{kind=summary}
- **Two people** check every number, working apart, before a table runs.
- **Made-up round numbers** keep sums easy to follow and private lives private.
- **We round only at the end,** and say so when rows do not add up exactly.
- **Corrections are dated** and explained, never hidden.
:::
