The Ledger Review is a small journal of research on household money. Every three weeks we publish one issue: a long study, worked through with round numbers, on a question ordinary households ask. How big should an emergency fund be? What does a yearly fee cost over thirty years? Which debt should go first?

We started in January 2024 because most money writing skips the arithmetic, and the arithmetic is the part a household can check for itself. We are now in our third volume.

## Who writes here

- **Miriam Calloway** edits every issue and writes the budgeting studies. She spent twelve years analysing household spending surveys.
- **Daniel Ferris** writes about debt. He was a debt adviser for nine years.
- **Anjali Desai** writes about saving and buying a home. She is a qualified accountant who ran payroll for small employers.
- **Lars Penrose** is our data editor. He builds and checks every table and writes the Research notes.

## How an issue is made

Each issue has four sections: Budgeting, Debt, Saving and Research notes. Not every issue has all four. Each study answers its question in the first two sentences, then shows the working.

Good places to start are [three budgeting methods compared on one month](/blog/budgeting-methods-compared/), [compound interest explained with one jar](/blog/compound-interest-explained/) and [the debt avalanche and snowball on three debts](/blog/debt-avalanche-snowball/).

## What we promise

- **Made-up households and round numbers.** Every worked example uses invented people and round sums, so you can follow them and nobody's private finances are shown.
- **No currency sign.** Our sums read the same wherever you live.
- **No products and no paid mentions.** We never name a bank, a provider or an investment, and nobody pays to appear.
- **No personal advice.** We explain how the arithmetic works. We are not your adviser, and our studies are not financial advice.

## How we check what we publish

Every table is worked out twice, by two people working apart, before it runs. We round only at the end. When we get something wrong, we correct it, date the correction and say what changed. The whole method is set out in [how we check every number](/blog/check-every-number/).
