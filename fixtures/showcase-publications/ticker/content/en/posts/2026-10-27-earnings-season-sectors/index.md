Twice a year, Varnholm's listed firms report how much they sold and earned. We are halfway through the autumn 2026 earnings season: 25 of the 50 firms in the Varnholm 50 have reported. Most did better than analysts expected. Wikipedia's page on [earnings season](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earnings_season) describes how it works in real markets.

## How to read the table

Each row is a sector. "Beat forecast" counts the firms whose profit came in above what analysts expected. "Sales growth" compares sales in the summer of 2026 with the summer of 2025.

| Sector | Firms reported | Beat forecast | Sales growth | Profit growth |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Machine tools | 4 | 4 | 9.1% | 14.2% |
| Car parts | 3 | 2 | 6.4% | 8.8% |
| Banks | 6 | 4 | 3.2% | 5.1% |
| Food makers | 5 | 3 | 2.7% | 1.9% |
| Utilities | 4 | 1 | 1.1% | -2.4% |
| Shipping | 3 | 3 | 7.8% | 21.5% |

## What stands out this earnings season

Shipping firms had the strongest earnings season of any sector, with profit up 21.5% on 2025. Busy ports and full ships raised sales, and [shipping costs that have only now started to ease](/blog/shipping-costs-ease/) kept prices high through the summer.

Utilities were the weak spot. Three of the four missed their forecasts, because a mild summer in 2026 meant people used less power for cooling.

Machine tool makers beat every forecast. Their order books grew all year, which matches the rise in [factory orders](/blog/factory-orders-lift-shares/).

## What a forecast is worth

A forecast is an analyst's best guess, and firms often guide analysts towards numbers they expect to beat. So "beat forecast" says more when a whole sector misses than when it beats. Our table of [the Varnholm 50 over ten years](/blog/varnholm-50-ten-years/) shows how much results have swung before.
