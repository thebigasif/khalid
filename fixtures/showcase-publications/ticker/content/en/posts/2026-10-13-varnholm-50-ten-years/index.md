The Varnholm 50 is the index of Varnholm's fifty largest listed firms. Readers often ask how it has done over the long run. This table sets out ten years of the Varnholm 50, from 2016 to 2025, one row per year.

## The Varnholm 50 year by year

"Return" includes dividends, the share of profits firms pay out to their owners. "Worst month" is the largest fall in any one month of that year.

| Year | Return | Best month | Worst month | Level at year end |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2016 | 6.2% | 3.1% | -2.8% | 1,412 |
| 2017 | 11.4% | 4.0% | -1.9% | 1,532 |
| 2018 | -4.7% | 2.6% | -6.3% | 1,417 |
| 2019 | 14.9% | 5.2% | -2.4% | 1,583 |
| 2020 | -8.1% | 6.8% | -13.5% | 1,412 |
| 2021 | 18.3% | 5.5% | -3.0% | 1,617 |
| 2022 | -2.2% | 3.9% | -5.6% | 1,531 |
| 2023 | 7.6% | 3.3% | -2.1% | 1,595 |
| 2024 | 9.8% | 4.4% | -3.7% | 1,693 |
| 2025 | 5.1% | 2.9% | -4.2% | 1,720 |

## What ten years show

Seven of the ten years ended higher. The three falls, in 2018, 2020 and 2022, were each followed by a rise the next year, but that is a pattern in made-up numbers, not a rule. In 2020 the index fell 13.5% in one month and still ended the year only 8.1% lower.

## Why the level and the return differ

The level counts only prices. The return adds the dividends paid during the year. That is why the level at the end of 2025 is about 22% above 2016, while the returns over the same ten years add up to far more. Wikipedia's page on [dividends](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dividend) explains how firms pay them.

## How to use a table like this

A long table shows how often an index rises and falls, and how far. It cannot say what the next year will bring. Read it for the size of the swings, not for a forecast: in these ten made-up years, a fall of 5% in one month happened in four of them.
