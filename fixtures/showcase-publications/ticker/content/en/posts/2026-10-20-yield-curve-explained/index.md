When a government borrows, it sells bonds: promises to pay back a sum on a set date, with interest each year. The interest a bond pays, measured against its price, is its yield. A yield curve lines up the yields of bonds that run for different lengths of time, from a few months to thirty years.

## A made-up yield curve

Here are the yields on Varnholm's government bonds on 19 October 2026.

| Length of the bond | Yield |
|---|---|
| 3 months | 3.20% |
| 1 year | 3.10% |
| 2 years | 2.95% |
| 5 years | 2.85% |
| 10 years | 3.05% |
| 30 years | 3.40% |

## Reading the shape of a yield curve

Normally, lending for longer pays more, so the yield curve slopes up. In this example from October 2026 it dips first and then rises. The short end follows the central bank's rate, which is high. The dip says markets expect that rate to fall over the next few years, as they began to after [the recent rate pause](/blog/varn-slips-after-rate-pause/).

## Three shapes to know

- **Upward:** longer loans pay more. Markets expect steady growth.
- **Flat:** every length pays about the same. Markets are unsure.
- **Inverted:** short loans pay more than long ones. Markets expect rates to fall, often because they expect slower growth.

Wikipedia's page on the [yield curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yield_curve) shows real examples of each shape.

## Why the yield curve matters to everyone else

Banks borrow short and lend long. When the yield curve is flat or inverted, lending earns them less, and they may lend less. That is one reason the shape of the curve is watched far outside the bond market, by firms planning to borrow and by people with a mortgage.

## How to follow it

You need only two numbers to follow the curve: the yield on a short bond and the yield on a long one. If the gap between them narrows, the curve is flattening. We give both in the weekly issues when they move.
