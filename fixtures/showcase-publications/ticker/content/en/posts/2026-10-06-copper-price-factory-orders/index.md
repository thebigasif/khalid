Copper is in wires, motors, pipes and almost every machine. When factories get more orders, they buy more copper, and the copper price tends to rise. Traders sometimes call copper the metal that reads the economy. Wikipedia's page on [copper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper) lists its many uses.

## The copper price beside factory orders

This table compares each quarter's change in Varnholm's factory orders with the change in the copper price, from 2025 to mid 2026.

| Quarter | Factory orders | Copper price |
|---|---|---|
| Q1 2025 | +1.2% | +2.4% |
| Q2 2025 | -0.8% | -3.1% |
| Q3 2025 | +0.4% | +0.9% |
| Q4 2025 | +2.1% | +4.6% |
| Q1 2026 | -1.5% | -2.2% |
| Q2 2026 | +1.8% | +3.7% |

In each of these six quarters of 2025 and 2026, the copper price moved the same way as orders, and further.

## Why the copper price moves further

Mines cannot dig more copper quickly. When demand rises, supply stays about the same for months, so the price has to do the work. The same is true when demand falls: mines keep digging, and the price drops until buyers return.

## When the copper price breaks away

Supply news can swamp demand. When a large mine closes, copper rises even if orders fall. When one reopens early, as the Eastreach mine did in [the week of the copper slide](/blog/copper-slide-quiet-week/), copper falls even while factories are busy.

Shipping matters too. [Shipping costs that keep falling](/blog/shipping-costs-ease/) lower the price of delivered copper, which can lift demand on its own.

## What to take from it

The copper price is a useful sign of what factories expect, but not a forecast. Read it beside the orders figures, not instead of them. The weekly issues give both, as in the issue on [factory orders lifting shares](/blog/factory-orders-lift-shares/).

## A note on units

All the changes here are in per cent, so you can compare them directly. A change in the copper price in varns per tonne would mean little without the starting price.
