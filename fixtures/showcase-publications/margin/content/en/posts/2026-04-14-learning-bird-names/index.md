For most of my life a bird on the shore was a gull, a duck or a big grey one. Then, the spring I turned forty, I decided to start learning the names of birds on my morning walk. Not all birds. Just the ones I see on that one stretch of coast.

## How I started learning the names of birds

I gave myself one rule: one new bird a week, and only a bird I had seen myself. I carried a small field guide and wrote the date and place beside each picture when I was sure.[^1]

The first was easy. The big grey one is a heron, and once I knew the name I saw herons everywhere: on the burn, by the harbour wall, standing in a field like a folded umbrella. The name did not change the bird. It changed how often I noticed it.

By the end of the year I had thirty-one birds. Some were common, like the oystercatcher, loud and orange-billed, which I had walked past a thousand times. Some were a surprise, like the purple sandpiper, which spends the winter on our rocks and goes north to breed.

## Why names matter

A name is a hook. Without it, the bird slides out of memory as soon as it flies. With it, I can ask questions. Where does it go in summer? Why is it always alone? Why does it stand on one leg?

Learning the names of birds also slowed my walk down, which is the reason I walk at all. A walk where you stop to look is a different walk from one where you only move.[^2]

## Where to begin

You need a small guide for your own country, a pair of cheap binoculars and the patience to be wrong. Start with the birds you see every day. The rare ones will find you later. The [RSPB](https://www.rspb.org.uk/) has a free bird identifier that helped me check the hard ones.
