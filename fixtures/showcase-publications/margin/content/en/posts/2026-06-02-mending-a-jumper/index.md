I have a blue jumper I have worn for fifteen years. It has been on every walk in this blog. This spring both elbows went through, and instead of buying another I spent three evenings mending a jumper for the first time in my life.

## Mending a jumper, the first try

I had no matching wool. The jumper is a faded navy, and the only wool in the house was a bright cornflower blue left from my mother's knitting basket. I decided to use it and stop pretending the repair could hide.

The stitch is called darning. You weave new yarn across the hole one way, then weave it again the other way, over and under, like a tiny loom.[^1] My first patch was too tight and puckered the sleeve. I unpicked it and started again, looser.

The second patch was better. The third, on the other elbow, was almost good. It took about two hours an elbow, with the radio on.

## Why a visible repair is worth it

Now the jumper has two bright patches, and people ask about them. That surprised me. Nobody ever asked about the jumper when it was whole.

A visible repair tells the story of the thing. It says somebody wore this and cared enough to keep it going. There is a Japanese tradition of mending with visible stitches, and a whole movement of people who darn on purpose in bright colours.[^2] I did not know any of that when I started. I just had the wrong wool.

## What it taught me about the essays

Mending a jumper is slow, repetitive work, and you cannot rush it. A darn pulled tight to save time puckers. So does a paragraph. Some of them began as [letters I never sent](/blog/letters-i-never-sent/). I have started rereading old essays the way I looked at that sleeve, looking for the thin places before they go through.

My father's generation mended everything. Owen Rees writes about that in his guest essay, [my father's allotment](/blog/my-fathers-allotment/). The [Repair Café](https://www.repaircafe.org/en/) network runs free sessions if you want someone to show you how.

[^1]: A darning mushroom helps. A wooden spoon held inside the sleeve works almost as well.
[^2]: The word *sashiko* comes up a lot. I have read about it but never tried it, so I will not pretend to explain it.
