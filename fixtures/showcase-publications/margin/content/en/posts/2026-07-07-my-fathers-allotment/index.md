My father's allotment is plot 14 on a strip of land between the railway and the river. He kept it for thirty years. When his knees went, two summers ago, he gave me the key to the shed and a notebook, and said the rest would be obvious. It was not.

## What was in the notebook

The notebook was thirty years of the same page. Each spring he wrote the date he planted each row, and each autumn what came up and what did not. No feelings. Just dates, rows and a tick or a cross.[^1]

At first it read like a list. By the third evening it read like a diary. The years with a cross by every row were the years my mother was ill. The year with the most ticks was the year I was born.

## The first year on my father's allotment

I did everything wrong in my first year on my father's allotment. I planted too early, because a warm week in April fooled me. I watered at noon, which my neighbour on plot 15 told me cooks the leaves. I put the tomatoes in the wrong bed, the one that floods.

The neighbours saved me. Allotments run on advice, given freely and often without asking.[^2] Plot 15 lent me canes. Plot 9 gave me seedlings. Someone I never met left a bag of horse manure by the shed with no note.

## The tomatoes

By August the tomatoes were the best thing on my father's allotment. Green for weeks, then red all at once. I took a photo and sent it to my father, who wrote back one word: *late*. He was right. His came a fortnight earlier every year in the notebook.

This year I started my own page in his notebook. Same columns. Dates, rows, ticks and crosses. I have added one column of my own, for the weather, because Nell's essay on [seven wet days in September](/blog/a-week-of-rain/) made me notice how much it decides. Her essay on [mending a jumper](/blog/mending-a-jumper/) is about the same kind of work. If you want a plot of your own, the [National Allotment Society](https://www.nsalg.org.uk/) explains how to find one.

*Nell asked me to write about this after I sent her a photo of the tomatoes. It is my first essay for anyone but a local paper.*

[^1]: He used a pencil, never a pen. A pen runs in the rain, he said, and allotments are mostly rain.
[^2]: The advice does not always agree. Plot 15 and plot 9 have argued about onions for a decade.
