In the left drawer of the kitchen table there are forty-three letters I never sent. Some are to people who have died. Some are to people I see every week. One is to a teacher I had at nine, who may or may not still be alive.

## Writing letters by hand

Each one is written by hand, on paper, with an address at the top as if they were going. The address matters. It makes the letter real enough that I write it properly, and not as a list of complaints.[^1]

A letter to one person is the clearest writing I do. An essay for many readers hedges. A letter to my sister says the thing. Many of these letters later became essays, with the names taken out.

## Letters I never sent, and the reasons

Some stay unsent because the person cannot read them. Others stay because the letter did its work in the writing. I wanted to say thank you, or sorry, and by the end of the page I had said it, and the saying was enough.

A few I have not sent out of cowardice. I know which ones. They sit at the front of the drawer.[^2]

## What the drawer taught me

Writing letters I never sent taught me to write to one reader. Now, before I start an essay, I pick a real person and write the first draft as a letter to them. The final version loses the *Dear* and the address. It keeps the voice.

If you want to try this, start with a thank-you letter to someone who will never know you wrote it. Write it on paper. Put it in a drawer. See how you feel a week later.
