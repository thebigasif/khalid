I have been walking the same path most mornings for six years. It runs from my back gate, along the top of the dunes, to a bench by the old lifeboat slip and back. It takes forty minutes if I do not stop, and I always stop.

People ask if the same path gets boring. It is the opposite. A new route asks you to watch your feet and read the signs. A route you know lets you look up.

## What changes on the same path

The path itself hardly changes. Everything around it does. The ferns are brown stubs in March and over my head by July.[^1] The sand moves with every storm, so the steps down to the beach are three steps one week and seven the next. In autumn the same twenty yards smell of salt, then of rotting kelp, then of somebody's wood smoke.

You only notice these things when the path is the same. On a new walk, everything is new, so nothing stands out. On walking the same path every day, the small change is the news.

## What it does for the writing

Most of my essays start on that path. Not because the path is interesting, but because my mind has nothing to do while my feet do the work. The walk becomes a kind of slow desk. I carry a small notebook and write down one line before I turn back at the bench.[^2]

The rule I keep is simple: no headphones, no phone, and the same route. If I change the route, I start planning it, and the planning crowds out the thinking.

## Choosing a route to walk every day

If you want to try one same path yourself, pick something short that starts at your door. Twenty minutes is plenty. Walk it every day for a month before you decide anything about it. Write down one thing that changed each time, even if it is only the light.

The tide sets the times of my walk now, which I wrote about in [what tide tables teach](/blog/what-tide-tables-teach/). The birds on the shore became their own project, in [learning the names of birds](/blog/learning-bird-names/), and on wet days the walk changes, as in [the week the rain kept me in](/blog/a-week-of-rain/). For the whole habit of walking as a writer, Rebecca Solnit's *Wanderlust* is the book I give to friends, and the [Ramblers](https://www.ramblers.org.uk/) list public paths across Britain if you need a start.

[^1]: Bracken, strictly. My neighbour corrects me every summer, and every summer I forget.
[^2]: A pocket notebook with squared paper. Lined paper feels like a promise to write something proper.
