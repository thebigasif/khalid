The last holiday cottage on our row shuts at the end of October. The next morning the beach is empty, the car park is empty, and the sea in winter begins. I wait for it all year.

## The light on the sea in winter

Summer light on this coast is flat and bright, and it bleaches the colour out of everything. Winter light comes in low from the south and lights the sea from the side. The water turns the colour of slate, then pewter, then for ten minutes at three in the afternoon, something close to gold.[^1]

The days are short, so the walk moves. In December I walk at noon, the only time the sun clears the dunes.

## The birds that arrive

The sea in winter has more birds, not fewer. The summer terns leave, and in their place come birds from the north: purple sandpipers on the rocks, eider ducks rafting off the point, and sometimes a long-tailed duck, which I have seen twice in six winters.[^2]

I learned their names one at a time, a story told in [learning the names of birds](/blog/learning-bird-names/). In winter the names pay off. A shore that looked empty to me ten years ago is busy now.

## The quiet

What I wait for most is the quiet. Not silence, since the sea is loud in winter. But the quiet of a place with nobody looking at it except you. I write more in winter than in any other season. The essays are slower and, I think, better.

If you visit a coast in winter, dress for twice the cold you expect, check the tide before you go down to the sand, and leave the seals alone. My notes on reading the tide are in [what tide tables teach](/blog/what-tide-tables-teach/), and on the wet weeks before winter in [the long autumn rain](/blog/a-week-of-rain/), and the [National Trust](https://www.nationaltrust.org.uk/) looks after much of the coast near me.

[^1]: I have tried to photograph it many times. The photos always look like a dull grey day.
[^2]: Both times I was without binoculars. I have decided this is the rule.
