Kingfishers are back on the lower Alder. In this summer's survey, our volunteers found six pairs nesting between Ashby and the estuary. In 2020 there was one.

## How we count the birds

These small birds nest in tunnels they dig in steep, bare banks of earth. From April to July, twenty volunteers walked the lower river once a fortnight. They noted each bird they saw, each tunnel and each time a bird carried a fish into a bank, which means there are chicks inside.

| Year | Pairs nesting | Volunteers |
|---|---|---|
| 2020 | 1 | 6 |
| 2022 | 2 | 11 |
| 2024 | 4 | 16 |
| 2026 | 6 | 20 |

## Why kingfishers came back

The [common kingfisher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_kingfisher) needs clear water to spot fish from its perch. The lower Alder was cloudy with soil washed off fields. Since 2020, farmers have fenced cattle out of nine kilometres of river, and the banks have grown over. The water is clearer, and the fish are easier to see.

The birds also need small fish. The fish surveys that come with our [otter crossings appeal](/blog/otter-crossings/) show twice as many minnows and young trout as six years ago. Our volunteers who test the water for [Clean Water Watch](/blog/clean-water-watch/) see the same change in their results.

## What the birds tell us

A bird at the top of the food chain is a good sign of a healthy river. If the water were dirty, there would be fewer insects, fewer small fish and so fewer birds. Six nesting pairs tell us that the work of the last six years is paying off, all the way down the chain.

The salmon that came back to Brackenford this autumn tell the same story higher up the river, as Tomasz writes in his [note on the Brackenford salmon](/blog/brackenford-salmon/).

## How you can help

Keep dogs away from steep banks in spring, when birds are nesting. If you see one of these birds, tell us where and when on the trust's sightings form. Every record helps us see which stretches need work next.
