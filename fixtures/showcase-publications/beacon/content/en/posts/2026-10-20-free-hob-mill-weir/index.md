Hob Mill weir is the biggest job the trust has ever taken on. It is a wall of stone and concrete, two metres high, built in 1846 to turn a mill wheel that stopped a century ago. Today it does only one thing: it stops salmon, sea trout and eels from reaching 14 kilometres of the upper Alder.

## Why Hob Mill weir has to go

Fish swim upstream to breed in clean gravel. The best gravel on the Alder lies above the mill, and almost no fish reach it. In our survey of summer 2026 we counted 212 young trout per hundred metres below the wall and 9 above it. The water above the wall is also slower and warmer, so it holds less oxygen in summer.

A fish pass would help some fish over the wall, but it costs nearly as much, needs clearing after every flood and still leaves the river slow. Taking the wall out lets the whole river run as it should. Across Europe, thousands of small weirs like this one have come out, and [dam removal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dam_removal) has a long record of bringing fish back within a few years.

## What the money pays for

| Work | Cost |
|---|---|
| Survey of the riverbed and the old mill walls | £8,500 |
| Taking out the wall and carting away the concrete | £46,000 |
| Shoring up the banks with logs and willow | £21,500 |
| Five years of fish surveys after the work | £14,000 |
| Total | £90,000 |

The landowner has agreed, and the county council gave permission for the work in September 2026. Our report on [where every pound went](/blog/where-every-pound-went/) shows how we spent last year's gifts on work like this.

## When the work happens

If we reach the goal by 31 March 2027, the diggers go in during August, when the river is at its lowest and no fish are spawning. The work takes about three weeks. Our [ten thousand trees appeal](/blog/ten-thousand-trees/) has already planted the banks just below the mill, so the new channel will have shade from the first summer.

The river is ready for it. This October, rangers filmed [salmon at Brackenford](/blog/brackenford-salmon/), six kilometres downstream, for the first time in sixty years. Those fish have now come as far up the Alder as they can.
