Otter crossings are ledges and dry pipes that let an otter pass under a road bridge without leaving the river. Otters are back on the Alder after forty years away, but seven died on the valley's roads in 2025. Most of them were hit at the same four bridges.

## Why otters cross the road

When the river is high, the water under a bridge runs too fast and too deep. An otter will not swim through it. It climbs out, crosses the road and slips back in on the other side. At night, on a fast road, that is often the last thing it does.

The [Eurasian otter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurasian_otter) travels many kilometres of river each night to find food, so one dangerous bridge can affect every otter in the valley.

## What otter crossings look like

At each bridge we fix a wooden or concrete ledge along one wall, above the highest flood. Where a bridge is too low for a ledge, we lay a dry pipe beside it. Low fences guide the otters to the crossing. The work at each bridge takes two days and does not close the road.

## How we will know it works

We will put a trail camera at each crossing for a year. Ledges like these work well on other rivers, and we expect to see otters on the cameras within weeks. Our [kingfisher survey](/blog/kingfishers-lower-alder/) shows how fast wildlife comes back once a river is safe.

The crossing at Fernley Road will also serve the new [Fernley Moor wetland](/blog/fernley-moor-wetland/), where we expect otters to hunt frogs and eels once the pools fill.

## Who helps us build them

The county council's road team has agreed to close one lane while we work, and two local builders have offered their time at cost. Volunteers will put up the guide fences and check the cameras each week.

At 2026 prices, £50 buys a metre of ledge and £250 pays for a trail camera for a year. If we reach £22,000 by the end of April, all four crossings will be in before the winter floods.
