We test a recipe three times before it goes in a box: once by me in our kitchen, once by me in an ordinary home kitchen with only the box's contents, and once by a beginner who has never seen it. Each test checks something different: whether it tastes right, whether the box holds everything it needs, and whether the card makes sense to someone new. A recipe that fails any test goes back to the start.

People sometimes ask why we bother. A recipe that works in a restaurant kitchen can fail at home for a dozen small reasons: a weaker hob, a smaller pan, a vague step. Here is how we test a recipe so it works on a Tuesday night in your kitchen.

## Test one: does it taste right?

The first cook happens in our development kitchen, with every tool and ingredient to hand, laid out as a [mise en place](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mise_en_place). I cook the dish four or five times over a week, changing one thing each time: more acid, less salt, a different cut. I write down every change.

At this stage I am only asking one question. Is this a dinner someone would want to cook again? If the answer is "it is fine", the recipe stops here. Fine is not enough for a box.

## Test two: does the box hold everything?

The second cook happens in a small home kitchen with a four-ring hob, one large frying pan, one saucepan and one tray. I cook only from what the box will contain, measured exactly as we will pack it, plus the basics we assume every kitchen has: salt, pepper, oil and water.

This test catches the gaps. A recipe that needs "a splash of vinegar" must either send the vinegar or not need it. A step that says "in a large pan" must work in the pan most people own. I time every step with a stopwatch, and the time on the card comes from this cook, not from the first one.

:::figure{src="wooden-spoons.jpg" alt="A pot of well-used wooden spoons and spatulas in different shapes, the wood dark with age, the ordinary tools we use to test a recipe at home"}
Test two uses the tools most home kitchens have. If a recipe needs anything special, we either send it or rewrite the step.
:::

## Test three: does the card make sense?

The last cook is the hardest way to test a recipe, and the most useful. Nell, our cookery teacher, gives the box and the recipe card to someone who has never cooked the dish, often a beginner from one of her classes. She watches them cook it and does not help.

She writes down every moment they hesitate, reread a step or ask a question. Each of those moments becomes a change to the card. Common fixes include:

- **Splitting a step.** "Fry the onion and garlic" becomes two steps, because garlic burns in the time an onion takes to soften.
- **Saying what done looks like.** "Cook for five minutes" becomes "cook for five minutes, until the edges turn golden".
- **Moving a job earlier.** If the rice needs to go on first, the card says so in step one, not step four.

## What a recipe has to pass

| Test | Who cooks | Where | What passes |
|---|---|---|---|
| One | Head cook | Development kitchen | Tastes worth cooking again |
| Two | Head cook | Small home kitchen | Box holds everything; time on the card is real |
| Three | A beginner | Their own kitchen | Cooked without help or confusion |

A recipe that passes all three goes on the menu. A recipe that fails test three twice goes back to test one, because the problem is usually the dish, not the card.

:::callout{kind=note}
About one recipe in three that I develop makes it to a box. The others are not wasted: they feed the team at lunch, and some come back a season later in a simpler form.
:::

## Why we test a recipe three times, not once

Each test protects you from a different kind of disappointment. Test one protects you from a dull dinner. Test two protects you from a missing ingredient at seven o'clock. Test three protects you from a step that makes sense to a chef and to nobody else.

The same thinking shapes everything we write here. When we test something, such as [how long herbs last in paper](/blog/herbs-paper-storage/), we say how we tested it. And once a recipe passes, it still has to survive [packing day](/blog/packing-day/) before it reaches your door.
