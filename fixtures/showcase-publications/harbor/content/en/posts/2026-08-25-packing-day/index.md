On packing day, your box is built by hand in about four minutes, after the produce in it has been checked three times since dawn. The day starts at six with a temperature check, the produce check follows at half past six, and the first boxes leave on the vans at eleven. Here is what happens at each step.

I run the packing kitchen, and I still walk the cold room myself every morning. Tuesday and Thursday are packing day, and these are the steps we follow on both.

## Six o'clock: the cold room

The first job of the day is the thermometers. We check the cold room, both fridges and the van loading bay, and we write the numbers on a sheet by the door. Leafy greens and herbs need to stay between 2°C and 5°C. If any reading is out, nothing moves until we know why.

## Half past six: the produce check

The farms deliver the afternoon before, so the produce has rested overnight in the cold. At half past six, two of us open every crate. We look for three things:

- **Freshness.** Leaves stand up, stalks snap, and nothing smells sour.
- **Size.** A recipe that says "one medium carrot" needs carrots that are actually medium.
- **Damage.** We take out anything bruised, split or soft, and it goes to the staff kitchen or the compost.

Anything we reject is written down against the farm it came from. Joel, who leads sourcing, reads that sheet every week and talks to the farm about it. Recipes get the same scrutiny before they reach the line: see [how we test every recipe](/blog/test-every-recipe/). You can read how he chooses those farms in [how we choose our farms](/blog/choose-farms/).

## Eight o'clock: the line

Packing happens on one long table. Each person stands at a station with one or two items, and the boxes move along the table from station to station. Heavy, hard produce goes in first: potatoes, onions, squash. Soft and leafy produce goes on top, so nothing is crushed on the van.

:::figure{src="packed-box.jpg" alt="A box finished on packing day, open and full of lettuce, broccoli, oranges, apples, potatoes and celeriac, seen from above"}
Heavy and hard at the bottom, soft and leafy on top. A packed box should be full enough that nothing rolls when you tip it.
:::

The measured pantry items, such as rice, spices and stock, travel in a paper bag with each recipe card. One bag per dinner means you never have to sort the box when it arrives.

## The last checks of packing day

Before the lid goes on, the last person on the line checks three things:

1. **Count.** Every recipe card in the box has its bag and its produce.
2. **Swaps.** If we had to swap an item, for example one kind of greens for another, the swap note is in the box.
3. **Cold.** Each box gets ice packs sized for the day's weather forecast. Keeping food cold from farm to door is called the [cold chain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cold_chain), and packing day is where it is easiest to break.

:::callout{kind=note}
If anything is missing or damaged when your box arrives, write to hello@example.com with a photo. We refund the item or send it again, and we trace it back to the line so it does not happen twice.
:::

## Eleven o'clock: the vans

The first vans leave at eleven and the last at two. Each van holds one round of deliveries, planned so the box spends as little time out of the cold as possible. By the evening of packing day the table is scrubbed, the crates are back with the farms, and the cold room is ready for the next delivery.
