To hold a chef's knife well, pinch the blade just in front of the handle between your thumb and the side of your bent first finger, and wrap your other three fingers around the handle. That pinch grip gives you control of the tip, so the knife goes where you mean it to. Your other hand curls into a claw and guides the food towards the blade.

Most people who find chopping slow hold a chef's knife by the end of the handle, like a hammer. It feels safe, but the blade wobbles, and a wobbling blade slips. In my beginners' classes, the grip is the first thing we fix, and it changes everything else.

## How to hold a chef's knife, step by step

1. Hold the handle loosely in your hand, the blade pointing away from you.
2. Slide your hand forward until your first finger touches the bolster, the thick part where the blade meets the handle.
3. Curl your first finger and lay its side flat against the blade.
4. Press your thumb against the other side of the blade, opposite that finger.
5. Wrap your middle, ring and little fingers around the handle.

When you hold a chef's knife this way, your grip should feel firm at the pinch and relaxed everywhere else. If your knuckles turn white, you are squeezing too hard, and your wrist will tire within ten minutes.

:::callout{kind=tip}
Practise the grip on something soft and cheap, such as a courgette or a stick of celery. You are learning a movement, not making dinner.
:::

## The claw hand keeps your fingers safe

The hand that holds the food matters as much as the hand that holds the knife. Curl your fingertips under, so your knuckles stand in front of them. Rest the flat side of the blade against your knuckles as you cut.

Now the blade can only touch your knuckles, and it slides along them instead of cutting. As you slice, walk your claw back along the food a little at a time. Because the knife follows your knuckles, your guiding hand sets the thickness of each slice.

Keep the board flat and the knife where you can see it. A damp cloth under the board stops it sliding.
:::

## Let the blade do the work

A sharp [chef's knife](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chef%27s_knife) cuts when it moves forward and down at the same time. Pushing straight down crushes food, which is why tomatoes burst and onions make you cry more.

Instead, rock the knife. Keep the tip on the board and lift the heel, then bring the heel down as you push the blade forward. That rocking cut is how a cook dices herbs and onions fast. For larger, firmer vegetables, such as squash or swede, use a long forward slice with the whole length of the blade.

## Three habits that make chopping safer

- **Stand square to the board.** Face the board with your feet apart and your knife arm free to move.
- **Cut a flat side first.** Round food rolls. Slice a thin piece off a carrot or a potato, then lay it on that flat side before you cut further.
- **Keep the knife sharp.** A blunt knife needs force, and force is what makes a knife slip. Read our guide to [honing and sharpening](/blog/honing-sharpening/) to keep yours in shape.

## What to practise this week

Pick one recipe from your week that needs an onion, and dice it slowly with the pinch grip and the claw hand. Speed comes after about a dozen onions, not before, and so does the habit to hold a chef's knife without thinking about it. Once the grip feels natural, try it on the tray of vegetables in [our one-tray roasting guide](/blog/roast-vegetables-one-tray/), where even pieces cook evenly.
