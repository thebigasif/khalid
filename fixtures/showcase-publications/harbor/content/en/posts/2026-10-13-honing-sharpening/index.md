Honing and sharpening do two different jobs. Honing straightens an edge that has bent out of line, takes a few seconds and should happen every week or two. Sharpening grinds away a little metal to make a brand-new edge, takes ten to fifteen minutes and is needed only a few times a year. Most knives that feel blunt need honing, not sharpening.

In my classes, the most common knife problem is not a bad knife. It is a good knife that has never been honed. Here is how to tell what yours needs and how to do each job.

## What happens to a knife edge

Seen very close up, a sharp edge is a thin strip of metal that comes to a point. Each time it meets a chopping board, that thin strip bends a tiny amount to one side. After a few weeks of cooking, the edge is still there, but it is rolled over and no longer meets the food straight on. The knife feels blunt.

Honing pushes that rolled edge back into line. Nothing is removed. Sharpening is for when the edge itself has worn away, and only grinding a new one will help.

## Honing or sharpening at a glance

| Question | Honing | Sharpening |
|---|---|---|
| What it does | Straightens the existing edge | Grinds a new edge |
| Tool | Honing steel or ceramic rod | Whetstone or a guided sharpener |
| Metal removed | None, or almost none | A thin layer |
| How long it takes | 10 to 20 seconds | 10 to 15 minutes |
| How often | Every week or two | Two to four times a year |
| Sign you need it | Knife slides off a tomato skin | Honing no longer helps |

## How to hone a knife

1. Hold the [honing steel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honing_steel) upright, its tip resting on a folded cloth on the counter.
2. Place the heel of the knife against the top of the steel, at a shallow angle, about the width of two stacked coins between the spine and the steel.
3. Draw the knife down and towards you, so the whole edge from heel to tip passes along the steel. Use light pressure; this is a stroke, not a scrape.
4. Repeat on the other side of the steel for the other side of the blade.
5. Do five or six strokes on each side, alternating.

Holding the steel upright against the counter is slower than the dramatic way chefs do it in the air, and much safer. Speed is not the point.

:::callout{kind=tip}
To test your knife, rest a ripe tomato on the board and draw the blade across the skin with no downward pressure. A honed knife bites at once. A knife that slides off needs honing first, then a second test.
:::

## Sharpening on a whetstone

Sharpening a knife on a whetstone takes practice, but the movement is simple. Soak the stone if the maker says to, set it on a damp cloth so it cannot slide, and hold the knife at the same shallow angle you use for honing.

1. Start on the coarse side of the stone. Push the blade across it, edge first, as if you were slicing a thin layer off the top.
2. Work along the edge in sections until you can feel a tiny rough lip, called a burr, on the other side of the blade.
3. Turn the knife over and repeat on the other side.
4. Move to the fine side of the stone and repeat both sides with lighter strokes.
5. Finish with a few strokes on the honing steel, then wash and dry the knife.

If a stone feels like too much, a guided pull-through sharpener is fine for most home knives. It removes more metal than a stone, so use it less often.

## Look after the edge between sharpenings

- **Cut on wood or plastic.** Glass, stone and metal boards blunt an edge in days.
- **Wash by hand.** A dishwasher knocks knives against each other and the heat can loosen handles.
- **Store it covered.** A knife loose in a drawer chips its edge on everything else in there.

A sharp knife is the safer knife, because it needs no force. Once yours is keen, practise [how to hold a chef's knife](/blog/hold-chefs-knife/) and try it on the hardest job in autumn cooking: [cutting a winter squash](/blog/cut-roast-winter-squash/).
