To roast vegetables on one tray so they all finish at once, cut the slow ones small and the quick ones large, and add them in two rounds: dense roots first, soft vegetables twenty minutes later. Use a hot oven, 220°C or 200°C with a fan, and give every piece room on the tray, because browning, the [Maillard reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maillard_reaction), needs dry heat. Crowded vegetables steam instead of roasting.

This is the method behind half the dinners in our boxes. A tray of roast vegetables can carry a salad, a pasta, a grain bowl or a soup, and it asks for ten minutes of chopping and one tin to wash.

::toc

## Why vegetables on one tray finish at different times

A carrot and a courgette cook at very different speeds. Carrots are dense and dry, so heat takes a long time to reach the middle. Courgettes are mostly water, so they soften in minutes and collapse if you leave them.

You can fix that in two ways, and the best trays use both:

- **Size.** Cut dense vegetables smaller and watery ones bigger, so heat reaches every middle at about the same time.
- **Timing.** Put dense vegetables in first and add the quick ones partway through.

## Roasting times for common vegetables

These are the times our test kitchen uses at 220°C, or 200°C with a fan, on a dark metal tray on the middle shelf. A pale or glass dish needs five to ten minutes longer.

| Vegetable | Cut | Time at 220°C | Goes in |
|---|---|---|---|
| Potato | 2 cm cubes | 40 to 45 min | First |
| Carrot | 1.5 cm batons | 35 to 40 min | First |
| Parsnip | 2 cm batons, core removed | 30 to 35 min | First |
| Beetroot | 2 cm wedges | 40 to 45 min | First |
| Winter squash | 2.5 cm cubes | 30 to 35 min | First |
| Red onion | Eighths, root left on | 30 to 35 min | First |
| Sweet potato | 2.5 cm cubes | 25 to 30 min | First |
| Cauliflower | 4 cm florets | 25 to 30 min | Second |
| Brussels sprouts | Halved | 20 to 25 min | Second |
| Pepper | 3 cm strips | 20 to 25 min | Second |
| Courgette | 3 cm half moons | 18 to 20 min | Second |
| Broccoli | 4 cm florets | 15 to 18 min | Second |
| Cherry tomatoes | Whole | 12 to 15 min | Last |

Start the timer when the first round goes in. When the timer shows twenty minutes left, add the second round. Add [ripe tomatoes](/blog/choose-ripe-tomatoes/) for the last fifteen minutes, or they split and run.

:::callout{kind=tip}
Heat the empty tray in the oven while you chop. Vegetables that land on hot metal start to brown at once, and they stick less.
:::

## How to roast vegetables, step by step

1. Heat the oven to 220°C, or 200°C with a fan, with the tray inside.
2. Cut the first-round vegetables to the sizes in the table. Toss them in a bowl with a tablespoon of oil per tray and a good pinch of salt.
3. Tip them onto the hot tray in one layer, cut sides down, with a finger's width between pieces.
4. Roast until twenty minutes remain, then add the second round, tossed in oil and salt the same way. Turn the first round with a spatula while the oven is open.
5. Roast until the edges are brown and a knife slides into the biggest piece of potato with no push.

## The mistakes that make vegetables soggy

Soggy roast vegetables almost always come from one of three things:

- **A crowded tray.** Water escapes from each piece as steam. When pieces touch, the steam has nowhere to go and the vegetables boil in it. Use two trays rather than one full one.
- **Too much oil.** Vegetables should shine, not swim. Oil pooling in the tin fries the bottoms and leaves the tops pale.
- **A cool oven.** Below 200°C, vegetables soften before they brown. Give the oven time to reach heat before the tray goes in.

## Turn one tray into three dinners

Roast a double tray on Sunday and it carries the week. Toss warm vegetables with pasta and a spoon of the cooking oil on Monday. Fold them through salad leaves with a sharp dressing on Tuesday. Then blend what is left with stock for a soup on Thursday. We plan the week that way in [our guide to planning a week of dinners](/blog/plan-week-dinners/), and the [Sunday batch cooking plan](/blog/sunday-batch-cooking/) puts this tray in the oven first.

On a night when the oven feels like too much, the same idea works on the hob: see [one-pan dinners](/blog/one-pan-dinners/). Even cuts matter more than any timing table. If your pieces come out uneven, spend a week on [how to hold a chef's knife](/blog/hold-chefs-knife/) and your trays will cook evenly too.
