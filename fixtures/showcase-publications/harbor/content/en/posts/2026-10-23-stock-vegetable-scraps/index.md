To make stock from vegetable scraps, keep your clean onion ends, carrot peel, celery leaves and herb stalks in a bag in the freezer, and when the bag is full, simmer them in a pot of water for forty-five minutes. Strain it, and you have a light, savoury [stock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stock_(food)) for soups, risotto and sauces. It costs nothing, because you were going to throw the scraps away.

I started making stock this way when I noticed how much of every vegetable ended up in the bin during a cookery class. A week of cooking for two fills a freezer bag, and a full bag makes about two litres.

## Which scraps to keep

For stock from vegetable scraps, keep scraps that are clean and fresh, just not the part you wanted to eat:

- **Onion, leek and shallot.** Ends, outer layers and the green tops of leeks. Onion skins add a golden colour.
- **Carrot.** Peel, tops and the ends of the root.
- **Celery.** Leaves, ends and the pale hearts.
- **Herb stalks.** Parsley, thyme, coriander and dill stalks carry as much flavour as the leaves.
- **Mushroom stalks.** They make a deeper, darker stock.
- **Garlic.** Ends and the papery outer cloves.
- **Tomato.** Cores and the ends of ripe tomatoes, in small amounts.

## Scraps that spoil a stock

Some vegetables turn stock bitter, cloudy or strong enough to take over:

- **Cabbage, broccoli, sprouts and cauliflower.** Long cooking makes them taste sulphurous.
- **Potato peel.** It clouds the stock and adds little flavour.
- **Beetroot.** It turns everything pink.
- **Anything mouldy, slimy or sour.** Stock concentrates flavours, including bad ones.

:::callout{kind=tip}
Keep a large freezer bag or a lidded tub in the freezer, labelled "stock". Add scraps as you cook. Frozen scraps go straight into the pot; there is no need to thaw them.
:::

## How to make stock from vegetable scraps, step by step

1. **Fill the pot.** Tip a full bag of scraps, about 600 g, into a large pan.
2. **Add flavour.** Add a bay leaf, a few peppercorns and, if you have one, a fresh onion cut in half. A fresh onion gives the stock a sweeter, rounder base.
3. **Cover with water.** Pour over cold water to cover the scraps by about 5 cm, roughly two and a half litres.
4. **Simmer, do not boil.** Bring it to the boil, then turn it down so only a few bubbles break the surface. Hard boiling makes stock cloudy.
5. **Wait forty-five minutes.** Leave the lid off and do not stir. Vegetable stock does not improve after an hour; it starts to taste stewed.
6. **Strain.** Pour it through a sieve into a bowl and press the scraps gently with a spoon. Then the scraps go in the compost.

Do not add salt while it cooks. Unsalted stock gives you control later, when it reduces in a sauce or a risotto.

## Store it

Cool the stock quickly, then keep it in the fridge for up to four days or the freezer for three months. Freeze some in an ice cube tray: a few cubes lift a pan sauce or a pot of rice.

## Use it this week

Use your stock in place of water when you cook rice or lentils, as the base for a squash or roast vegetable soup, or to loosen a sauce. If you batch cook on Sunday, the trimmings from [the two-hour plan](/blog/sunday-batch-cooking/) fill most of a bag in one afternoon. Making stock from vegetable scraps is part of the way we cut waste in our own kitchen, alongside [how we store root vegetables](/blog/store-root-vegetables/).
