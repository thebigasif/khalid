We choose farms by visiting each one, tasting what it grows, and then ordering small amounts for a whole season before we commit to more. A farm grows for Harbor Kitchen only when it shows us five things: good flavour, steady quality, honest communication, care for its soil and workers, and a distance short enough for produce to reach us within a day. Most of our farms are within two hours' drive of the kitchen.

I have bought produce for most of my working life, first on a wholesale market floor and now for our boxes. The way we choose farms is slow on purpose. Here is how it works and why.

::toc

## Why the farm matters more than the price

A meal kit lives or dies on its produce, which is why we choose farms with such care. A recipe can be perfect, but if the carrots are woody or the herbs are wilted, the dinner is a disappointment. And most of what makes a carrot good happens on the farm: the variety, the soil, when it was picked and how it was handled.

So we do not start with a price list. We start with a visit.

## Step one: the visit

I visit every farm before we buy anything. I walk the fields, look at how crops are planted and picked, and see where the produce waits after harvest. I always ask to see the cold store, because a farm that cools its produce fast keeps it fresh for days longer.

I also taste, because no list helps you choose farms as well as a mouthful does. I pull a carrot from the ground, break a lettuce leaf, or eat a tomato in the polytunnel. The best farms are proud to let you.

:::figure{src="lettuce.jpg" alt="A frilly green lettuce growing in dark soil, seen close up at ground level with more lettuces blurred behind it"}
A visit always includes the field itself. Healthy soil shows in the plants: even growth, strong colour and leaves that stand up.
:::

## Step two: the trial season

If the visit goes well, we order small amounts for a whole season. A single good week tells us little. A season tells us whether quality holds through a wet month, whether orders arrive on time and whether the farm tells us early when a crop fails.

During the trial, Theo's packing team checks every crate from the farm at six each morning and writes down anything they reject. You can read how that check works in [what happens on packing day](/blog/packing-day/). At the end of the season, we sit down with the farm and go through that sheet together.

## Five things we look for when we choose farms

| What we look for | How we check it |
|---|---|
| Flavour | We taste on the visit and at every recipe test |
| Steady quality | The packing team's rejection sheet over a full season |
| Honest communication | Whether the farm warns us early about short or failed crops |
| Care for soil and workers | The visit, and a conversation about crop rotation, [cover crops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cover_crop) and pay |
| Distance | Produce reaches our cold room within a day of picking |

No farm is perfect on all five every week. We look for farms that are good on all five over a season, and that tell us when they are not.

## How we buy once a farm is with us

Once a farm grows for us, we agree on the crops for the next season together, months ahead. That gives the farm a planned order before it plants, and it gives us produce grown for our recipes. A farm that knows we need medium carrots in March can plant for medium carrots in March.

We also take what the field gives. When a crop comes in early or heavy, Ruth writes a recipe around it and [tests it three times](/blog/test-every-recipe/) like any other. When a crop fails, we swap it and say so in the box. Our recipe cards follow the farms, not the other way round, which is why a [late summer box](/blog/choose-ripe-tomatoes/) looks so different from an autumn one.

:::callout{kind=note}
We pay every farm within fourteen days of delivery. Small farms run on thin margins, and waiting two months for payment is one of the reasons good growers stop growing.
:::

## What a farm gets from us

Working with a meal kit is different from selling to a supermarket or a market stall. We tell our farms the crops and sizes we need a season ahead, we take odd-shaped produce that tastes good, and we send back every crate. In return, we ask for consistency and honesty.

:::figure{src="carrots.jpg" alt="A large bunch of freshly pulled orange carrots with leafy green tops, laid on a mesh table outdoors"}
Carrots straight from one of our growers. We take them in all shapes; size matters more than looks, because the recipe card says "one medium carrot".
:::

## When we stop working with a farm

It happens rarely, but it happens. The most common reason is not quality. It is silence: a farm that sends short orders without warning, so we only find out on the packing line. A farm that tells us on Monday that the leeks failed is a farm we can work with. A farm that sends half the leeks on Tuesday morning is not.

## What this means for your box

Every vegetable in your box comes from a farm someone at Harbor Kitchen has walked and tasted. It is also why our boxes change with the seasons, and why we can tell you exactly where your dinner came from.
