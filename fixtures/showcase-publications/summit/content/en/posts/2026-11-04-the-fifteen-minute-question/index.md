Can you buy bread, see a doctor, drop a child at school and catch a bus, all within fifteen minutes' walk of your front door? That is the fifteen-minute question. Ines Albarran closes the summit by putting Easthaven to that test, one neighbourhood at a time.

## Mapping the fifteen-minute question

Before the summit, Ines and a group of volunteers mapped every school, surgery, food shop, park and bus stop in Easthaven. Then they drew a fifteen-minute walk around every home in town, along real pavements, not as the crow flies.

The result is a map that answers the fifteen-minute question for every street: where daily life is easy on foot and where it is not. The old town and the harbourside pass the test easily. Two estates on the edge of town, built in the 1970s, pass it for almost nothing: no food shop, no surgery and a bus every hour.

## Not a plan to keep people in

The idea has drawn fears that it means stopping people from leaving their area. Ines will take that fear seriously, and then set it aside. A fifteen-minute neighbourhood is about adding things close to home, not about taking anything away. Nobody in Easthaven will need permission to drive across town.

## What the summit adds up to

The keynote also draws the three days together. Trees and shade, a readable bus map, homes above shops and repaired pavements are all part of the same test. Each one makes daily life a little easier without a car.

Ines ends the fifteen-minute question with three changes that would bring the two estates closest to passing the test, and asks the hall to vote on which the council should try first. The council's head of streets will be in the room. The term itself comes from the [15-minute city](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/15-minute_city) idea, which is worth a read before May.

The closing keynote is open to every ticket, in the main hall at the Ropeworks. Ines also gave the [opening keynote](/blog/streets-are-for-staying/) on Thursday, so come to both if you can. Drinks in the foyer follow.
