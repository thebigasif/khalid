Ines Albarran opens the summit with one question about why streets are for staying: how long do people choose to spend in a street? For most of the last century, towns measured streets by how fast cars got through them. Ines measures the other thing, and she has the numbers from eleven high streets to show why it matters.

## Why streets are for staying

Over six years, Ines and her team counted people on high streets before and after each one was redesigned. They did not count footfall alone. They counted how many people stopped, sat, talked or let a child play, and for how long.

The streets that changed the most had three things in common:

- **Somewhere to sit every fifty metres,** without having to buy a coffee first.
- **Pavements wide enough for two prams to pass,** so nobody steps into the road.
- **Shade and shelter,** from trees in summer and from canopies in the rain.

None of these is expensive. However, each one takes space from somewhere else, usually from parking, and that is where the arguments start.

## The case for staying, in money

Shopkeepers often fear that fewer parking spaces mean fewer customers. In Ines's counts, people who walked or cycled to a high street came more often and spent more each month than people who drove, even though each visit was smaller. A street where people stay longer is a street where they spend longer. In money terms too, streets are for staying.

The keynote also looks at the streets where the redesign failed, and why. In two towns the benches went in, but nothing else changed, and the benches stayed empty. A street for staying is a whole set of choices, not one piece of furniture.
