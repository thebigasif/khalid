Two years ago, Easthaven council put every report of a broken pavement or a pothole on an open map, along with the date it was fixed. Tomasz Wrona's talk is about what happened next, and why the most reported street repairs were not always the most needed ones.

## What the open data on street repairs showed

When the map went live, anyone could see how long a report took to fix. Within a month, local news had found that reports from some parts of town were fixed twice as fast as others. The reason was simple. The council fixed reports in the order they came in, and some streets reported far more.

Streets with many reports were not the streets in the worst state. They were streets where people knew how to report a fault, had time to do it and expected it to be fixed.

## What the council changed

The street team now ranks every repair by three things, not one:

- **How bad it is,** from a survey of every street once a year, not only from reports.
- **Who uses it,** with extra weight for routes to schools, clinics and bus stops.
- **How long it has waited.**

Reports still matter, because they show new damage between surveys. But a report no longer puts a street at the front of the queue on its own.

## Did it work?

Tomasz will show the numbers. The gap in repair times between parts of town has closed by two thirds. The total number of repairs has not changed, but more of them are now on pavements, where a trip can break a hip, and fewer on quiet roads.

The talk ends with what other councils can copy for very little money. The data itself is published under an [open data](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_data) licence, and you can download it from the council's site before May.
