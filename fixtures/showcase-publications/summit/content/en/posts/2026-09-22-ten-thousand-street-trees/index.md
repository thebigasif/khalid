Planting street trees is the easy part. Keeping them alive for their first five years is the hard part. In this talk, Tomasz Wrona tells how Easthaven planted ten thousand street trees since 2018, and what the council changed after losing almost a third of the first thousand.

## Why young street trees die

Most young trees in a town die of thirst or of a pit that is too small. A tree in a pavement grows its roots into whatever soil it can find. When the pit is a small square of earth under paving slabs, the roots soon hit compacted ground, and the tree stops growing.

Easthaven's first planting in 2018 used standard pits and watered each tree twice in its first summer. By 2021, 290 of the first 1,000 trees were dead or dying.

## What the council changed

Tomasz's team changed four things for every tree planted since 2021:

1. **Bigger pits.** Each tree now gets a trench of loose soil under the pavement, joined to the next tree's trench, so roots can spread.
2. **The right species for the street.** Narrow streets get narrow trees. Streets near the sea get trees that put up with salt.
3. **Three summers of watering,** with a bag at the foot of each tree that drips water slowly over a day.
4. **A name and a number.** Every tree is on the council's open map, and neighbours can adopt one and report a problem.

Since then, fewer than one tree in twenty has died in its first three years.

## What it costs

A tree with a joined trench costs about three times as much to plant as a tree in a small pit. Tomasz shows why it is still cheaper per living tree, and how the council paid for it. The [urban forest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_forest) idea, where a town plans its trees as one woodland, shaped the whole programme.
