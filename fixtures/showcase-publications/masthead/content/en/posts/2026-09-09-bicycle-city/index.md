Here is a simple test for any street. Could a twelve-year-old ride a bicycle along it to school, alone, without their parents worrying? If the answer is yes, the street almost certainly works for everyone else too.

## Why the test works

A street that is safe for a child on a bicycle has slow traffic, clear crossings and room to pass. Those same things help a pensioner crossing the road, a parent with a pram and a wheelchair user on the pavement. The bicycle test is not really about cycling. It is about whether a street was designed for people or only for cars.

## What makes the difference

- **Slower speeds.** At twenty miles an hour, crashes are fewer and far less serious.
- **Protected lanes** on busy roads, separated from traffic by a kerb.
- **Quiet routes** through side streets, with through traffic kept out.
- **Somewhere to lock up** at shops, schools and stations.

## What happens when towns try

Towns that have built safe routes see more children cycling to school within a year or two. Shops on those streets often do better, not worse, because people on foot and on bicycles stop more often than drivers do. The charity [Sustrans](https://www.sustrans.org.uk/) has studied many of these schemes and publishes what worked.

## A test you can run

Walk your nearest school route at the start of the day. Count how many children ride or walk, and note where they have to cross. Then ask your council what it plans for that route. For another small thing that changes a street, read Daniel's piece [in praise of the park bench](/blog/park-bench/).
