A park bench costs a town less than a parking space. It needs no power, no staff and no app. Yet for many people it decides whether a walk is possible at all. If there is nowhere to rest, an older person or a parent with a tired child simply stays at home.

## Who needs a seat

Walking groups say the same thing again and again. Many of their members can walk a mile, but not a mile without a stop. A bench every few hundred metres turns a short walk into a long one. It also turns a street into somewhere to be, not just somewhere to pass through.

Benches help in quieter ways too. A seat outside a shop gives a carer a place to wait. A seat by a bus stop gives someone a place to sit while they catch their breath. A seat in the sun is where neighbours meet without planning to.

## Where good benches go

- **On the routes people already walk,** such as the way from the station to the high street.
- **Every 300 metres or so,** which suits most people who need to rest.
- **With a back and arms,** so it is easy to sit down and stand up again.
- **Facing something,** such as a view, a playground or a busy corner.

## Why some towns take them away

Some councils remove benches because they worry about who might sleep or gather there. Taking the seat away rarely solves that. It only moves the problem and leaves everyone else standing. The charity [Living Streets](https://www.livingstreets.org.uk/) has good advice on making streets work for people on foot, benches included.

## A small test for any town

Walk from your station to your library and count the seats. If you find fewer than five, write to your council. And if you are curious how one simple thing can change a street, read Daniel's piece on [the bicycle as a test of a good street](/blog/bicycle-city/).
