For twenty years the corner bakery was a story of loss. Supermarket bread was cheaper, rents went up, and one by one the small shops closed. Now, in town after town, a new one opens. I worked in bakeries for six years, so I went to find out why.

## A different kind of shop

The new bakeries are small and do a few things well. Most bake two or three kinds of bread, a handful of pastries and one cake a day. They open early, sell out by early afternoon and close. That is not a failure. Baking less means less waste, and selling out means the bread on the shelf is always fresh.

Many began somewhere else first: a market stall, a home kitchen, a weekly bread club. By the time they took a shop, they already had customers.

## Why it works now

- **People will pay for good bread,** if it is good every day.
- **Empty shops are cheaper** on many high streets than they were ten years ago.
- **Bread clubs and preorders** tell a baker how much to make, so less is thrown away.

## The hard parts

The hours are brutal. A small bakery starts work at four in the morning, six days a week. Flour and energy prices swing from month to month. Most of the bakers I met pay themselves very little in the first two years. The [Real Bread Campaign](https://www.realbreadcampaign.org/) helps new bakers with advice and a network of others who have done it.

## What a bakery gives a street

A bakery brings people out early. It gives the street a smell, a queue and a reason to stop. Several of the owners told me the same thing: their best days are not the busiest, but the ones when regulars stay to talk.
