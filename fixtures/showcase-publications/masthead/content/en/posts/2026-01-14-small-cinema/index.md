The small cinema on Mill Street has ninety seats, one screen and a popcorn machine older than most of its staff. Twice in ten years it nearly closed. Both times the town kept it open, and the way it did so says a lot about what a shared room is worth.

## Two near misses

The first came in 2017, when the owner retired and no buyer appeared. A group of regulars formed a trust, borrowed against a summer of fundraising and bought the lease. The second came in 2020, when the doors were shut for most of a year. The trust survived on memberships alone: people kept paying for a cinema they could not visit.

## Run by the people who watch

Today about forty volunteers run the place. They sell tickets, clean the seats and choose a third of the programme. The rest comes from a booking service that sends new releases a few weeks late. Nobody minds the wait. Tickets cost less than half the price of the big screen out of town.

The volunteers are clear about one thing. The films matter, but the room matters more. People come for a Tuesday matinee because they will see the same faces, sit in the same rows and talk about the film on the way out.

## What other towns can borrow

- **Start with members, not donors.** A small yearly fee from many people is steadier than a large gift from one.
- **Show what people ask for.** The request book by the door fills a page a week.
- **Keep it cheap.** Low prices fill seats, and full seats sell drinks.

The [British Film Institute](https://www.bfi.org.uk/) keeps a list of help for community screens, from licences to grants. It is a good first stop for any group that wants to try.

## The next ten years

The trust now wants a second, smaller screen in the old projection room, for documentaries and children's films. It has raised about half of the money.
