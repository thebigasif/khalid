The Bow Lane townhouse was built in 1790: four storeys of red brick with a stone doorcase. It had been split into five flats in the 1960s and left empty for six years. A family bought it to make it one house again.

## Repair first

Most of the budget went on repair. We took out the 1960s partitions, mended the sash windows rather than replacing them, and relaid the roof with the old slates and a few new ones. The front rooms are now as they were built: two rooms on each floor either side of the stair. Where the old lime plaster had fallen we patched it with lime, never with gypsum.

## A kitchen in the yard of the Bow Lane townhouse

The old kitchen was in the basement, dark and damp. We moved it to the back of the ground floor, into a new single storey room that fills part of the yard. Its roof is a shallow slope of lead with one long roof light against the old wall, so daylight washes down the brick.

## Old and new side by side

The new room is plain: lime plaster walls, a floor of the same stone as the hall and oak doors. Inside the old house we matched what was there. Outside, the new work is clearly new, in a darker brick, so anyone can read the history of the Bow Lane townhouse from the yard. The planning officer asked for exactly that.

## What it gave the family

The house went from five small flats to one home with six bedrooms, a study at the top and a kitchen where the family spends most of its day. The basement is now a dry store and a utility room, kept dry by a lime floor that lets the ground breathe.

## Georgian houses and light

Georgian houses were built for daylight, with tall windows and shallow rooms, and they reward care. Wikipedia has a good overview of [Georgian architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georgian_architecture).
