Buying a first telescope is easy to get wrong. The box promises huge magnification, the mount wobbles, and the telescope ends up in a cupboard. This week telescope maker Ama Mensah joins Nadia to explain what really matters, and Owen confesses what he bought first.

## In this episode

- Why aperture, the width of the main lens or mirror, decides what you can see.
- Why a box that boasts huge magnification is a warning sign, not a feature.
- The case for a simple reflector on a Dobsonian mount, which you point by hand.
- Why a good pair of binoculars might be the best first step of all.

## In a first telescope, aperture beats magnification

A telescope collects light. The wider its main lens or mirror, the more light it gathers and the fainter the things it shows. Magnification only spreads that light out. As Ama puts it in the episode: "A small telescope at high power shows you a big, dim blur."

A [common rule of thumb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_telescope) is that the most magnification a telescope can use is about twice its aperture in millimetres. So a 70 mm telescope tops out at about 140 times, whatever the box says.

## The mount matters as much as the tube

A shaky mount makes any telescope hard to use. At high power, a touch of the focuser sets the view dancing for seconds. Ama's advice is to spend as much care on the mount as on the tube, and to try a telescope before you buy it if you can. A local astronomy club or a [public star party](/blog/star-party-live-show/) is a good place to look through a few.

## Start with binoculars

Nadia's own first instrument was a pair of 10x50 binoculars, and she still uses them every clear night. They show the craters of the Moon, the moons of Jupiter and star clusters that the eye alone misses. They also teach you the sky, which makes a telescope much easier to use later. When you do move up, a small reflector is enough to [see Saturn's rings](/blog/saturns-rings/) for yourself.
