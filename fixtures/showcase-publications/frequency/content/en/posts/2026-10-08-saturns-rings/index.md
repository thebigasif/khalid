Saturn's rings are the sight that turns visitors at Nadia's observatory into astronomers. This week planetary scientist Dr Mei Lin Zhou tells Owen what the rings are made of, how thin they are, and why they may be a short chapter in Saturn's long story.

## In this episode

- What the rings are made of: countless pieces of water ice, from grains to boulders.
- How a ring system wider than the Earth and Moon apart can be so thin.
- What the Cassini spacecraft found before it ended its mission in 2017.
- Why the rings seem to vanish from view every 13 to 15 years.

## Wide and thin

The main rings stretch across about 280,000 kilometres, yet in most places they are only tens of metres thick. If you made a model of them as wide as a football pitch, it would be thinner than a sheet of paper. Their brightness comes from ice, which reflects sunlight well.

## Saturn's rings may be young

Measurements by the Cassini spacecraft suggest the rings may be far younger than Saturn itself, perhaps a few hundred million years old. Material from the rings also falls into the planet, which some scientists call ring rain. Dr Zhou explains that over a very long time the rings could thin out and fade. "We are lucky to be here while they are bright," she says.

## Edge on from Earth

Saturn's tilt means we see its rings from different angles as it goes around the Sun. Every 13 to 15 years the rings line up edge on with the Earth and almost disappear in small telescopes. That last happened in March 2025, so the rings are now opening up again for the years ahead.
