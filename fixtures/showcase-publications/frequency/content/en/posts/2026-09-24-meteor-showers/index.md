Meteor showers are the easiest sky event to enjoy. You need no telescope, no binoculars and no skill, just a dark spot and some patience. In this short episode Owen and Nadia go through the meteor showers of the coming months and how to make the most of each one.

## In this episode

- What a meteor is: a grain of dust that burns up high in our air.
- Why showers come back on the same dates every year.
- The four showers from October to January, and which one is best.
- How to watch: where to look, how long to wait and what to bring.

## The meteor showers to come

| Shower | Peak night | Best rate |
|---|---|---|
| Orionids | about 21 October | 20 an hour |
| Leonids | about 17 November | 15 an hour |
| Geminids | about 14 December | up to 120 an hour |
| Quadrantids | about 3 January | up to 100 an hour, briefly |

The best rates are for a perfect, dark sky with the shower's radiant high up. From a town, expect far fewer. The Moon matters too: a bright Moon near the peak hides all but the brightest meteors.

## Why the same dates every year

A comet or asteroid leaves a trail of dust along its orbit. Each year the Earth crosses that trail on about the same date, and the dust burns up as it hits our air at tens of kilometres a second. The Orionids come from Halley's Comet and the Leonids from Comet Tempel-Tuttle. The Geminids are unusual because their dust comes from an asteroid, 3200 Phaethon, and the Quadrantids probably come from another, 2003 EH1.

## How to watch

Lie back on a reclining chair and look about halfway up the sky. Meteors appear all over the sky, not just near the point they seem to come from. Give your eyes twenty minutes in the dark, and give yourself at least an hour. Our episode on [picking a dark spot](/blog/find-dark-sky/) helps you choose where to go. While you wait, you may [spot the space station](/blog/space-station-orbit/) gliding past, and in a strong year the same nights can bring [aurora season](/blog/aurora-glow/) to your sky.
