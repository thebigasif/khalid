In the last few years, people far from the Arctic have seen an aurora from their own gardens. Space physicist Dr Sigrid Aune joins Nadia to explain what makes the sky glow, where the colours come from, and why the Sun has been so lively.

## In this episode

- How particles from the Sun reach the Earth and get steered to the poles.
- Why most auroras are green, and what makes the rare red ones.
- The Sun's eleven-year cycle, and why it matters for anyone hoping to see the lights.
- How to use a forecast, and why your phone may see more than your eyes.

## Where the aurora's glow comes from

The Sun sends out a steady stream of charged particles, and now and then a huge burst. When these reach the Earth, our magnetic field guides them towards the poles. There they crash into the thin upper air and make it glow, much as electricity makes the gas in a neon sign glow.

## The colours

The common green glow comes from oxygen, roughly 100 to 250 kilometres up. Red comes from oxygen higher still, and shows mainly in strong displays. Nitrogen adds blues and purples near the lower edge. Dr Aune points out that a phone camera picks up colour that our eyes, which see poorly in the dark, often miss.

## Catching one

The Sun goes through a cycle of about eleven years, and displays are most common near its busiest point. Forecasts use the Kp index, a scale from 0 to 9. The higher the number, the farther from the poles the lights may show. Look north if you live in the northern half of the world, from somewhere with a clear, low view, as our [dark sky episode](/blog/find-dark-sky/) explains.
