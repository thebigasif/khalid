The International Space Station has been crewed without a break since November 2000. It circles the Earth about 400 kilometres up, and it never stops falling. Orbital engineer Dr Tomás Ibarra explains to Nadia and Owen how that works, and why the space station needs a push now and then.

## In this episode

- Why there is still plenty of gravity at the station's height.
- What it means to be in orbit: falling around the Earth, not away from it.
- Why the thin air up there slowly drags the station down.
- How to see the station from your garden with your own eyes.

## Falling around the Earth

At 400 kilometres up, gravity is still about nine tenths as strong as on the ground. The crew float because the station and everyone in it fall together. The station moves sideways at about 28,000 kilometres an hour, so fast that as it falls, the curved Earth drops away beneath it. It goes around the planet roughly every 90 minutes, about 16 times a day.

## Why the space station needs a push

There is a little air even at that height. It drags on the station and slowly lowers its orbit. Every few weeks or months, engines on the station, or on a cargo ship docked to it, fire for a few minutes to lift it back up. Dr Ibarra calls these reboosts "topping up the height".

## See it for yourself

The station is often the brightest thing in the sky after the Moon and Venus. It looks like a steady white light that crosses the sky in a few minutes, with no blinking. You can only see it shortly after sunset or before sunrise, when you are in the dark but the station is still in sunlight.
