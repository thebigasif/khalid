Your body can fight a germ much faster the second time it meets it. Vaccines use that memory. This explainer covers how vaccines work: they give the immune system a safe preview of a germ, so the first real meeting feels like the second.

## The first meeting is slow

When a new germ gets in, the immune system has to work out what it is. Special white blood cells inspect the germ's surface and find an [[glossary:antigen|antigen]], a shape that belongs to the germ and not to you. Building a strong response to that shape takes one to two weeks. In that time, the germ can make you very ill.

## Memory makes the second meeting fast

Once the fight is won, some of the cells stay on as memory cells. B cells remember how to make the right [[glossary:antibody|antibody]], a protein that locks onto the antigen and marks the germ for destruction. T cells remember how to find and kill infected cells.[^1]

:::figure{src=b-cell.jpg alt="A colourised microscope picture of a human B cell, the memory cell behind how vaccines work"}
A human B cell. After an infection or a vaccine, some B cells stay on as memory cells that can make the right antibody within days.
:::

The next time the same germ arrives, memory cells recognise it at once. They make antibodies in days, often before you feel ill at all.

## How vaccines work: a safe first meeting

A vaccine gives the immune system the antigen without the danger. Different vaccines do it in different ways:

- **Weakened or killed germs**, which cannot cause the full disease.
- **A piece of the germ**, such as one surface protein.
- **Instructions for a piece**, such as messenger RNA, which your own cells read to make the protein for a short time.

In each case, the body builds memory cells, just as it would after a real infection, without the illness.

## Why some vaccines need boosters

Memory fades for some germs, and some germs change their surface shapes. A booster reminds the immune system, and a new flu vaccine each year matches the latest strains.

Edward Jenner gave the first vaccine in 1796, against smallpox.[^2] Smallpox was declared wiped out in 1980.
