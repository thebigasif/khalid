Many people think summer comes when Earth is closest to the Sun. It does not: Earth is closest in early January, in the middle of the northern winter. So what causes the seasons? The answer is a tilt.

## Earth spins on a tilted axis

Earth spins around an imaginary line through the poles. That line leans at 23.4 degrees from upright, and it points the same way in space all year. This lean is Earth's [[glossary:axial-tilt|axial tilt]].[^1]

As Earth travels around the Sun, the tilt means that for half the year the northern half leans toward the Sun, and for the other half the southern half does.

## Why leaning toward the Sun makes summer

When your half of the world leans toward the Sun, two things happen.

- **The Sun climbs higher.** Its light hits the ground more directly, so each square metre gets more energy. Shine a torch straight down and you get a bright spot. Tilt it and the same light spreads over a wider, dimmer patch.
- **Days are longer.** The Sun is up for more hours, so the ground has more time to warm and less night to cool.

Six months later your half leans away. The Sun stays low, days are short and it is winter.

## Distance matters very little

Earth's orbit is nearly a circle. Our distance from the Sun changes by only [about 3 percent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apsis) over the year. That makes a small difference to the sunlight we get, far less than the tilt does.[^2] It also cannot explain why Australia has summer while Europe has winter.

## The seasons near the equator and the poles

Near the equator, the Sun is high all year and day length barely changes, so there is little summer or winter. Many places there have wet and dry seasons instead. Near the poles, the tilt brings months of daylight in summer and months of darkness in winter.

## The short answer

The seasons come from Earth's tilt. The half of the world that leans toward the Sun gets higher sun and longer days, which is summer.
