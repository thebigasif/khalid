A lightning bolt is the same kind of spark you get from a door handle on a dry day, made a billion times bigger. This explainer covers how lightning forms: a storm cloud separates electric charge, and when the gap grows too large, the air gives way.

## Storm clouds separate charge

A thunderstorm cloud is tall, often reaching ten kilometres or more. Strong winds inside carry water up to where it freezes. Small ice crystals rise, while heavier soft hail, called graupel, falls.

When they collide, electrons pass from one to the other. The small crystals end up with a positive charge and are carried to the top of the cloud. The graupel takes a negative charge and gathers in the middle and lower parts.[^1] Over minutes, a huge [[glossary:static-charge|static charge]] builds up.

:::figure{src=thunderheads.jpg alt="Tall thunderstorm clouds over the sea, seen from orbit, where lightning forms"}
Thunderstorm clouds near Borneo, seen from orbit. Their tops reach high enough for water to freeze, which is where the charge is made.
:::

## The air breaks down

Air normally stops electricity. But when the charge difference grows large enough, a faint channel of charged air, called a leader, creeps down from the cloud in steps. As it nears the ground, a streamer rises to meet it from a tree, a tower or a person.

When they join, a huge current rushes through the channel in a fraction of a second. That is the bright flash you see. Most flashes never reach the ground at all. They jump inside a cloud or between clouds.

## Why lightning makes thunder

The current heats the channel to about 27,000 °C, hotter than the surface of the Sun. The air expands so fast that it makes a shock wave, and you hear it as thunder.[^2]

Light reaches you almost at once, but sound travels about one kilometre every three seconds. Count the seconds between flash and thunder and divide by three to find how many kilometres away the strike was.

## The short answer

Ice and hail collide in a storm cloud and separate charge. When the charge is great enough, the air breaks down and a current flashes through, heating the air so fast that it booms.
