Why is the sky blue? Sunlight holds every colour, and the air scatters the blue part of it far more than the red part. That scattered blue light reaches your eyes from every direction, so the whole sky is blue to us. This is why we see a blue sky at midday and a red one at sunset.

## Sunlight is a mix of colours

White sunlight is a mix of colours, each with its own [[glossary:wavelength|wavelength]]. Blue has short waves, about 450 nanometres from crest to crest. Red light has long waves, about 700 nanometres. A prism splits the mix apart, and so does a rainbow.

:::figure{src=midday.jpg alt="A clear, even blue sky with no clouds"}
The midday sky. Blue light reaches you from every part of it, because air molecules have scattered it in all directions.
:::

## Why we see a blue sky: air scatters short waves most

The molecules in air are far smaller than a wave of light. When light meets them, a little of it bounces off in a new direction. This is called [[glossary:rayleigh-scattering|Rayleigh scattering]], after the physicist who worked out the rule in 1871.[^1]

The rule is steep. Halve the wavelength and the scattering grows sixteen times. So blue at 450 nanometres scatters about six times more than red light at 700 nanometres. Violet scatters even more, but the Sun sends less of it, and our eyes are less sensitive to it.[^2]

## Why sunsets turn red

At noon, sunlight crosses a short path through the air. At sunset, it comes in low and crosses many times more air before it reaches you. Along that long path, most of the blue is scattered away to the side. What is left is orange and red.

Dust and smoke make sunsets brighter, because larger particles scatter red light too. The same physics explains why distant hills look blue. You see them through miles of air that adds its own blue glow. For another case of light meeting matter, read [how photosynthesis works](/blog/how-photosynthesis-works/), where leaves take in red and blue light and reflect green.

## The short answer

- Sunlight holds every colour.
- Air scatters short blue waves far more than long red ones.
- Scattered blue comes from the whole sky, so we see a blue sky.
- At sunset the light crosses more air, so the blue is lost and red is left.

The [Wikipedia article on Rayleigh scattering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rayleigh_scattering) gives the full maths.
