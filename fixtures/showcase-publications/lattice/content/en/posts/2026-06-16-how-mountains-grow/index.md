Mount Everest is still getting taller. To see how mountains grow, picture two slabs of Earth's crust pushing into each other so hard that the rock between them has nowhere to go but up.

## Earth's surface is broken into plates

Earth's outer shell is cracked into about a dozen large pieces, each called a [[glossary:tectonic-plate|tectonic plate]]. They float on hot, slowly flowing rock below and move a few centimetres a year, about as fast as your fingernails grow.[^1]

Plates meet in three ways. They pull apart, slide past each other or push together. Most of the highest mountains rise where they push together.

## Where mountains grow: when continents collide

Ocean crust is dense, so when it meets another plate it usually sinks beneath it. Continental crust is lighter, so it cannot sink. When two continents meet, they crumple. Layers of rock fold, break and stack on top of each other, and the land rises.

:::figure{src=from-the-air.jpg alt="A wide view from above of snowy ridges, where mountains grow as rock is pushed up"}
The Himalayas from above. The ridges are folds and stacks of rock pushed up as India drives into Asia.
:::

About 50 million years ago, [the plate carrying India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Plate) crashed into Asia. India is still moving north at about 5 centimetres a year. The crust in between has thickened and risen into the Himalayas and the high plateau of Tibet. Fossils of sea creatures are found near the top of Everest, in rock that was once the floor of an ocean.[^2]

## Mountains also wear down

Rain, ice and rivers grind mountains away. A range grows only while the push is faster than the wearing down. Old ranges, such as the Appalachians in North America, were once as high as the Himalayas and have been worn down over hundreds of millions of years.

The same push causes earthquakes. When stuck plates slip, the stored strain is released all at once.

## The short answer

Mountains grow where plates collide and continental rock, too light to sink, crumples upward. For more on how light rock rides on heavy rock, read [why ice floats](/blog/why-ice-floats/). The [Wikipedia article on plate tectonics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plate_tectonics) maps every plate.
