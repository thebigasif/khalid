Twice a day the sea climbs the beach and twice a day it slips away. This is how tides work: the Moon's [[glossary:gravity|gravity]] stretches the oceans into two bulges, and Earth turns through both of them every day. The Sun adds a smaller stretch of its own.

## The Moon pulls unevenly

Gravity grows weaker with distance. The side of Earth that faces the Moon is about 12,700 kilometres closer to it than the far side, so it is pulled a little harder. The middle of Earth is pulled less than the near side and more than the far side.

That difference is the [[glossary:tidal-force|tidal force]]. It stretches Earth along the line to the Moon. The solid planet stretches a little. The oceans, which can flow, stretch much more.[^1]

:::figure{src=moon.jpg alt="The full Moon over Earth's atmosphere, seen from orbit, whose pull explains how tides work"}
The Moon seen from orbit. It is far smaller than the Sun, but it is so much closer that its tidal pull on Earth is about twice as strong.
:::

## Why there are two high tides

The stretch makes two bulges of water: one on the side facing the Moon and one on the far side. Earth turns once a day under these bulges, so most coasts pass through two high tides and two low tides.

The Moon also moves along its orbit while Earth turns. Earth needs about 50 extra minutes to catch up with it, so high tides come about 12 hours and 25 minutes apart, a little later each day.

## How tides work when the Sun joins in

The Sun raises bulges too, about half as big as the Moon's. At new Moon and full Moon, the Sun and Moon line up and their bulges add together. These are spring tides, with the highest highs and lowest lows. At half Moon they pull at right angles, and the tides are smaller. These are neap tides.

## Coasts change the size of the tide

The coast is the last part of how tides work. In the open ocean the tide is less than a metre. Coastlines and the shape of the sea floor make it much bigger in some places. In the Bay of Fundy in Canada, the water can rise about 16 metres between low and high tide.[^2]
