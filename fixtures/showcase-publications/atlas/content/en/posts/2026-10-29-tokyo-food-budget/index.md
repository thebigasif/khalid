A small Tokyo food budget goes a long way, because the city's cheapest food is often its best. A full day of good meals cost one of us about ¥2,800 (March 2026): rice balls for breakfast, a set lunch at a small restaurant, a snack from a food hall and a bowl of ramen at night.

Tokyo has a reputation as an expensive city, and its famous restaurants are. But most people in Tokyo eat every day at places that are quick, cheap and very good, and visitors can do the same. This guide covers where to find them.

::toc

## A day on a Tokyo food budget

These are Asha's own costs on an ordinary weekday in March 2026, rounded. Prices vary by area, and central districts cost a little more.

| Meal | What and where | About |
|---|---|---|
| Breakfast | Two rice balls and tea from a convenience store | ¥450 |
| Lunch | A set lunch of grilled fish, rice, soup and pickles at a small restaurant | ¥1,000 |
| Snack | A cake or a skewer from a department store food hall | ¥350 |
| Dinner | A bowl of ramen at a counter, bought from a ticket machine | ¥1,000 |
| **Total** | | **about ¥2,800** |

Tap water is free in every restaurant, and nobody expects a tip, so the price you see is the price you pay.

## Breakfast: the convenience store

Japan's convenience stores are open day and night on almost every corner, and their food is fresh, cheap and changes with the seasons. They are the backbone of any Tokyo food budget. Rice balls, called [onigiri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Onigiri), are the classic breakfast: a triangle of rice with a filling such as salmon, pickled plum or tuna, wrapped in a sheet of seaweed you add just before eating. They also sell boiled eggs, sandwiches, salads and hot drinks.

## Lunch: the set meal

At lunch, many small restaurants serve a set meal, called a teishoku: a main dish, such as grilled fish or fried pork, with rice, miso soup and pickles. Look for a menu board outside the door and a queue of office workers at noon. Lunch is the best value meal of the day, often costing far less than the same food at dinner.

- **Noodle counters near stations** serve buckwheat noodles hot or cold in a few minutes, often eaten standing up.
- **Rice bowl shops** serve beef or chicken over rice, quickly and very cheaply, at any hour.
- **Curry shops** serve Japanese curry with rice, mild and filling.

:::callout{kind=tip}
At many cheap restaurants, you order and pay at a ticket machine by the door. Press the button for your dish, pay, and hand the ticket to the cook. If the buttons are only in Japanese, match the photo on the machine with the dish you want.
:::

## Afternoon: the food halls

The basements of Tokyo's big department stores hold food halls, called depachika, with hundreds of counters selling cakes, sushi, salads, skewers, pickles and boxed meals. Many offer free tastes. In the hour before closing, many counters cut the prices of the day's fresh food, and a boxed meal can cost much less, which stretches a Tokyo food budget further.

## Dinner: ramen, skewers and supermarkets

- **Ramen** is the classic cheap dinner, and every district has dozens of counters. Most bowls cost about a thousand yen when we went.
- **Grilled chicken skewers** are sold at small bars, often under the railway arches, with a drink.
- **Supermarkets** reduce the price of their ready meals and sushi in the evening, with a sticker on each pack. It is a common way for people in Tokyo to buy dinner after work.

## Habits that keep the bill low

- **Eat your big meal at lunch.** The set lunch costs much less than dinner.
- **Drink water or tea.** Both come free at most restaurants.
- **Shop where people live.** Food near the famous sights costs more than food in the streets behind them.
- **Try the markets.** Food streets and markets, such as the shopping street in Yanaka, sell snacks for a few hundred yen.
