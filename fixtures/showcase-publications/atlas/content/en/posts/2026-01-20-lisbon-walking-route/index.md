This Lisbon walking route covers the city in three days: one hill district a day, with the river as your guide back down. It takes in Alfama and the castle hill on day one, Baixa, Chiado and Bairro Alto on day two, and the long flat riverside walk to Belém on day three, about 25 km in all.

Lisbon is built on steep hills above the Tagus, and its pavements are polished limestone. That makes it beautiful and tiring in equal measure. The trick is to climb in the cool of the morning, walk downhill in the afternoon and let a tram or a lift do the worst of the climbing when your legs say so.

::toc

:::callout{kind=summary}
- **Three days, three areas.** Alfama, then the centre, then the river to Belém.
- **About 25 km in total,** with most of the climbing on day one.
- **Shoes with grip.** The stone pavements are slippery when dry and worse when wet.
- **Climb early, descend late.** The highest viewpoints look west, so the late light is best from the top.
:::

## The Lisbon walking route at a glance

We walked this route twice in late autumn, once on weekdays and once over a weekend. The distances come from our own maps and are rounded.

| Day | Area | Start and finish | Distance | Climbing |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Alfama and the castle hill | Praça do Comércio to Graça | about 7 km | Steep, most of the day |
| 2 | Baixa, Chiado and Bairro Alto | Rossio to the Santa Catarina viewpoint | about 6 km | Two short, steep climbs |
| 3 | The riverside to Belém | Cais do Sodré to the Belém tower | about 12 km | Flat |

## Day one: Alfama and the castle hill

Start at Praça do Comércio, the big square that opens onto the river, and walk east along the water before you turn uphill into Alfama. This is the oldest quarter, and it survived the earthquake of 1755 better than the streets below it, so its lanes still follow a medieval plan. Do not try to follow a map step by step here. Head uphill, keep the river behind you and you will reach the Miradouro de Santa Luzia, a terrace with tiles on its walls and the view in our lead photograph.

From there, climb to the castle, then on to Graça, which sits higher still. The Miradouro da Graça and, a short climb further, the Senhora do Monte viewpoint look west across the whole city. Arrive in the late afternoon and you will see the light turn the roofs orange.

- **Rest stop:** the terraces at the viewpoints sell coffee and pastries, and the benches are free.
- **Easy way down:** tram 28 runs back down through Alfama to the centre. It is crowded in the middle of the day and much calmer early or late.

## Day two: Baixa, Chiado and Bairro Alto

Day two starts flat. Baixa, the grid of streets rebuilt after 1755, runs from Rossio square down to the river, and it is the easiest walking in the city. Walk it end to end once, then turn west and climb into Chiado, the old shopping district with its cafés and bookshops.

:::figure{src="calcada.jpg" alt="Close detail of a Portuguese stone pavement, small hand-laid white and grey limestone blocks set in a pattern of large triangles, the polished stone underfoot all along the Lisbon walking route"}
The hand-laid stone pavement, called calçada, is the first thing you notice in Lisbon. It is lovely to look at and slippery underfoot.
:::

Above Chiado is Bairro Alto, a grid of narrow streets that is quiet by day and loud late at night. Walk through it in the afternoon, then end the day at the Santa Catarina viewpoint, which looks over the river and the red bridge at sunset.

The pavements deserve a word. Lisbon's streets are laid with [Portuguese pavement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portuguese_pavement), small blocks of white and grey stone set by hand. Polished by centuries of feet, they are smooth, and on the steep streets of Chiado they are as slippery as ice after rain.

:::callout{kind=tip}
Wear shoes with a soft rubber sole and real grip. Leather soles and worn trainers both slide on Lisbon's stone, and the steepest streets have handrails for a reason.
:::

## Day three: along the river to Belém

On the third day, rest your knees. From Cais do Sodré, follow the riverside path west towards Belém. It is flat and open, with the river on your left the whole way, and it passes under the long red suspension bridge. At an easy pace with stops, the walk takes most of a morning.

In Belém you will find the monastery, the monument to the discoveries and the small fortified tower on the water. Belém is also where many visitors queue for custard tarts, and the queue moves faster than it looks. Come back to the centre by train or tram, both of which run along the river. It is the easiest day of the Lisbon walking route, and a good one to end on.

## How to make the Lisbon walking route easier

- **Use the lifts and funiculars.** The city's lifts and funiculars were built to carry people up its steepest slopes. Check which are running before you set out, as they close at times for repairs.
- **Carry water in summer.** The hills are hot from June to September, and shade is rare at the viewpoints.
- **Go in spring or autumn.** March to May and late September to November are cooler and less crowded; [our month by month guide to Seville](/blog/best-months-visit-seville/) explains the same pattern further south.
- **Stay central.** A room in Baixa or Chiado puts every day of this Lisbon walking route within a short walk.
