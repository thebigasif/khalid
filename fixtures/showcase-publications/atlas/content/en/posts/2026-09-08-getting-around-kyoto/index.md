The easiest way of getting around Kyoto is to use the trains and the subway for the long trips, walk within each district, and save the city buses for the few places no train reaches. The buses go everywhere, but in the busy seasons they are slow and packed, and a train plus a short walk is often faster.

Kyoto is laid out on a grid, flat in the middle and ringed by hills where many of the temples stand. Most visitors move between a handful of districts: the centre, the old streets of Higashiyama in the east, Arashiyama in the west and the shrine of Fushimi Inari in the south. Knowing which line serves which district saves hours.

## Which way to go where

| To reach | Best way | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Fushimi Inari shrine | JR train or the Keihan line | The station is at the shrine gate |
| Arashiyama | JR train, the Hankyu line or the small tram | Go early, it fills by mid-morning |
| Gion and Higashiyama | Keihan line or walk from the centre | Then walk, the lanes are steep and narrow |
| The north and the Golden Pavilion | Bus, or subway plus a bus | One of the few places a bus is the best choice |
| The Philosopher's Path | Bus, or walk from the subway | Then walk the path itself |
| Central Kyoto | Subway or walk | The centre is flat and easy on foot |

## Getting around Kyoto by train and subway

Kyoto has two subway lines, one running north to south under the main avenue and one running east to west. They are quick, clean and never stuck in traffic. Around them run several railways that reach the edges of the city: the national railway's lines from Kyoto Station, the Keihan line along the east bank of the river, the Hankyu line to the west and a small tram out to Arashiyama.

A rechargeable transit card from Tokyo or Osaka works on all of them and on the buses. Tap in and out, and the fare comes off the card.

## The city buses

The buses reach almost every temple, but they are the weak point of the system. In spring and autumn, the routes to the famous sights fill so full that buses pass stops without stopping. The city has changed its day passes and fares in recent years to move visitors onto the trains, so check the current rules before you plan around a bus pass.

:::callout{kind=tip}
If you must take a bus to a busy sight, board at the start of the route, such as Kyoto Station, and travel before 9am. You will get a seat, and you will reach the sight before the crowds.
:::

## Bicycles

Central Kyoto is flat, and a bike is one of the best ways to see it. Many shops and hotels rent them by the day. Two rules matter: ride on the left, and only park in the paid bicycle parks. Bikes left on the pavement are taken away, and getting one back costs time and money.

Bikes are less useful in the hills of Higashiyama and on the narrow lanes there, which are crowded with people on foot.

## Walking: the best way of getting around Kyoto

Each district is best seen on foot, and some of the city's loveliest walks link one temple to the next.

- **The [Philosopher's Path](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosopher%27s_Walk)** runs for about 2 km beside a canal under cherry trees, between the Silver Pavilion and the temples to the south. Walk it early in the morning. In early April, the cherry blossom makes it one of the most crowded paths in the city.
- **Higashiyama** is a hillside of stone lanes and old wooden shops. Walk from Kiyomizu down through the lanes to Gion.
- **Fushimi Inari** has paths of red gates up the hill behind the shrine. The crowds thin out after the first half hour of climbing.
- **Along the river** in the centre, the banks of the Kamo river make a flat, easy walk at any hour.

## Getting around Kyoto in the busy seasons

The cherry blossom in late March and early April and the autumn colour in November bring Kyoto's biggest crowds. In those weeks, start every day early, take trains rather than buses, and visit the most famous sights first thing or late in the afternoon. Many temples are as lovely, and far quieter, a few streets away from the famous ones.
