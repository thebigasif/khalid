To shop Valencia Central Market like a local, go on a weekday morning before eleven, walk the whole hall once before you buy, and choose what is in season rather than what you planned. The market opens early and closes in the afternoon, so mornings are when it is fullest and freshest.

[Valencia Central Market](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Market_(Valencia)) is one of the great food halls of Europe, a huge building of iron, glass and painted tiles in the old centre of Valencia. It opened in 1928 and still serves the neighbourhood every day. Here is how to make the most of a morning there.

## When to visit Valencia Central Market

The market is open from Monday to Saturday, in the morning and early afternoon, and closed on Sundays. Hours can change on public holidays, so check before you go.

| Time | What it is like |
|---|---|
| 7.30 to 9am | Traders setting up, restaurant cooks buying, very calm |
| 9 to 11am | The best time: full stalls, local shoppers, room to move |
| 11am to 1pm | Busier, with tour groups in the main aisles |
| 1pm to closing | Quieter, some stalls packing up, fewer choices |
| Saturday morning | The busiest time of the week, lively and crowded |

## How the hall is laid out

The building is in two parts. The large main hall, under the central dome, holds most of the stalls: fruit and vegetables, meat and cured ham, bread, cheese, nuts and dried fruit, and food to eat now. A second, smaller hall to one side holds the fish and seafood, kept apart for the smell and the ice.

Walk the whole market once before you buy anything. Prices and quality vary from stall to stall, and the best stalls are not always the ones by the doors.

## What to buy

- **Seasonal vegetables.** In winter and spring, look for artichokes, as in our lead photograph, broad beans and tender garlic shoots.
- **Oranges and lemons.** Valencia's oranges are at their best from about December to April.
- **Nuts and dried fruit.** Almonds, figs and dates, sold by weight.
- **Cured ham and sausage.** Ask for it sliced thin, and ask for a taste first.
- **Paella rice.** Short, round rice from the lagoon south of the city, sold in cloth bags.

:::callout{kind=tip}
Most stalls sell by weight, and you can buy as little as you like. Point and ask for a hundred grams; nobody will mind. Bring a cloth bag and small change.
:::

## Buying for a paella

Paella was born in the countryside around Valencia, cooked over a wood fire in a wide, shallow pan. The traditional Valencian version uses rabbit and chicken, two kinds of flat green bean, rice, olive oil, tomato and saffron. There is no seafood and no chorizo in it.

You can buy every ingredient in the market in one morning. Ask the vegetable stalls for the beans used for paella, and they will know what you mean.

A paella cooking over a wood fire near Valencia. In the city, people eat paella at lunch, never at dinner, and often on Sundays.

## Eating in and around Valencia Central Market

The market has a few counters where you can eat and drink, from fresh juice to small plates. Across the square stands the old silk exchange, a late Gothic hall that is now a museum and a World Heritage site. The streets behind it are full of small bars for lunch.

For something sweet, try horchata, a cold, milky drink made from tiger nuts, with long, soft sugared buns called fartons for dipping. It is the city's own summer drink.

## Two more markets worth a visit

- **The Ruzafa market,** in a lively neighbourhood south of the centre, is a working local market in a colourful modern building.
- **The Colón market** is a beautiful modernist hall that now holds cafés and restaurants rather than stalls. Come for a coffee, not for shopping.
