The best way to see Montreal neighbourhoods is a one-day walk north from Mount Royal through the Plateau and Mile End to Little Italy, ending at the Jean-Talon market. It is about 7 km on flat streets after the first hill, and it passes the city's outdoor staircases, two famous bagel bakeries and its biggest food market.

Montreal is a city of neighbourhoods, each with its own churches, parks, cafés and accent. This route links four of them in a line, so you never need to double back.

## Four Montreal neighbourhoods at a glance

| Stage | From and to | About | What you see |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Mount Royal lookout to Parc La Fontaine | 3 km | The view, the park, the Plateau's side streets |
| 2 | Parc La Fontaine to Mile End | 2 km | Outdoor staircases, murals, cafés |
| 3 | Mile End to Little Italy | 2 km | Bagel bakeries, rail bridge, small shops |
| 4 | The Jean-Talon market | 0.5 km | Lunch and a picnic for later |

## Stage one: Mount Royal and down into the Plateau

Start at the lookout on [Mount Royal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Royal), the hill that gives the city its name, for a view over downtown and the river. The park was laid out in the 1870s by the same landscape architect who designed Central Park in New York. Walk down its east side and cross into the Plateau.

## Stage two: the Plateau's staircases

The Plateau is the heart of the city's French-speaking life, a grid of two- and three-storey brick and stone houses, each with a door for every flat. Many of the upper flats are reached by iron staircases on the outside of the building, which curve and twist up the front walls. They were built that way to keep the space inside for living. In winter, residents clear them of snow, and in summer they are where people sit with a coffee.

Walk the side streets between the big avenues to see them at their best, then stop at Parc La Fontaine, with its pond and old trees.

:::callout{kind=tip}
The staircases are private homes. Photograph them from the pavement, and do not climb them or sit on the steps.
:::

## Stage three: Mile End

North of the Plateau, Mile End is smaller and quieter than the other Montreal neighbourhoods on this walk, with a long history as the first stop for new arrivals in the city. Its two famous bagel bakeries are a few streets apart and open around the clock. Montreal bagels are smaller, thinner and sweeter than New York ones, boiled in honey water and baked in a wood-fired oven. Buy one hot, and eat it on the pavement.

## Stage four: Little Italy and the Jean-Talon market

Cross under the railway into Little Italy, with its espresso bars and its church, and finish at the [Jean-Talon market](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Talon_Market), one of the largest open-air markets in North America. It is open all year, with the stalls closed in by walls in winter. In summer and early autumn, the farmers' stalls overflow with Quebec produce: berries, corn, tomatoes, apples and maple syrup.

:::figure{src="berries.jpg" alt="Rows of small boxes of strawberries, raspberries, blueberries, blackberries, cherry tomatoes and ground cherries, packed tight on a market stall at the Jean-Talon market, the last stop on our walk through Montreal neighbourhoods"}
Berries at the Jean-Talon market in late summer. Buy a box for the walk home, and taste before you buy: most stalls are happy to offer one.
:::

## Getting back

From the market, the Jean-Talon metro station is a short walk away, and two metro lines meet there. The ride back to downtown takes about a quarter of an hour.

## When to walk Montreal neighbourhoods

- **Late May to October** is the walking season, with café tables out and parks in use.
- **September and early October** bring autumn colour to Mount Royal and the best of the market.
- **Winter** is cold and snowy, often far below freezing. The walk is still possible, but shorter days and icy pavements make the metro and the underground city more pleasant.
