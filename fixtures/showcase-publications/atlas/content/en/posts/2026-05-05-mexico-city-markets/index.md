To eat well in Mexico City markets, go at lunchtime, sit at a busy counter in the food section and order the set lunch or whatever the people beside you are eating. Every neighbourhood has a covered market, and most have a row of small kitchens at the back that cook breakfast and lunch for the traders and the street.

Mexico City has hundreds of public markets, and they are the best and cheapest places to eat in the city. This guide covers how they work, five worth crossing town for, and what to order once you sit down.

::toc

## How Mexico City markets work

Most neighbourhood markets have the same plan. The front is fruit and vegetables, then meat, fish and cheese, then dry goods and household things. At the back or along one side, there is a row of small kitchens, called fondas, each with a counter, a few stools and a cook at a griddle or a pot.

- **Arrive between 1pm and 3pm** for the main meal of the day, which in Mexico is lunch.
- **Look for the set lunch.** Many fondas serve a fixed menu of soup, rice, a main dish and a drink for one low price.
- **Sit where it is busy.** A crowded counter means fresh food and fast turnover.
- **Carry small notes and coins.** Most stalls take cash only, and few can change a large note.

:::callout{kind=note}
A set lunch at a market fonda cost us about the price of a coffee and a cake at a smart café, when we went in the spring of 2026. It is one of the best value meals in any big city we have costed.
:::

## Five Mexico City markets worth the trip

| Market | Area | Go for | Best time |
|---|---|---|---|
| La Merced | East of the historic centre | Size, chillies, dried goods | Weekday mornings |
| San Juan | Historic centre | Cheese, rare meats, specialist produce | Late morning |
| Medellín | Roma | Fruit, Latin American groceries, lunch | Lunchtime |
| Coyoacán | Coyoacán | Tostadas, a relaxed weekend | Weekend lunch |
| Jamaica | Jamaica | Flowers, fruit, quesadillas | Early morning |

### La Merced

[La Merced](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Merced_Market) is one of the largest traditional markets in the Americas, a district of halls and streets east of the historic centre. Come for the scale: whole aisles of dried chillies, mountains of fruit, sacks of beans. It is busy and easy to get lost in, so go in the morning, keep your bag in front of you and agree a place to meet if you lose your group.

### San Juan

San Juan, near the historic centre, is where chefs buy unusual ingredients. It is small, tidy and more expensive than other markets, with stalls of cheese, cured meat and produce you will not see elsewhere.

### Medellín

The Medellín market in Roma is our favourite for an easy first visit. It is clean, friendly and full of fruit, with stalls selling groceries from across Latin America and a good row of kitchens for lunch.

### Coyoacán and Jamaica

Coyoacán's market, in the south of the city, is known for its tostadas, piled high and eaten standing up, and it makes a good lunch stop on a weekend in that neighbourhood. Jamaica is the city's great flower market, open through the night before big holidays, with fruit and food stalls alongside.

## What to order at a market counter

- **Set lunch:** a soup, rice, a main dish with tortillas and a jug of fruit water.
- **Quesadillas:** made to order on the griddle, with a choice of fillings.
- **Tostadas:** crisp tortillas piled with toppings, a speciality at Coyoacán.
- **Fresh juice and cut fruit:** sold in cups, often with lime and chilli powder, as in our lead photograph.
- **Tacos:** found at most markets, especially in the morning.

## Staying well

Most visitors eat in Mexico City markets without trouble. Choose busy stalls, eat food that is cooked in front of you, and be careful with uncooked salads and ice at first if your stomach is new to the city. Drink bottled or filtered water. Mexico City is about 2,240 m above sea level, so give yourself a slow first day.
