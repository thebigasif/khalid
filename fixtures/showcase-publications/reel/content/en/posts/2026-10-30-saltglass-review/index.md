This *Saltglass* review starts with a warning: the film takes its time. For its first half hour, almost nothing happens. A keeper and her brother paint a lighthouse, mend a radio and argue about the weather. Then a storm arrives, and so does a stranger, and the film you thought was slow turns out to have been loading every scene.

Maren Oduya wrote and directed it, her second feature after a run of short films. She shot it on a real headland over two winters, and you can feel the cold in every frame. *Saltglass* is in cinemas now, and it deserves the biggest screen you can find.

## What Saltglass is about

Ilse Marrow plays Eda, who has kept the light since her father died. Teodor Vance plays her younger brother, Sander, who wants to sell up and leave. Hanne Ostrova is the stranger, a sailor pulled from the rocks on the night of the storm, who will not say where her boat came from.

The plot is simple. The film's interest lies in what the three of them do not say to each other, and in how long Oduya is willing to wait before anyone says it.

## The performances

Marrow is extraordinary. She has very few lines, and she makes each one land like a slammed door. Vance is warmer and looser, and the scenes between the siblings feel lived in. Ostrova has the hardest part, since the script keeps her a mystery for most of the film, but she finds a dry humour in it that stops the stranger from becoming a device.

## Our Saltglass verdict

:::review{item="Saltglass" type="Movie" rating="4.5" best="5" author="Nadia Brennan"}
Saltglass is a slow, patient lighthouse drama that rewards anyone willing to meet it halfway. Ilse Marrow gives the performance of the year, the storm sounds astonishing in a cinema, and the near silent final ten minutes are as good as anything on screen this autumn. One late scene explains too much.
:::

The pace will test some viewers, so go when you are rested. The sound of the storm alone is worth the ticket, and the film deserves the biggest screen near you.
