A great opening scene does one job above all: it teaches you how to watch the film. In the first five minutes, a good director tells you how fast the story will move, how much you will be told, and what kind of attention the film wants. I spent ten years cutting documentaries, and the opening was always the scene we rebuilt most often.

Here is what I look for, with examples from films we reviewed this year.

## A great opening scene sets the speed

The opening of [the lighthouse drama Saltglass](/blog/saltglass-review/) is four minutes of a woman painting a lighthouse rail. Nothing happens, and that is the point. By the time the storm arrives, you have learned to watch slowly, so every small change feels huge.

[Night Ferry](/blog/night-ferry-review/) does the opposite. Its first shot is a running man, and the camera never really stops. Both openings work, because each makes a promise the film keeps.

## It trusts the audience

The weakest openings explain. A voice tells you the year, the place and the stakes, and the film begins with nothing left to discover. The best openings drop you into the middle of something and let you catch up.

[The Quiet Orbit](/blog/quiet-orbit-review/) opens on two engineers mid-argument about a bolt. We do not know where they are or why the bolt matters. Within a minute, we know them both, and we want to know more.

## It plants something for later

A great opening scene often hides an object, a sound or a line that pays off at the end. In [Paper Kingdoms](/blog/paper-kingdoms-review/), the first sound is the mill's machines. The last sound is the same room in silence. That echo is the whole film in two shots.

## A test you can try

Next time you start a film, stop it after five minutes and ask yourself three questions. How fast is this film? How much will it tell me? What does it want me to notice? If you can answer all three, the opening has done its job. For a longer history of the craft, read about [film editing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Film_editing).
