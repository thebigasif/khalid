My Paper Kingdoms review could be one line: it is the best film about work I have seen this year. Hana Petrov spent twelve months in a mill town in the north, from the day the closure was announced to the day the last roll of paper left the yard. The result is patient, warm and quietly furious.

*Paper Kingdoms* is streaming now on Halcyon Play.

## Who Paper Kingdoms follows

Petrov chose four people and stayed with them. There is a machine operator who has worked at the mill for thirty-one years, his daughter who works in the office, a young apprentice who only started in spring, and the manager who has to tell them all. Nobody narrates. Petrov lets each of them talk, and then lets the silences run.

The access is remarkable. We see the meeting where the closure is announced, and the long walk back to the machines afterwards. Petrov never pushes for tears. She simply waits, and the people trust her enough to let her see.

## How it is made

As a former editor, I watched the cut closely, and it is very fine work. The film moves through the seasons without title cards. The noise of the machines fills the first half, so their silence in the second half hits hard. One shot of the empty mill floor, held for nearly a minute, says more than any interview.

## The one weakness

The film says little about why the mill closed. A short scene with the owners, or even a letter, would have given the anger in the town a target. As it is, the cause stays off screen.

## Our Paper Kingdoms verdict

:::review{item="Paper Kingdoms" type="Movie" rating="4" best="5" author="Ines Varga"}
Paper Kingdoms is a patient, warm and quietly angry documentary about a mill town's last year with its paper mill. Hana Petrov's access is remarkable, and the edit is very fine work. It says too little about why the mill closed, but it is the best film about work this year.
