The review of The Quiet Orbit you need is short: watch it with the lights off and the sound up. Kasper Holm's film is a space drama with almost no action, two main characters and one problem. It is also the tensest thing on Lanternbox this month.

Priya Castell and Teodor Vance play the last two engineers on a research station whose orbit is slowly failing. The crew has gone home. The pair have nine days to fix the station or bring it down safely, and they disagree about which.

## What works

Holm cares about how things work. Every repair takes as long as it would, and every tool has a weight. That patience makes the danger feel real. When a panel will not open, you hold your breath with them.

Castell is the film's centre. Her engineer is calm, funny and tired, and you believe she has done this job for twenty years. Vance, in a very different part from [his part in Saltglass](/blog/saltglass-review/), plays the younger engineer as a man trying hard not to panic. Lev Aramyan has a small, warm part as the voice from mission control.

The pictures are beautiful, but they never show off. Most of the film happens inside the station, in grey light, so the few shots of the stars outside land with real force.

## Where the film drags

At 131 minutes, the film is long. A middle section about a lost radio signal goes nowhere, and could have gone. The score is lovely, but it explains feelings the actors have already shown.

## Our verdict on The Quiet Orbit

:::review{item="The Quiet Orbit" type="Movie" rating="4" best="5" author="Nadia Brennan"}
The Quiet Orbit is a grown-up space drama about work, trust and doing a hard job well. Priya Castell is superb as a tired, funny engineer, and every repair feels real. A dead-end middle section and an eager score keep it from five stars, but it is the tensest film on Lanternbox this month.
:::

It belongs beside the best [hard science fiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hard_science_fiction) on screen. Watch it at night, on the biggest screen in your home.
