Annie Pascoe grew the same broad beans on the same allotment for sixty years. When she gave up her plot she gave us a cake tin of seed and a page of notes in her neat hand. Annie died last winter, at ninety-one. Still, her beans are in the tin, and this spring forty growers will sow them.

## What Annie knew about broad beans

Annie sowed in November, under a cloche, and then again in March. She pinched out the tips as soon as the first pods set, because that keeps the black fly away. She never sold a bean. Instead she gave them to anyone who asked, with one rule: grow them where nobody else grows the same crop, so they stay true.

## Growing broad beans in a cold spring

If the ground is cold and wet, start the seed in deep pots on a windowsill and plant out in April. Sow each one five centimetres deep. These beans cross with each other easily, so keep different kinds apart if you want to save seed. For example, Tomasz grows one kind on his plot and a second one at a friend's farm, half a mile away. [Wikipedia's page on the broad bean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vicia_faba) also covers their long history.

## From the tin: Annie's Longpod

Annie's Longpod has long, drooping pods with up to nine beans each. Its pale green bean keeps its colour when cooked, while most kinds fade to grey. We have grown it out every few years since she gave it to us, and it has never let us down. Next autumn we hope to fill a second jar.
