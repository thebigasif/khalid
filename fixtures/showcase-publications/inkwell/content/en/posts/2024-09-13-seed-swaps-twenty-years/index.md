Our seed swaps are twenty years old this autumn. The Penhallow Seed Library began with one biscuit tin of beans and a notice in the post office window. Today it holds about four hundred varieties, and more than three hundred households borrow from it.

## How our seed swaps began

The first swap was a trestle table in the chapel porch and eleven people. We swapped beans for beans and wrote the names on brown envelopes. Nobody kept a list, so by the third year we had lost track of half of it. After that we kept a list, a second tin and a rule that still stands: take what you need, and bring back what you can.

## Why seed swaps work

Swaps work because seed is generous. For example, one bean plant left to ripen gives back fifty beans, and one sunflower head gives back a thousand seeds. A library of seed therefore needs only a few growers each year to keep every variety alive. [Wikipedia's page on seed libraries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seed_library) lists others like ours, from small towns to big cities.

## Growing: drying seed heads without the birds

Tomasz ties a paper bag over each sunflower head once the back turns yellow. The bag keeps the birds off, and it also catches the seed that falls. Then cut the head with a long stalk and hang it in a dry shed for three weeks. Only after that should you rub the seed out, since damp seed goes mouldy in its envelope.

## From the tin: Cornish Gold

Cornish Gold is a short, tough sunflower bred by a farmer on the cliffs above Penhallow. It stands up to the wind that flattens taller kinds. Its seed is small and striped, and the bees love it, so plant it where you can watch them.
