Every summer someone tells us they have too many green beans. It is the best problem a grower can have, so this issue is about it. Pick often and the plants keep flowering. Stop picking, however, and they give up within a week. The trick is to do both: pick most of the row hard, and then leave a few plants alone for seed.

## Too many green beans, and what to do with them

Pick every two or three days while the pods are thin and snap cleanly. Blanch what you cannot eat, then freeze it flat on a tray so the pods do not stick together. Give the rest away at the gate. A full bucket in July is the reward for a cold April spent watching the soil.

Next, choose three or four strong plants at the end of the row and stop picking them. Their pods will swell, turn pale and dry to paper by September. Those are the pods we want back in the tin, because seed from the healthiest plants keeps the variety strong.

## Growing green beans you can save seed from

Tomasz picks his beans before the seeds show as bumps in the pod. Once a plant has seed to ripen, it puts its strength there and sets fewer flowers. In a dry spell, also give the roots a mulch of grass cuttings to keep them cool. Climbing kinds rarely cross with each other, so you can save seed from two kinds in one garden. You can read more about the plant on [Wikipedia's page on the green bean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_bean).

## From the tin: Penhallow Purple

Penhallow Purple came to the library in a jam jar, labelled 1952 in pencil. It climbs to two metres and has violet flowers. Its pods are purple but turn green when cooked, which still surprises children at the open day. It was the bean on the front of our first issue, and we have more requests for it than seed.
