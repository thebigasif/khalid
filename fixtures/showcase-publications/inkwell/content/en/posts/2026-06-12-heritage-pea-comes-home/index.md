In January a letter came from Nova Scotia with twenty dried peas taped inside. The writer's grandmother, Gwenna, had left Penhallow in 1968 and taken a twist of seed with her. Her family grew this heritage pea every year since. Then her granddaughter read about the library and sent some home.

## A heritage pea lost and found

Nobody in Penhallow still grew it. The old growers remembered a tall pea with purple flowers and wrinkled seed that grew on the farms above the harbour. It had gone, the way old seed goes when one grower stops and no one takes it up.

We sowed all twenty in the new greenhouse in March. Nineteen came up. Now they stand two metres tall on hazel sticks behind the chapel, purple flowered, just as the old growers said.

## Growing: letting pods dry on the plant

To save seed, stop picking a few plants once they have set pods. Then let the pods dry on the plant until they rattle. If rain is coming, pull the whole plant and hang it upside down in a shed instead. Finally, shell the peas and dry them for another week before you store them. Peas rarely cross, because each flower pollinates itself before it opens, so even a small garden can keep a heritage pea true. [Wikipedia's pea page](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pea) explains why.

## From the tin: Gwenna's Pea

We have named it after the woman who carried it across an ocean. Its sweet, wrinkled peas are best eaten young, while the pods are still bright green. This year we will save every pod and lend the seed out next spring. In the meantime, the family in Nova Scotia has asked for a photo of the row.
