Lampwork glass is made at a bench, not a furnace. Petra, who runs a small studio behind a bike shop, sits at a torch with a rod of clear glass in each hand and turns them in the flame until they soften like honey. Then she breathes into one end, slowly, and a bubble opens inside.

## The studio

The studio is one room with a long bench, two torches, a kiln in the corner and a fan that never stops. Glass rods stand in jars by colour, like pencils. Petra makes beads, small bottles and the odd glass whistle for a toy shop in town. Everything she makes goes into the kiln afterwards to cool slowly overnight, or it cracks.

She learned from her uncle, who made laboratory glass for a university for forty years. You can read about [lampworking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lampworking) and how it grew out of making scientific glassware.

## The bench torch

The tool this week is the bench torch, which mixes gas and oxygen into a flame hot enough to melt glass in seconds. Petra works in the soft blue tip of the flame, not the bright core. Too far in and the glass boils; too far out and it cools before she can shape it.

Turning is the whole skill. Glass in the flame wants to sag, so it never stops moving. Petra turns both rods at the same speed, all day, without looking at her hands. I tried for ten minutes and made a lump. Petra keeps her notes on [paper made one sheet at a time](/blog/paper-one-sheet-at-a-time/), which survives a stray spark better than a notebook.

## Lampwork glass and patience

Every piece of lampwork glass takes longer than you think. A plain bead is ten minutes at the torch and a night in the kiln. A bottle is an afternoon. Petra says the breath is the hardest part to teach: too fast and the wall splits, too slow and nothing happens.

## Letters

A reader asked whether a cracked glass jug can be mended. It can be glued for display, but it will never hold hot water again. Last month's issue on [visible mending](/blog/visible-mending/) has better luck with cloth.
