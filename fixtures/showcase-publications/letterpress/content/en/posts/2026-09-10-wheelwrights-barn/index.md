A wheelwright makes wheels, and almost nobody needs one any more. Henrik does it anyway, in a timber barn at the end of a farm track, for museums, carriage drivers and the odd wedding. His barn smells of oak shavings and hot iron.

## The barn

Iron tyres hang from the beams like giant rings. Spokes stand in bundles by the door, drying for a year before they are used. A wheel takes Henrik about three weeks: the hub from elm, the spokes from oak, the rim from ash, and an iron tyre around the outside.

The tyre is the dramatic part. It is heated in a fire until it glows, dropped over the wooden wheel and cooled with buckets of water. As it shrinks, it pulls every joint tight. You can read about [the wheelwright's trade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wheelwright) and how the tyre is fitted.

## The spokeshave

The tool this week is the spokeshave, a short blade held between two handles. You pull it towards you along the wood, taking off thin curls. Henrik shapes every spoke with one, checking it by eye against the last.

Henrik reads The Proof Sheet in print, one of the copies we explain in [why this letter is printed first](/blog/why-this-letter-is-printed/). He says a spokeshave tells you about the wood. Pull with the grain and it sings. Pull against it and it tears. Beginners fight the wood; wheelwrights listen to it.

## Why a wheelwright still matters

Henrik says a wheelwright is also a joiner, a blacksmith and a geometry teacher. A wheel is not flat: it is dished, like a shallow bowl, so it stays strong as a cart sways. Getting the dish right takes years, and he still checks every wheel twice.

## Letters

A reader asked where to buy green oak for a first project. Ask a local sawmill for offcuts, and say what you are making. Most will sell you a few pieces. The [winter workshop list](/blog/winter-workshop-list/) has a spoon carving day that uses green wood too.
