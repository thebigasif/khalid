Visible mending is the opposite of hiding a repair. Lena, a tailor with a narrow shop by the station, darns holes in bright wool and sews patches on the outside. A worn knee becomes a square of blue. A moth hole becomes a small star.

## The shop

Lena first wrote to us after reading [what a potter learns in a year at the wheel](/blog/a-year-at-the-wheel/). She said mending is the same: the first year is all holes.

Lena's shop is about two metres wide. A sewing machine sits in the window, and a rail of mended coats waits for their owners. She takes in alterations to pay the rent, but her favourite work is mending: clothes people love too much to throw away.

She keeps her supplies in an old biscuit tin that belonged to her grandmother. Ribbons, tapes, needles and a ball of wool in every colour she has ever needed. The tin is the first thing she would save in a fire.

## The darning mushroom

The tool this week is the darning mushroom, a wooden dome on a handle. You slide it inside a sock, under the hole, so the cloth is stretched smooth while you work. You can read about [darning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darning) and how the weave is built up thread by thread.

Lena darns in two directions. First she runs threads up and down across the hole. Then she weaves across them, over and under, until the hole is a small patch of new cloth. Done well, it is stronger than the sock around it.

## Why visible mending

Lena says visible mending is honest. A hidden repair pretends nothing happened. A visible one shows that someone cared enough to fix it. Her customers agree: most of them now ask for a bright colour on purpose.

## Letters

A reader asked what to do with odd socks. Lena's answer: darn the holes in a colour that matches the other odd sock, and wear them as a pair. The [winter workshop list](/blog/winter-workshop-list/) has a dyeing day if you want to change their colour too.
