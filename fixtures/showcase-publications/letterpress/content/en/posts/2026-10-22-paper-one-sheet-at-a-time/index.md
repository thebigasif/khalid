Handmade paper starts as old shirts. At the small mill Tobias visited this week, cotton rags are cut into squares, soaked and beaten for hours until they fall apart into a thin grey soup. Every sheet the mill sells began in that tub.

## The mill

The mill sits on a stream that once turned its beater. Now the beater runs on electricity, but the rest of the work has not changed in two hundred years. Two people make about four hundred sheets on a good day. On a wet day the drying yard fills up and they stop early.

The sheets dry hanging on lines in the open air, which gives them a soft, uneven surface printers love. You can read more about the [history of papermaking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papermaking) and how rag paper was replaced by wood pulp in the nineteenth century.

## The mould and deckle

The tool this week is the mould and deckle. The mould is a wooden frame with a fine mesh across it. The deckle is a loose frame that sits on top and keeps the pulp from running off the edges. The papermaker dips both into the tub, lifts them out level and gives a quick shake from side to side.

That shake is the skill. It knits the fibres together so the sheet is strong in every direction. Too hard and the sheet is thin in the middle; too gentle and it tears when you lift it.

## Why handmade paper matters to printers

Handmade paper is soft enough to take a deep impression from a press, and strong enough not to tear when it does. Ada, whose shop we visited for [our fiftieth issue](/blog/fifty-issues-set-by-hand/), buys her wedding stock from this mill. Petra, the glass maker from [our issue on glass](/blog/glass-heat-and-breath/), wraps every bead she sends in a scrap of it.

## Letters

A reader asked which paper takes ink best. For a hand press, a soft cotton paper, slightly damp. For a fountain pen, something harder and smoother, or the ink spreads into the fibres.
