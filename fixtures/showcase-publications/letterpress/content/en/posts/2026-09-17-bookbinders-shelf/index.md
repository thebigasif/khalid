Every bookbinder keeps a shelf of books they will never sell. Ruth, who runs a bindery above a secondhand bookshop, keeps hers by the window: twelve old leather books, each rebound by her, each with a story about what was wrong with it when it arrived.

## The bindery

The bindery is one bright room with a press, a guillotine and a long table covered in paper offcuts. Ruth rebinds family bibles, old cookbooks and the odd school register. Most of her work comes from the shop downstairs, which sends up any book too loved to sell as it is.

She says a book tells you how it was read. A cookbook falls open at the cake recipes. A bible is worn at the edges where thumbs turned the pages. She rebinds them so they still fall open in the same places.

## The bone folder

The tool this week is the bone folder, a flat smooth blade once made from bone and now often from plastic. Ruth uses it to crease paper sharply, to smooth glue under leather and to rub down the edges of a new cover. You can read about [bookbinding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bookbinding) and the tools a binder uses.

Her own folder is forty years old and has worn into the shape of her hand. Jonas, from [our carving issue](/blog/carving-by-lamplight/), made her a new handle for it last spring. She lends out the plastic ones and keeps the bone one in her apron pocket.

## How a bookbinder thinks

A bookbinder thinks in layers: the paper, the thread, the spine, the boards and the cover. A repair that ignores one layer fails within a year. Ruth always starts by taking the old binding apart, carefully, to see how the first binder did it.

## Letters

A reader asked how to save a book with a loose spine. Do not use tape. Keep it closed, wrap it in clean paper and take it to a binder. The [handmade paper](/blog/paper-one-sheet-at-a-time/) mill sells the kind of paper binders use for repairs.
