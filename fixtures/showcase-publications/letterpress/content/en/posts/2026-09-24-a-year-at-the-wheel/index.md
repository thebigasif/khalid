Sam has spent eleven years at the wheel, and her first year at the wheel still makes her laugh. She still keeps her first bowl on a shelf above the sink. It leans to one side and is too heavy to lift with one hand. She says it reminds her how far she has come, and how much further there is.

## The pottery

The pottery is in an old dairy at the edge of a village. The cold stone floor keeps the clay damp, which is why dairies make good potteries. Shelves of drying pots line every wall, waiting for the kiln. Sam fires once a fortnight, and the kiln takes a whole day to cool.

She sells mugs, bowls and plates to two shops and a restaurant. Each restaurant plate is thrown by hand, so no two are quite the same. The chef says that is the point.

## The kick wheel

The tool this week is the kick wheel, a heavy stone flywheel turned by the potter's foot. Sam has an electric wheel too, but she teaches on the kick wheel because it makes you feel the speed. You can read about [the potter's wheel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potter%27s_wheel) and how the kick wheel came before the electric one.

## What a year at the wheel teaches

The first thing the wheel teaches is centring: pressing a lump of clay into the exact middle of the wheel so it spins without a wobble. Every beginner spends weeks on it. Sam says most people quit here, and the ones who stay become potters.

The second thing is letting go. A pot that is nearly right is a pot you ruin by touching it once more. Knowing when to stop takes years.

## Letters

A reader asked whether you can work with clay at home without a kiln. Yes: air dry clay is fine for practice, though it will never hold water. Sam's evening classes are on the [winter workshop list](/blog/winter-workshop-list/) if you want to fire something real.
