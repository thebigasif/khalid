Carving is quiet work. Jonas works in an attic above a hardware shop, under one lamp, cutting patterns into wood blocks for printers and into leather for a bag maker down the road. He starts when the shop closes and stops when his eyes tell him to.

## The attic

The attic is long and low, with a bench under the window and a rack of tools on the wall. Every tool has its own slot. Jonas says that is not tidiness but safety: a sharp gouge left on the bench ends up in a hand. Shavings cover the floor, and a broom stands by the stairs.

Most of his work is for printers. A wood block for a poster is carved backwards, so it prints the right way round. You can read about [woodcut printing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woodcut) and why blocks are cut along the grain.

## The V gouge

The tool this week is the V gouge, a small chisel with a blade bent into a narrow V. It cuts a clean line with two walls, which is exactly what a printing block needs. Jonas owns eleven of them in different sizes and uses two.

He pushes the gouge with his right hand and steers it with his left thumb on the blade. The left hand never leaves the tool. That is the rule he teaches first, before anything about patterns.

## Carving for printers

Carving a block for a printer is a slow business. A poster heading might take three evenings. Then Ada, the printer from [our fiftieth issue](/blog/fifty-issues-set-by-hand/), pulls one proof and finds the one letter that needs another hour. Jonas does not mind. He says the proof is the only honest critic he has.

## Letters

Several readers asked how to keep carving tools sharp. Jonas strops his gouges on a strip of leather with polishing paste every twenty minutes. He sharpens on a stone only when stropping stops working, about once a month.
