A landscape needs depth: the feeling that some things are near and others far away. Watercolour gives you two simple tools for it, colour and edges. This lesson uses both to paint trees in front and distant hills behind them.

## Distant hills are pale and cool

Air between you and a hill makes it paler and bluer. Painters call this [aerial perspective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aerial_perspective). Paint distant hills with a weak, cool mix: blue with a little red and plenty of water. Keep the shapes simple, with no detail at all.

## Near is strong and warm

Things close to you are darker and warmer, with more detail. Paint the nearest trees with the warm greens from [mixing greens](/blog/mixing-greens/) and a little more red. Add their darks last.

## Edges

Far things have soft edges, so paint them wet in wet. Near things have sharp edges, so paint them on dry paper. This one rule does as much for depth as colour does.

## A tree in four marks

1. Paint the shape of the whole tree as one light green mass.
2. While it is damp, drop a darker green into the shadow side.
3. When it is dry, add a few dark marks for gaps between branches.
4. Paint the trunk with the small brush, darker where it meets the leaves.

Use the flicks and dry strokes from [your first marks](/blog/first-brush-marks/) for grass and leaves at the edges.

## The exercise

On a half sheet, paint three bands of hills, each one darker and warmer than the one behind it. Then paint one tree in front, using the four marks. Step back across the room and look. If the distant hills sit behind the tree, it worked. If they jump forward, paint them again paler and bluer, with softer edges.

## Why distant hills matter

Most landscapes have three layers: something near, something in the middle and distant hills or sky at the back. When each layer is a step paler and cooler than the one in front, the eye reads depth at once, even in a painting the size of a postcard.
