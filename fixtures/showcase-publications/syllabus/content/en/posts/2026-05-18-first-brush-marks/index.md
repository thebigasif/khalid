Most beginners hold a brush like a pen, close to the tip, and press hard. The marks come out stiff and small. This lesson loosens your hand with simple brush marks before we paint any wash.

## How to hold the brush

Hold the brush about halfway up the handle, more lightly than feels natural. For broad strokes, hold it further back still. Move from the elbow and the shoulder, not just the fingers. Your marks will grow longer and smoother at once.

## How much water

A brush can be dry, damp or loaded. A loaded brush holds a drop at its tip. Most watercolour work uses a loaded brush. If your paint looks dull and scratchy, you need more water. If it floods and runs, touch the brush to kitchen paper to take some off.

## Five brush marks to practise

1. **A line:** touch the tip down and pull. Keep the pressure even.
2. **A swelling stroke:** start on the tip, press down to the belly, then lift back to the tip.
3. **A dot:** touch and lift straight up.
4. **A dry stroke:** take most water off and drag the brush on its side. The grain of the paper breaks it up.
5. **A flick:** a quick stroke that lifts as it goes, good for grass.

## The exercise

Fill a sheet of scrap paper with rows of each mark, in one colour. Do not paint anything real yet. When the sheet is full, turn it over and do it again in another colour. Twenty minutes of this is worth more than an hour of careful painting.

## What good brush marks look like

Look for brush marks with clean edges and an even flow. A mark that looks sure was made quickly. If you hesitate, the paint pools. Keep the sheet; you will meet these marks again when you paint [grass and trees](/blog/trees-distant-hills/).

Painters have made the same brush marks for centuries; the [Wikipedia page on the paintbrush](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paintbrush) shows how the round brush is built. This is the last lesson of the first module. Next comes [the flat wash](/blog/flat-wash/), the first real watercolour skill.
