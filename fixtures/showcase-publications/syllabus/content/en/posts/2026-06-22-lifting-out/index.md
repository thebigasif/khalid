People say watercolour cannot be corrected. That is only half true. On good paper you can take a lot of colour back out. Lifting out fixes mistakes, and it is also a way to paint light shapes, such as clouds or a sunlit edge.

## Lifting out while the wash is wet

While a wash is still wet, press a crumpled tissue into it and lift. The tissue takes the colour with it and leaves a soft pale shape. This is the quickest way to paint a cloud in a blue sky.

## Once the paint is dry

When the wash is dry, wet the shape you want to lighten with a clean, damp brush. Count to five. Then dab it with kitchen paper. Repeat until it is light enough. A small, stiff brush gives sharp edges; a soft brush gives soft ones.

## Which colours allow lifting out

Some paints stain the paper and hardly lift at all. Others sit on top and lift almost to white. Ultramarine lifts well. Many reds and some greens stain. Test your three colours on a scrap of the same paper before you rely on lifting.

## What the masters did

Look at the watercolours of [Winslow Homer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winslow_Homer). The white shirts and the light on the rocks are often lifted or scraped out, not left white from the start. He painted boldly because he knew he could take paint away.

## The exercise

Paint a flat blue wash. While it is wet, lift three clouds with a tissue. When it is dry, lift a thin horizon line with a damp brush and a ruler. Compare the soft wet lifts with the sharp dry one.

## When to stop

Lifting out too often wears through the paper surface. It goes fluffy and takes paint badly. Two or three lifts on one spot is the limit on cotton paper, and fewer on cellulose.
