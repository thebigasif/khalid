A sky is where watercolour shines. Painting skies brings together the [graded wash](/blog/graded-wash/), [wet in wet](/blog/wet-paper-painting/) and [lifting out](/blog/lifting-out/). In this lesson you paint a whole sky in one go, while the paper is wet, and then leave it alone.

## Plan before you wet

Decide where the horizon is, lightly in pencil. Decide where the clouds go and where the sky is clear. Once the paper is wet, there is no time to think.

## The steps

1. Wet the sky area with clean water, down to the horizon.
2. Lay a graded blue from the top, paler towards the horizon.
3. While it is still wet, lift soft cloud shapes with a tissue.
4. Drop a little weak red and blue into the bottom of each cloud for its shadow.
5. Tilt the board slightly and let it dry flat.

## Painting skies in grey weather

Not every sky is blue. For an English sky, mix the grey from [three colours](/blog/three-colour-palette/) and use it wet in wet. John Robert Cozens painted mountains under grey skies like this two hundred years ago, with very few colours.

## The hardest part

The hardest part is stopping. When the sky is wet, it always looks unfinished. Students go back in to fix it and make blooms. Paint it, lift the clouds, and walk away for ten minutes.

## Watching real skies

The best teacher for painting skies is the sky itself. Look up for a minute each day and notice where it is darkest and where the clouds have soft or hard edges. The [Wikipedia page on clouds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cloud) names the common shapes, which helps you see them.

## The project

On a half sheet, paint three small skies side by side: a clear morning sky, a cloudy day and a grey rainy sky. Keep the land under them as a simple dark band. Painting skies three times in a row teaches you more than one careful sky. These are the start of your [first finished landscape](/blog/first-landscape/).
