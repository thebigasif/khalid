So far you have controlled every stroke. Wet in wet means letting go a little. You wet the paper first, then drop colour in, and the water spreads it. The edges come out soft, and colours mix on the paper in ways you could not paint by hand.

## How wet the wet paper should be

The paper should shine evenly, with no puddles. If it is too wet, colour runs everywhere and fades to nothing. If it is too dry, you get hard edges. Wet the paper with clean water and the large brush, then wait until the shine is even.

## Dropping colour into wet paper

Load the brush with strong colour, stronger than you would use on dry paper. The water already on the paper will thin it. Touch the tip to the paper and lift. Watch the colour bloom out. Do not brush it about; let it move.

## Timing on wet paper

Wet paper changes as it dries. In the first minute, colour spreads far and soft. After two or three minutes, it spreads less and keeps more shape. Just before the shine goes, it hardly spreads at all. Learning these stages is the real skill, and only practice teaches it.

## Exercise one: soft clouds

Wet a postcard-sized patch. Drop in blue around the edges and leave the middle white. Tilt the board a little and watch the blue drift. You have a cloudy sky.

## Exercise two: two colours meeting

Wet the patch again. Drop yellow at one side and blue at the other, and let them meet in the middle. Do not stir. Where they touch, a soft green appears. You will use this again when [mixing greens](/blog/mixing-greens/). The [Wikipedia page on watercolour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watercolor_painting) calls this way of working on wet paper wet on wet.

## When it goes wrong

A bloom is wet in wet at the wrong moment. If one appears where you did not want it, let it dry and decide whether you like it. Many painters keep them. If not, the next lesson, [lifting out](/blog/lifting-out/), shows you how to soften it.
