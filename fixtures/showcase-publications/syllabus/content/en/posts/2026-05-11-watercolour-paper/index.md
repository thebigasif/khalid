If you change one thing in your kit, change the watercolour paper. Good paper forgives mistakes and lets paint spread evenly. Poor paper buckles, streaks and refuses to let you fix anything. This lesson shows you why.

## Weight

Paper weight is given in grams per square metre, or gsm. Most watercolour pads are 300 gsm. Lighter paper, such as 190 gsm, cockles when it is wet: it rises in hills and the paint runs into the valleys. For this course, use 300 gsm.

## Cotton or wood pulp

Paper made from cotton soaks up water slowly and evenly. Paint stays workable for longer, and you can lift colour back out, as you will in [lifting out and fixing mistakes](/blog/lifting-out/). Paper made from wood pulp, often sold as cellulose, is cheaper. Paint sits on top of it and dries fast, with hard edges.

Cotton paper costs more, so many students use cellulose paper for practice and cotton for finished work. That is a sensible plan.

## Grain

Watercolour paper comes in three surfaces. Hot pressed is smooth. Cold pressed, sometimes called NOT, has a fine grain. Rough has a strong texture. Cold pressed is the best all-round surface, and it is what the tutors use in every video.

## Taping watercolour paper down

Wet paper swells, then shrinks as it dries. Tape all four sides to a board with masking tape before you start. The tape holds the paper flat, and when you peel it off at the end you get a clean white border.

## The exercise

Cut one strip from your pad and one from cheap drawing paper. Paint a stripe of blue across each one with plenty of water. Watch how each strip dries, then try to lift some colour out with a damp brush. The difference is the whole point of this lesson.

The [Wikipedia page on watercolor paper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watercolor_paper) explains how each sort is made. Next, in [first brush marks](/blog/first-brush-marks/), you will start painting on your watercolour paper.
