A flat wash is a patch of even colour, the same from top to bottom. It sounds simple, and it is the skill under every watercolour. Once you can lay a flat wash, skies, walls and still water follow.

## Mix more than you need

Mix a big pool of colour before you start, at least twice what you think you need. If you run out halfway, the new mix will never match and you will see a line. Use the large brush and plenty of water.

## Tilt the board

Raise the top of your board about 15 degrees with a book. The slope makes the paint gather in a bead along the bottom of each stroke. That bead is what keeps the wash even.

## A flat wash stroke by stroke

1. Load the brush fully and paint a band across the top of the shape.
2. Load again and paint the next band, just touching the bead of the first.
3. Carry on down the paper. Never go back into paint that has started to dry.
4. At the bottom, dry the brush on kitchen paper and touch it to the last bead to soak it up.

## Why blooms happen

A bloom, sometimes called a cauliflower, is a pale shape with a frilly edge. It happens when wet paint runs back into a patch that is half dry. The fix is to keep the wash moving, and to soak up the final bead.

## The exercise

Tape down a quarter sheet and paint four flat washes, each the size of a postcard. Use ultramarine for two and red for two. Let them dry flat. Hold them up to the window and look for streaks. Most students need three or four tries before a wash comes out clean.

The [Wikipedia page on washes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wash_(visual_arts)) shows how painters have used them. Good [watercolour paper](/blog/watercolour-paper/) makes a flat wash far easier.
