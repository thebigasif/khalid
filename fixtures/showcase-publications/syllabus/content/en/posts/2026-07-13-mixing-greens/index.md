Green is the colour students ask about most. A landscape painted with green straight from the tube looks flat and false. Real greens change: yellow in the sun, blue in the distance, nearly brown in the shade. This lesson is about mixing greens that change with the light.

## Start with yellow and blue

Yellow and blue make green. Your warm yellow and ultramarine make a soft, slightly grey green. More yellow gives a fresh spring green. More blue gives a deep, cool green.

## Warm it or cool it

Add a touch of red to a green and it turns warm and earthy, like summer grass or an olive tree. Add more blue and water and it turns cool and pale, like hills far away. This is how one mix becomes a whole range.

## Look at a master

[Albrecht Dürer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albrecht_D%C3%BCrer) painted a clump of grass in 1503, known as the Great Piece of Turf. Look at it closely. There is hardly one pure green in it. The leaves are yellow, grey, blue and brown greens, side by side.

## The green ladder

Paint a row of seven squares. Start with yellow and a little blue, and add a little more blue in each square. Then paint a second row with a touch of red in every mix. Compare the two rows. The second row looks more like real plants.

## Mixing greens on wet paper

Use the method from [wet in wet](/blog/wet-paper-painting/): wet a patch, drop in yellow and blue, and let them mix on the paper. Greens mixed on the paper are livelier than greens mixed on the palette.

## The exercise

Go outside or to a window and find three greens: one in sun, one in shade and one far away. Mix each one and paint a small swatch beside a pencil note of what it is. Mixing greens from life like this teaches more than any chart. You will use these when you paint [trees and distant hills](/blog/trees-distant-hills/).
