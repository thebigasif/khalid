Anna Atkins made her book of British algae in 1843 by laying seaweed on paper coated for the cyanotype process and leaving it in the sun. The plant blocked the light and left a pale shape on deep blue. Tidewater Books asked us for a cover for their new history of the seashore, and three prints to sell beside it.

## The blue is the hard part

A cyanotype blue sits outside what four printing inks can reach. Offset printing makes it a little grey and a little purple. So the cover uses a fifth ink, a special blue we mixed against an original page in the library. The pale seaweed is the bare paper, as it is in the original.

Ptilota plumosa, the second print, kept with the edge of the bound book in view.
:::

## Real cyanotypes for the prints

For the three prints we went back to the process itself. We made film negatives from the restored scans and printed them on cotton paper coated by hand, in the sun on the studio roof. Each print is a little different, as each of her pages is. A cool day with thin cloud gave the deepest blue.

:::figure{src="polysiphonia.jpg" alt="A cyanotype page with five rounded clumps of fine seaweed, pale on a bright blue ground"}
Polysiphonia fastigiata, the third print, with five clumps on one page.
:::

## Keeping her labels on the algae

Atkins wrote the name of each plant at the foot of the page. We kept every label, since the hand is hers. Read more about [Anna Atkins and her book](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anna_Atkins). For another plant book, see [Merian's Surinam plates](/blog/surinam-plates/).

## What the algae teach

Each page of algae is a record of one plant pressed flat. Atkins made it to help people name the algae they found on the shore, so the book was a field guide before it was art. The publisher wanted readers to feel that. We kept the plates at the size of the book page, with the algae as large as they really are.
