Katsushika Hokusai made Fine Wind, Clear Morning around 1831, in his series Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji. People call it Red Fuji. The Coastline Festival asked us for a pair of risograph posters: the mountain for the hills of its site, and the Great Wave for its shore.

## Red Fuji in three colours

A risograph prints one colour at a time, like a screen print, but fast and cheap. We had three drums of ink: red, blue and a warm yellow. Every colour in the posters comes from those three, laid over each other or broken into fine dots. The red mountain is red over a little yellow. The sky is blue with the paper showing through for the clouds.

:::figure{src="great-wave.jpg" alt="A wide Japanese print of a huge blue wave with white claws of foam curling over three boats, with a small snowy mountain far behind"}
The Great Wave, the second poster, from the same series.
:::

## Keeping the grain

Hokusai's prints show the grain of the wood in the flat areas of colour. A clean digital fill would lose that, so we kept the grain from the scans in each layer. On the risograph it prints as a soft texture that reads as wood from a step away.

## Two hundred of each

The festival sold the posters at its gate and gave a pair to every volunteer. Tomas printed all four hundred in two days. Read more about [the series of views of Fuji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thirty-six_Views_of_Mount_Fuji). For a hand-printed edition of a Japanese print, see [Rain at Atake](/blog/atake-rain/).

## Why Red Fuji works as a poster

Red Fuji is a poster before it is printed as one. It has one large shape, three main colours and a clear edge against the sky. At a distance the red mountain reads first, then the clouds. That is what a festival poster needs on a fence or a lamp post. For another bird and nature poster, see [Audubon's flamingo](/blog/flamingo-poster/).
