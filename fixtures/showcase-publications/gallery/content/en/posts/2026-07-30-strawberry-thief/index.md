William Morris designed Strawberry Thief in 1883, after he saw thrushes stealing fruit in his kitchen garden. It was printed on cotton by a slow method that took weeks for each length of cloth. Linden and Row, a shop for cloth and paper, asked us for a clean repeat they could print on fabric and on gift wrap.

## Finding the repeat

A repeat is one tile of a pattern that fills a surface when it is laid side by side. Strawberry Thief repeats in a mirror: the left half is the right half turned over. We measured a printed length from the 1930s and found the tile, then traced every leaf and bird as a clean shape.

:::figure{src="second-pattern.jpg" alt="A square of Strawberry Thief, thrushes and strawberries in red and cream on deep blue, from a later printing"}
A later printing of the same design, which we used to check the colours.
:::

## Eight colours or four

The cloth was printed in many colours. Gift wrap needs fewer, so we made two versions. The fabric keeps eight colours for screen printing. The paper uses four and lets two of them overlap to make a fifth. From across a room you cannot tell them apart.

## The blue

The deep blue ground is the hardest colour to match. Old cloth has faded unevenly, so we matched the blue to the darkest part of the old print, which has seen the least light. Read more about [the design and its making](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strawberry_Thief). For another bird picture, see [the kingfisher prints](/blog/kingfisher-prints/).

## Testing the Strawberry Thief repeat

Before any cloth was printed, we printed the Strawberry Thief repeat on paper at three sizes and taped the sheets to the studio wall. From across the room we looked for seams, places where the tile's edge showed. One leaf crossed a seam at the wrong angle, and we redrew it. For a print made one colour block at a time, see [Rain at Atake](/blog/atake-rain/).
