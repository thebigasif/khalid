Haeckel's jellyfish are some of the most loved pictures in natural history. The Harbour Science Museum asked us for six of them as prints for its new sea gallery and its shop. Ernst Haeckel drew them for his book Art Forms in Nature, printed between 1899 and 1904, and the plates have been in the public domain for a long time.

## Choosing six jellyfish plates

The book has a hundred plates. We laid out twenty of the sea plates on the studio wall and lived with them for a week. The six we kept all have one large shape in the middle and fine lines at the edges, so they read from across a room and reward a close look. The jellyfish plate leads the set. The siphonophores and the sea anemones follow it.

## Cleaning a plate by hand

Our scans came from a library copy of the book. The paper had browned and the next page showed through in places. We cleaned each plate on screen, a small patch at a time, and never with a filter that works on the whole picture. A filter softens the finest lines, and those lines are why people love Haeckel. Each plate took about two days.

:::figure{src="anemones.jpg" alt="A plate of sea anemones in orange, yellow and pink, crowded on a dark sea floor"}
The sea anemones, the darkest plate of the six, needed the most care in the shadows.
:::

## Paper and colour

The prints are on a heavy cotton rag paper with a soft surface, close to the feel of the book. Tomas matched the colours to the library copy under daylight, not to the scan. You can read more about [Ernst Haeckel and his book](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kunstformen_der_Natur). For another plate from the same book, see [Haeckel's hummingbirds](/blog/hummingbird-plate/).

## Why jellyfish sell

The museum's shop sells more jellyfish prints than any other subject. A jellyfish has no hard edge, so Haeckel drew it as lines and soft colour, and that reads well on a wall at any size. The jellyfish plate also works in a small frame, where the bells still show and the tentacles become a texture. We printed a test of each plate at three sizes before the museum chose the final one. For another sea subject printed in its own colour, see [Anna Atkins's algae](/blog/blue-algae/).
