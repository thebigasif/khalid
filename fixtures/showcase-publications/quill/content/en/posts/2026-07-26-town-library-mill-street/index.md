I have worked at the town library on Mill Street for six years. It is one long room with tall windows, about thirty thousand books and a radiator that bangs at ten past nine every morning. It is the busiest public building in town, and most people never think about it.

## Who comes in to the town library

The first visitors are always the same: two retired men who read the newspapers, and a woman who uses the computers to write to her daughter in Canada. After school, the children's corner fills up. On Saturday mornings it is families, and on wet days it is everyone.

Some people come to borrow books. Many come for other things: to print a form, to sit somewhere warm, to ask a question they did not know where else to ask.

## What they borrow

Crime novels go out more than anything else. After that come cookery books, local history and large print. The book with the longest waiting list this summer is a guide to the birds of the estuary, which my sister would say is no surprise. She wrote about the birds on her [winter estuary walk](/blog/winter-estuary-walk/).

## The Thursday group

Every Thursday evening a dozen of us meet to talk about one book. We have read everything from short novels to a history of salt. Nobody has to finish the book. The only rule is that you come and say what you thought of the part you read.

## Why it matters

A town library is one of the last places where you can stay as long as you like without buying anything. It lends books for free to anyone who asks. In a small town, that is a lot.

If your own library has a reading group or a quiet room, it is worth a visit. [Libraries Connected](https://www.librariesconnected.org.uk/) explains what public libraries do and how to find yours.
