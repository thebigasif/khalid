The best winter estuary walk I know starts at the end of Quay Row and follows the sea wall out towards the river mouth. On a clear day you can see the far bank. Last Sunday you could not see much at all, and it was all the better for it.

## Low tide, early

I set out at half past seven, an hour after low water. The tide tables are worth a look before any walk here, because the mud changes the whole place. At high water the estuary is a flat grey sheet. At low water it opens into miles of shining mud cut by channels, and the birds come down to feed.

The mist was thick enough that the sea wall seemed to run into nothing. Every few minutes a shape came out of it: a post, a moored boat lying on its side, a dog walker with a torch.

## Sounds on a winter estuary walk

In fog you hear the estuary before you see it. Curlews call all along the shore, a long rising note that carries a long way. Redshank go up in alarm as you pass. Somewhere out on the mud a group of geese talked to each other the whole time I was walking, and yet I never saw them once.

I have started noting the birds I hear, not just the ones I see. As a result, the list doubles on a misty day.

## The cold, and what helps

Winter on the wall is colder than it looks, because nothing stops the wind. I wear more than seems sensible and take off a layer at the turn. A flask of tea helps more than anything else. I keep a [walking bag list](/blog/walking-bag-list/) by the door, so I never have to think about what to take.

## Coming back

By the time I reached the turn at the old sluice, the mist had begun to lift. On the way back the far bank came slowly into view, then the church tower, then the roofs of the town. The tide was coming in fast over the mud.

If you try a winter estuary walk of your own, check the tides, stay on the path and keep well away from the soft mud. The [RSPB's guide to birds and wildlife](https://www.rspb.org.uk/birds-and-wildlife) helps you name what you hear.
