Alongside my daily notebook, I keep one other: a commonplace book. It holds no thoughts of my own. It is only for other people's sentences that I want to keep, copied out by hand.

## What a commonplace book is

The idea is centuries old. Readers kept a book of passages they had copied from their reading, so they could find them again and think about them. Some arranged them by subject. Mine is much plainer: each line copied out with the book and the page, in the order I find them.

## Why copy by hand

Copying a sentence by hand means reading it slowly, word by word, three or four times. By the time I have written it out, I know how it works: where the weight falls, and which word is doing the most. It is the closest I come to studying writing, and it takes a minute.

I already keep [handwritten drafts](/blog/handwritten-drafts/) each morning, so it felt natural to add this.

## What goes in

Anything that makes me stop reading for a moment:

- a description so exact I can see the thing;
- a short line that says something I had only half thought;
- a sentence that is simply built better than mine are.

Kit adds lines too, when he finds one he thinks I will like. His come on slips of paper tucked into the back.

## Reading the commonplace book back

Once a season I read the whole book through from the start. It takes an evening. I always find that some lines have gone flat and others have grown. The book is a record of what I was paying attention to, and that changes more than I expect. Reading it back is a little like the ten minutes of [sitting still](/blog/sitting-still/) I keep each morning.

## Starting one

You need a notebook and a book you are reading. Copy out one sentence you liked, with the title and the page. Do it again tomorrow. Kit's notes on [reading slowly](/blog/reading-slowly/) pair well with it, and the [Bodleian Libraries](https://www.bodleian.ox.ac.uk/) hold some fine old commonplace books if you want to see how others did it.
