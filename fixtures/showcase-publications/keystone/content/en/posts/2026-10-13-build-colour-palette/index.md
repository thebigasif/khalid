We build a colour palette by finding two to four colours in the client's own place and then writing each one down as a recipe for every material it will appear on. A colour that looks right on screen, on paper, on a sign and on a van is a colour the client can keep using for years.

Most colour problems we are asked to fix come from one cause. A colour was chosen on a screen and then left for each printer and sign maker to guess. The palette drifted, one job at a time, until the shop's bags, sign and website were three different greens.

## Start from the place, not a chart

We look for colours the client already has before we open a colour chart. At [Fernhill Bakery](/blog/fernhill-bakery-identity/) the colours came from the oven brown of the crust and the cream of the price cards. At [Pell and Sons](/blog/pell-hardware-website/) it was the dark green of a sign painted in 1962. People recognise colours they have seen for years, even if they could not name them.

We photograph the colours in daylight, hold paint and ink swatches up against them, and choose the nearest match by eye. Then we check the colours work together and that text on them can be read.

## Keep the colour palette small

We rarely give a client more than four colours. Each has a job.

| Role | What it does | How many |
|---|---|---|
| Main colour | The colour people remember; used on the sign, the van, the bags | 1 |
| Ground | The background, usually a warm white, cream or natural paper | 1 |
| Text | A dark colour for words, often near black | 1 |
| Accent | An extra colour for one purpose, such as sale labels or a children's area | 0 or 1 |

A small colour palette is cheaper to print, easier to keep consistent and stronger in the memory. If a client needs more, such as the six play colours for the [Northgate Theatre posters](/blog/northgate-theatre-posters/), we add them as a set with clear rules.

## Check that words can be read

Every pair of colours that carries text must have enough contrast to read easily, including for people with low vision or colour blindness. We measure contrast with the method in the [Web Content Accessibility Guidelines](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/contrast-minimum.html), which gives a ratio between two colours. We aim for at least 4.5 to 1 for normal text everywhere, in print as well as on screen, because it is a good rule for signs and labels too.

## Write a recipe for every material

This is the step that makes a colour palette last. For each colour, we write down how to make it on every material the client uses. One green might need five different recipes.

| Material | How the colour is specified | Who uses it |
|---|---|---|
| Screen | A hex code and an RGB value | The web designer |
| Coated paper | A spot ink reference and a CMYK mix for coated stock | The printer |
| Uncoated paper | A separate spot ink reference and CMYK mix for uncoated stock | The printer |
| Painted signs and vans | A paint reference from a standard paint range | The sign maker and paint shop |
| Vinyl and enamel | The maker's nearest standard colour, checked against a sample | The sign maker |

The same ink looks brighter on coated paper and softer on uncoated paper, so one colour needs two print recipes. We explain why in [how we choose paper stock](/blog/choose-paper-stock/).

## Give the client physical swatches

Numbers are not enough. Every client gets a small box of real swatches: the colour on each paper and material they use, cut to the size of a business card. When a new printer quotes, the client posts them a swatch and says "match this". It is the most useful thing we hand over, and we describe the rest of the handover in [what goes in our brand guidelines](/blog/brand-guidelines-handover/).

:::callout{kind=tip}
Keep your swatches out of the sun. A swatch that sits on a sunny windowsill fades within a year, and then every job is matched to the wrong colour.
:::
