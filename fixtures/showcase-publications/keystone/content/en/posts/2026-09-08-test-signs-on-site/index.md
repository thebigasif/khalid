We test signs on site by printing every sign at full size on paper, taping it exactly where the real sign will go and leaving it there for a week while we watch people use the building. On most projects, about one sign in ten moves, changes or is removed before anything is made.

Paper is cheap and aluminium is not. A sign that is wrong in a drawing costs a few minutes to fix. A sign that is wrong on a wall costs a remake, a second fitting and, often, a patch of repainted wall.

## Why drawings are not enough

A sign plan shows where signs go from above. Visitors never see a building from above. They see it at eye height, moving, with a pillar in the way or the sun in their eyes. Only a sign at full size, in its real place, shows what a visitor will see. That is why we test signs where they will hang.

We once planned a direction sign for a corridor that looked perfect on the plan. On site, a fire door stood open all day and hid it completely. Paper found that on the first morning.

## How we test signs on site

:::pullquote{by="Tobias Reyne, signage and wayfinding lead"}
Paper on the wall for a week tells us more than a month of drawings.
:::

### What we print

To test signs well we print every sign at full size, on plain paper or thin card, in black and one colour. Large signs are printed in tiles and taped together with the [workshop tools on our bench](/blog/studio-workshop-tools/). We do not try to make them look finished, because people should react to where the sign is and what it says, not to how it looks.

### Where and how we fix them

Each paper sign goes exactly where the real one will: the same height, the same wall, the same angle. We use low-tack tape so nothing marks the walls. Every paper sign carries a small number in one corner, which matches the sign plan, and a line asking passers-by to write comments on it.

### What we watch for

For the first two days, one of us spends a few hours near the busiest routes and simply watches. We look for five things.

| What we watch for | What it tells us | What we usually change |
|---|---|---|
| People stopping and looking around | A decision point without a sign | Add a sign |
| People walking past a sign without looking | Wrong height, wrong side or hidden | Move it |
| People reading a sign twice | Too many words, or a confusing word | Shorten it |
| People asking staff the way | The route breaks somewhere | Walk the route again with them |
| Notes written on the paper | Visitors telling us directly | Read every one |

:::figure{src="folded-facade.jpg" alt="A white facade of folded triangular panels seen at a steep angle, light and shadow on each fold, showing how a surface changes with the angle you test signs from"}
Walls are rarely flat to the eye. A sign that reads well face on may vanish at the angle most people pass it.
:::

## What changed when we test signs

The numbers below come from our last four tests, run in 2025 and 2026. In each case we counted the signs that moved, changed their words or were removed after the paper week.

| Project | Signs tested | Moved | Words changed | Removed | Added |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| [Calder Street Library](/blog/calder-street-library-wayfinding/) | 64 | 7 | 5 | 2 | 3 |
| [Tidewater Ferries pier](/blog/tidewater-ferry-timetables/) | 9 | 1 | 2 | 0 | 1 |
| [Wrenfield Allotments](/blog/wrenfield-allotment-signs/) | 21 | 3 | 1 | 0 | 0 |
| [Saltmarsh Lido](/blog/saltmarsh-pool-signs/) | 28 | 4 | 3 | 1 | 2 |

Removing signs matters as much as adding them. Every sign that is not needed makes the others harder to see. Researchers call this [information overload](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_overload), and a wall of signs is a good example of it.

## What it costs

A week to test signs adds three to five days of our time to a project, plus printing. On a wayfinding project that is about 5 percent of the design fee. It has never failed to save more than it cost. We include it in every signage quote, as [what a project costs](/blog/what-a-project-costs/) shows.

:::callout{kind=tip}
You can test signs on site yourself for a shop or a small office. Print the sign on paper, tape it up, and ask five people who have never been there to find the room. Watch where they look first.
:::
