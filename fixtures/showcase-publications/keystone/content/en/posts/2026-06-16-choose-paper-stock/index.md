We choose paper stock by holding it first and reading the catalogue second. For each job we decide what the paper must do, such as survive an ice bucket or take a pencil, then compare three or four real samples under daylight before we pick one.

A paper's numbers tell you a lot, but not how it feels to the person who holds it. A menu, a label and a theatre poster each meet a different hand, and the right paper stock is the one that suits that moment.

## What we decide first

Before we look at any paper, we write down four things about the job.

1. **Where it lives.** A cider label meets water and ice. A poster meets sun and rain. A letter sits in a drawer.
2. **What happens to it.** Is it folded, written on, posted, stuck down or torn off?
3. **How long it must last.** A flyer lasts a week; a book of brand guidelines lasts years.
4. **What it should say about the client.** A heavy uncoated card says "made with care". A bright coated sheet says "news".

## The words that describe paper

Printers describe paper stock in a few terms. Knowing them makes ordering much easier.

- **Weight**, given as grams per square metre (gsm). Office paper is about 80 gsm. A postcard is about 300 to 350 gsm.
- **Coated or uncoated.** A coated paper has a thin layer that keeps ink on the surface, so colours look bright and sharp. An uncoated paper soaks ink in a little, so colours look softer and the paper feels natural.
- **Finish**, such as gloss, silk or matt for coated papers, and smooth or textured for uncoated ones.
- **Shade**, from bright blue-white to warm natural white and cream.
- **Grain direction**, the way most fibres lie. Paper folds cleanly along the grain and cracks across it. The [history of paper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paper) explains why: fibres line up as the sheet is formed.

## The paper stock we use most

We keep a drawer of samples, but most of our jobs use one of six papers.

| Paper | Weight | Finish | What we use it for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Natural uncoated | 120 gsm | Smooth, warm white | Letterheads, guidelines books, notes that are written on |
| Natural uncoated card | 350 gsm | Smooth, warm white | Business cards, menus, price cards |
| Recycled kraft | 90 gsm | Brown, slight texture | Bread bags, wrapping, gift box sleeves |
| Matt coated | 170 gsm | Matt | Posters with photographs, leaflets |
| Wet-strength label paper | 80 gsm | Uncoated, textured | Bottle labels that meet ice and water |
| Coloured uncoated board | 300 gsm | Smooth, dyed through | Covers and folders, where an edge shows colour |

:::callout{kind=tip}
Ask the printer for a blank dummy: an unprinted copy of your job, at full size, on the real paper stock. It costs little or nothing, and it settles more questions than any sample book. You can feel the weight, fold it, post it to yourself and see how it arrives.
:::

## How we compare samples

We lay three or four samples side by side by a north-facing window, because daylight shows true colour and studio lights do not. Then we test each sample for the job's real life.

| Test | How we do it | What it shows |
|---|---|---|
| Fold | Fold it along and across the grain | Whether it cracks at the fold |
| Write | Write on it in pencil and ballpoint | Whether ink smudges or pencil slides |
| Wet | Leave it in water or an ice bucket | Whether a label peels or a sheet cockles |
| Light | Hold it to a window | Whether print on the back shows through |
| Post | Post it to ourselves | Whether it arrives bent, scuffed or torn |

For the [Orchard Lane cider labels](/blog/orchard-lane-cider-packaging/), the wet test ruled out two of six papers in an hour. For the [Northgate Theatre posters](/blog/northgate-theatre-posters/), the light test mattered most, because posters go up in glass cases that catch the sun.

## Paper and colour go together

The same ink looks different on different papers. A deep green prints brighter on coated paper and softer on uncoated paper. A warm white paper pulls every colour slightly towards yellow. So we always choose the paper stock before we sign off a colour, never after, and we proof the colour on the paper we will use. Our post on [building a colour palette](/blog/build-colour-palette/) explains how we write down a colour for each paper.

## Cost

Better paper costs more, but less than people expect. On a run of 1,000 menus, moving from a standard coated card to a good uncoated card usually adds less than the cost of one meal in the restaurant. On a box of business cards it is the price of a coffee. We show the difference in every quote, so a client can choose with the numbers in front of them.
