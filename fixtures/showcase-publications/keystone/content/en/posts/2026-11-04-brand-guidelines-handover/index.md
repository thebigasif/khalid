The brand guidelines we hand over at the end of a project are short: usually twelve pages, with a folder of files and a box of colour swatches. They tell the people who will use the brand every day what to do, in plain words, so the client can keep their brand right for years without calling us.

Many studios hand over a hundred-page book that nobody opens twice. We used to do the same. Then we visited a client a year after a project and found their guidelines still in the shrink-wrap, and every new leaflet slightly wrong.

## Who reads brand guidelines

The people who use brand guidelines are rarely designers. They are the shop owner ordering new bags, the volunteer making a poster for a one-off event, the printer quoting for business cards and the nephew updating the website. So we write for them.

- **Short sentences.** One instruction per line.
- **Pictures of right and wrong.** A real bag, a real poster, side by side.
- **No design words without a meaning.** If we must say "clear space", we show it.

## What goes in our brand guidelines

Every set of brand guidelines we write has the same eight sections, and most fit on one or two pages each.

| Section | What it holds | Pages |
|---|---|---|
| The brand in a sentence | What the business is and how it should feel, from the [first workshop](/blog/first-client-workshop/) | 1 |
| The marks | The wordmark and any stamp or symbol, with the space to leave around them and the smallest size | 2 |
| Colour | Each colour with its recipe for screen, print, paint and signs | 2 |
| Type | The typefaces, where to get them and which free one to use if they are missing | 1 |
| Photographs | Two or three rules, with examples | 1 |
| Things we made | Pictures of every item we designed, as a reference | 2 |
| Making new things | How to make a poster, a sign or a label that fits, step by step | 2 |
| Who to call | The printer, the sign maker, the web host, and us | 1 |

The colour section uses the recipes we describe in [how we build a colour palette](/blog/build-colour-palette/). The section on making new things is the one clients thank us for later, because it lets them make a poster for a summer fair without paying a designer.

## What goes in the folder

The brand guidelines are only half of the handover. The other half is a folder of files, sorted so that anyone can find what they need.

| Folder | What is inside | For whom |
|---|---|---|
| Marks | The wordmark and stamp in every colour and format | Anyone |
| Templates | Editable files for posters, notices, labels and letters | The client's staff and volunteers |
| Print files | Print-ready files for every item we designed | Printers |
| Sign files | Drawings and cutting files for every sign | Sign makers |
| Web | Colours, type and images sized for the website | Whoever looks after the site |
| Swatches | A note of where the physical swatch box is kept | Everyone |

We name every file in plain words, such as "Fernhill stamp, brown, for print". A file called "final_v7_FINAL" helps nobody in two years.

:::callout{kind=tip}
Keep one printed copy of your brand guidelines by the till or in the office, not only on a computer. A printed copy gets used, and people write notes in the margins, which tells you what to change next time.
:::

## Why short works

Short brand guidelines get read. Our clients use theirs because they can find the answer to a question in a minute. The [Calder Street Library](/blog/calder-street-library-wayfinding/) staff have made eleven new signs from their guidelines since February, and every one matches. The Northgate volunteers make their own event posters from the template we left them.

Longer guidelines make sense for a large company with many teams in many places. [Brand management](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brand_management) at that scale needs rules for every case. A bakery, a [bookshop](/blog/lantern-bookshop-sign-website/) or a theatre needs rules for the cases that actually happen.

## A visit a year later

We offer every client a free visit a year after handover. We walk round with them, look at everything they have made since and update the brand guidelines where they needed something we did not foresee. It takes us half a day, and it is the best way we know to learn what a handover really needs. It also explains why we stay a [small design studio](/blog/small-design-studio/): we want to be around a year later to make that visit.
