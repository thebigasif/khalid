We gave Fernhill Bakery a new bakery identity in 2025 by drawing it from things the shop already had: the gold lettering on its window, the stamp on its flour sacks and the price cards the owner writes by hand. The result is a wordmark, a stamp, two colours and a set of paper bags and labels that look as if they have always belonged to the shop.

## The brief

Fernhill Bakery has baked on the same corner of Eastbridge since 1936. The third generation of the family runs it now, and they had started selling bread to two cafés and a farm shop. Their bags said nothing, their van was plain white and the cafés wrote "local bakery" on the chalkboard because there was no name to copy.

The family asked for three things:

- a name people could read from across the street and remember;
- packaging that cost no more than their plain bags did;
- nothing that made the shop look new, because their customers liked it old.

## What we found in the shop

We spent the first morning in the bakery from five o'clock, when the ovens are loaded, until the queue cleared at ten. We photographed everything with a mark on it. Three things kept coming back.

1. **The window lettering.** A [sign writer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sign_painting) painted "Fernhill" in gold on the glass in the 1950s. Customers knew it, but it was flaking, and nobody had a copy of it.
2. **The flour sack stamp.** The mill that supplies the bakery stamps every sack with a round mark. The family had always liked its shape.
3. **The price cards.** The owner writes each day's prices on small cream cards. Regulars said the cards were the friendliest thing in the shop.

A good bakery identity grows from what customers already trust. So we set ourselves a rule: every part of the new bakery identity had to come from one of those three things.

## What we designed for the bakery identity

### A wordmark from the window

We traced the window lettering from photographs and redrew it as a clean wordmark. We kept its wide, slightly soft letters and its long tail on the "l", and we tidied the spacing so it holds up when it is printed small on a label. The sign writer's grandson repainted the window from our drawing in March.

### A stamp for everything else

The round stamp became our second mark. It holds the name, the year and a wheat ear, and it works in one colour on anything: paper bags, boxes, the café chalkboards and a rubber stamp the bakers use on wrapping paper.

### Two colours and one paper

We chose a deep oven brown and a warm cream taken from the price cards. Everything prints in brown on cream or brown on kraft paper. There is never a second ink to pay for, which kept the bakery identity inside the budget for plain bags.

| Item | Before | After | Cost per unit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bread bag | Plain white paper | Kraft paper, one-colour stamp print | Same as before |
| Cake box | Plain white card | White card, brown wordmark on the lid | 2p more |
| Café label | None | Cream label with name and bake day | 1p each |
| Van | Plain white | Brown wordmark on both sides | One-off cost |
| Price cards | Handwritten | Still handwritten, on printed cream card | Same as before |

:::callout{kind=note}
We kept the handwritten price cards on purpose. We printed only the border and the stamp, and the owner still writes the prices. Regulars told us they would miss her handwriting more than any logo.
:::

## How long it took

The whole bakery identity took eleven weeks, from the first morning in the shop to the first printed bags.

| Stage | Weeks | What happened |
|---|---|---|
| Listening | 1 to 2 | A morning in the shop, interviews with the family and two café owners |
| First ideas | 3 to 5 | Three directions, all drawn from the shop; the family chose the window |
| Design | 6 to 8 | Wordmark, stamp, colours, bags, boxes and labels |
| Printing | 9 to 11 | Proofs with the local printer, then the first run of bags |

## What changed

Six months after the launch, the two cafés print the Fernhill name on their menus, because now there is one to print. The farm shop moved the bread to the front of its counter. The family say the most common comment from customers is "you painted the window", which is exactly what we hoped to hear.
