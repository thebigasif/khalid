A first client workshop at Keystone Studio is a three-hour meeting, before any design starts, where we and the people who run your business agree what the project must do. You leave with a two-page summary of what we heard, what we will make and how we will know it worked.

Every project we take on starts this way, from a bakery's paper bag to a library's signs. The workshop is the cheapest part of a project and the one that saves the most money later.

## Why we start with a workshop

Most problems in a design project start as a misunderstanding in week one. The owner thinks the brief is about a logo; the staff think it is about the website; the person paying thinks it is about cost. Three hours in one room brings those views out before anyone has drawn a line. A [design brief](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Design_brief) is an agreement between client and designer, and an agreement is easier to reach in a room than by email.

We also learn things a written brief never says. At the workshop for [the Fernhill bakery identity](/blog/fernhill-bakery-identity/), the owner mentioned in passing that regulars loved her handwritten price cards. That remark shaped the whole identity.

## Who should come

Bring the people who will live with the result, not only the people who sign it off. For a small business that is usually three to five people.

- **The owner or manager**, who decides.
- **Someone who serves customers every day**, who knows what customers ask.
- **Someone who will update the work**, such as whoever edits the website or orders the printing.
- **Anyone who must approve the budget**, so cost is never a surprise later.

From our side, the designer who will lead the project comes, with one other designer to take notes.

## What happens in the three hours

1. **Introductions and the business in one sentence (20 minutes).** Each person says what the business does, in one sentence. The differences between the sentences are often the most useful thing we hear all day.
2. **Who comes to you (40 minutes).** We list your customers or visitors, what they want and where they get stuck. For a library this is a route through the building; for a shop it may be finding the opening hours.
3. **What to keep (30 minutes).** We walk through what you already have, such as a sign, a colour or a phrase, and sort it into keep, change and drop.
4. **What good looks like (40 minutes).** We agree how we will judge the result a year from now, in plain words such as "fewer people ask the desk where the toilets are".
5. **Limits (30 minutes).** Budget, dates, rules from a landlord or council, and anything already ordered.
6. **Questions and next steps (20 minutes).** You ask us anything, and we agree when you will see the summary.

## What you take away

Within three working days you get a two-page summary. It is short so that everyone actually reads it. It always holds the same five parts.

| Part | What it says | Example from a real workshop |
|---|---|---|
| The business in one sentence | The sentence everyone agreed on | "A neighbourhood bookshop that runs events for children" |
| Who it is for | The two or three groups that matter most | Parents, teachers, local book clubs |
| What we will keep | Things that stay as they are | The shop's green door and its name |
| What we will make | The list of things we will design | A shop sign, a website, an events poster |
| How we will judge it | Two or three measures, in plain words | "Parents can find Saturday events online in one click" |

We ask you to read it, correct it and agree to it before design starts. If something in it is wrong, it is far cheaper to fix a sentence than a sign.

## How to prepare for a client workshop

You do not need to prepare anything, but three things help.

- Bring examples of what you have now, such as a bag, a menu or a printed page of your website.
- Bring one thing you like from another business, and say why you like it.
- Think about what people ask you most often. Those questions point straight at what the design must solve.

:::callout{kind=tip}
If you cannot get everyone in one room for three hours, we split the client workshop into two shorter sessions on the same day. We do not run it by email, because the useful remarks come in conversation.
:::
