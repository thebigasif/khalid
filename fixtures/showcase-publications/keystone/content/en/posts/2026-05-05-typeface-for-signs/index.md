A good typeface for signs has open, distinct letters, a large lower case and plain shapes that stay clear from a distance and at an angle. We test every candidate at full size, from the distance people will read it, before we choose.

Reading a sign is not like reading a page. People read signs while walking, often at an angle, in poor light, sometimes in a hurry or a second language. A face that looks beautiful on a poster can fail completely on a corridor wall.

## Six tests for a typeface for signs

We hold every candidate to six tests. A face must pass all of them.

| Test | What we look for | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Open shapes | Wide openings in "a", "c", "e" and "s" | Closed shapes blur into blobs at a distance |
| Distinct letters | Capital "I", lower case "l" and the figure "1" all look different | Room numbers and names must never be confused |
| Large lower case | A tall [x-height](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-height), the height of the small letters | Small letters carry most words; taller ones read from farther away |
| Even strokes | Little contrast between thick and thin parts | Thin strokes vanish when lit from behind or seen at an angle |
| Plain figures | Numbers of the same width, easy to tell apart | Timetables, floors and room numbers line up and read quickly |
| A full family | Regular and bold weights at least, with accents | Signs need emphasis and must spell every name right |

## Why we avoid condensed faces

A condensed face is rarely a good typeface for signs. Narrow, condensed faces fit long words on short signs, which is why they are tempting. But their letters sit close together, and from a distance the spaces inside and between letters close up. We would rather shorten the words, or make the sign longer, than squeeze the type.

The same goes for all capitals. Words set in capitals are all the same height, so they lose the shape that lets us recognise a word at a glance. We use capitals for very short labels only, such as "WC" or "EXIT", where the shape is the word.

## How big the letters need to be

The size of a letter depends on how far away the reader is, whichever typeface for signs you choose. We use a simple rule as a starting point: about 25 millimetres of capital height for every 10 metres of reading distance, then we test and adjust on site.

| Reading distance | Example | Capital height we start from |
|---|---|---|
| 1 metre | A room sign read at the door | 15 to 20 mm |
| 5 metres | A direction sign in a corridor | 25 to 30 mm |
| 10 metres | A hanging sign in a library hall | 40 to 50 mm |
| 20 metres | A building name across a car park | 80 to 100 mm |
| 30 metres | A [ferry berth sign](/blog/tidewater-ferry-timetables/) seen from the water | 120 to 150 mm |

:::callout{kind=warning}
These numbers are a starting point, not a rule. Light, contrast and the angle a sign is seen from change them. We always print a full-size copy and read it from the real spot before we order anything.
:::

## Reading the shortlist from the real spot

:::figure{src="wood-type.jpg" alt="Old wooden printing letters of many sizes heaped at an angle, their worn faces showing the open shapes we look for in a typeface for signs"}
Old wood type is a good reminder that letters for distance were always drawn wider and plainer than letters for books.
:::

For each typeface for signs on our shortlist, usually three or four faces, we set the same test words in each: the hardest names on the project, a room number with a "1" in it and a word with "rn", which can read as "m". We print them at full size and pin them where the real sign will go. Then three people who have not seen them read them from the real distance, walking past, and we note which face they read first and which they misread.

On [the wayfinding at Calder Street Library](/blog/calder-street-library-wayfinding/), the face we liked best on screen came third in this test. Its "l" and "1" were too alike at five metres, and two of our readers could not tell whether a door said Room 11 or Room Il. The face we chose had a small tail on the "l" that settled it.

## Fashion is not a reason

We do not choose a typeface for signs by fashion, by what the client's website uses or by what looks good in a presentation. A sign's type may stay on a wall for twenty years. If the client's brand face passes the six tests, we use it. If it does not, we pair it with one that does and keep the brand face for headings in print.
