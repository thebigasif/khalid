We designed a new hanging bookshop sign and a small events website for the Lantern Bookshop in 2026, as one piece of work, so the shop looks the same on Quay Street and on a phone. The sign is a painted lantern shape that hangs over the pavement, and the website shows the week's events in two clicks.

## Two problems, one shop

The Lantern Bookshop sits in a narrow building on Quay Street, between a café and a chandler. It sells new and second-hand books and runs at least four events a week: story time, two book clubs and an author evening. The owner, Hannah Ward, came to us with two problems.

1. **People walked past.** The shop's name was painted above the window, flat to the wall, and nobody walking along the street could see it until they were outside the door.
2. **People missed events.** Events were listed on a printed sheet in the window and on social media, where they were lost among other posts. Parents often arrived for story time on the wrong day.

We treated these as one problem. Both were about being found: on the street and online.

## The bookshop sign

A hanging sign sticks out from the wall, so people see it from along the street. Quay Street has a long tradition of them; the chandler next door still has a painted anchor. We designed a lantern shape cut from aluminium, painted deep green with a warm yellow window, and the shop's name painted on both sides.

| Part | Choice | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Shape | A lantern, 600 mm tall | The shop's name, as a shape people remember |
| Material | Aluminium, 3 mm, powder-coated | Light, does not rust by the harbour |
| Colours | Deep green and warm yellow | Taken from the shop's painted door and window frames |
| Lettering | Painted by hand on both sides | Matches the street's older signs |
| Bracket | Black steel, made by a local smith | Strong enough for winds off the harbour |
| Light | A small lamp in the bracket, on a timer | Lit on winter evenings for events |

Hanging signs need permission from the council in a conservation area. We prepared the drawings and the application, and permission came through in six weeks. The bookshop sign was painted by the same sign writer who repainted the [Fernhill Bakery](/blog/fernhill-bakery-identity/) window.

:::callout{kind=note}
In the UK, most projecting signs need advertisement consent from the local council, and a conservation area adds rules about size and lighting. Check with your council before you order one. The [Planning Portal](https://www.planningportal.co.uk/) explains the rules for England.
:::

## The website

The website does one job above all: it shows what is on this week. The home page opens with the next seven days of events, each with a day, a time, a title and one line about it. Everything else, such as opening hours, how to order a book and how to find the shop, sits one click away.

- **Events are kept in one list** that Hannah edits from her phone, like a calendar.
- **Repeating events,** such as Saturday story time, are entered once and repeat on their own.
- **Each event has its own short page,** with a link to add it to a phone calendar.

The website uses the same green, the same yellow and the same lettering as the bookshop sign. The lantern shape appears small at the top of every page. Somebody who sees the sign in the street and then looks up the shop on their phone sees the same thing twice. We built it the way we built the [Pell and Sons hardware website](/blog/pell-hardware-website/): small, fast, and kept up to date by its owner.

## What changed

Hannah counts the people who come in each day. In the first month after the bookshop sign went up, weekday visitors rose by about a fifth compared with the same month last year. Story time now fills every week, and Hannah says the most common thing new customers say is "I saw the lantern".
