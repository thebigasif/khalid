The workshop tools on our studio bench are simple hand tools for cutting, folding, measuring and sticking, and we use them to make a mock-up of every box, label and sign before it goes to a printer or maker. A mock-up in the hand shows problems no screen can.

The bench is a long oak table under the north window at Ropewalk Yard. It belongs to everyone, but Hana, who writes this, keeps it in order.

## Why we make things by hand with workshop tools

A label on a screen is flat and perfect. A label on a bottle curves, catches light and sits next to five other bottles on a shelf. A box on a screen has no thickness; a real one has folds that crack, flaps that do not close and corners that crush in the post.

So every piece of packaging, every sign at small scale and every printed piece is made up on the bench first, with the workshop tools below. For the [Orchard Lane cider packaging](/blog/orchard-lane-cider-packaging/) we made 23 mock-up labels and four gift boxes before the printer saw anything.

## The workshop tools we use every day

| Tool | What it is for | What to look for |
|---|---|---|
| Steel rule, 60 cm | Measuring, and a straight edge for cutting | Cork backing so it does not slip |
| Self-healing cutting mat | A surface to cut on that closes up after each cut | A printed grid in centimetres |
| Scalpel and spare blades | Fine, accurate cuts in paper and thin card | A handle that fits your hand; change blades often |
| Rotary cutter | Long, straight cuts in paper and fabric | A guard that covers the blade when put down |
| Bone folder | Scoring and creasing folds so they are sharp | Real bone or a smooth plastic one |
| Drawing compass | Circles, and measuring distances off a drawing | A screw adjustment so it holds its setting |
| Cutting square | Square corners on boxes and labels | Steel, with a lip that hooks over the mat |
| Spray mount and double-sided tape | Fixing printed labels to bottles and boxes | Low-tack spray so labels can be moved |
| Small hand drill and bradawl | Holes for neck tags and sign fixings | Sharp points |

## How to score a fold

The tool people ask about most is the bone folder. Card cracks when you fold it without a crease first. To score a clean fold:

1. Mark the fold line lightly in pencil on the inside of the card.
2. Lay the steel rule along the line.
3. Draw the point of the bone folder along the rule, pressing firmly, to make a groove.
4. Fold the card away from the groove, then run the flat of the bone folder along the fold to set it.

Fold along the grain when you can. A fold across the grain is more likely to crack, which is why we check the grain on every [paper stock](/blog/choose-paper-stock/) before we design a box.

:::callout{kind=warning}
A blunt scalpel is more dangerous than a sharp one, because you press harder and it slips. Change the blade as soon as it drags, and always cut away from the hand holding the rule.
:::

## Signs at small scale

For signs, the same workshop tools make models at one tenth of full size: a corridor in foam board with tiny printed signs pinned to its walls. It is a quick way to see how signs line up along a route before we [test the real signs on site](/blog/test-signs-on-site/). Tobias uses the models to show clients where signs will go, because a model is easier to read than a plan.

## Looking after the workshop tools

Good workshop tools last for decades if you look after them. We keep blades in a closed box, wipe the steel rule after spray mount, and store the cutting mat flat and out of the sun, because heat warps it. Once a month, someone tidies the bench completely, and whoever does it gets to choose the radio station for the week. The [Crafts Council](https://www.craftscouncil.org.uk/) has good guides on hand tools and making, if you want to set up a bench of your own.
