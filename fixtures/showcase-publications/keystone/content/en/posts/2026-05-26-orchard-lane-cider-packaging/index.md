We designed new cider packaging for Orchard Lane Cider in 2026: one label system for six ciders, each named after the orchard its apples come from. The labels, gift boxes and crates now share a layout, so a customer can tell the ciders apart at a glance and still see they come from one maker.

## The brief

Orchard Lane makes cider from six small orchards around Wrenfield, and each orchard's apples taste different. The old labels were all the same apart from a small line of text, so shops put the wrong bottles in the wrong places and customers bought the dry one by mistake.

The owners wanted three things from the new cider packaging:

- each cider easy to tell apart on a crowded shelf;
- the orchard named on every bottle, because that is what makes their cider different;
- no plastic, and nothing that could not go in the paper or glass recycling.

## One system, six orchards

We built the labels as a system: a fixed frame that stays the same on every bottle, and one part that changes.

- **The frame** holds the Orchard Lane name, the bottle size, the strength and the legal text. It never moves.
- **The orchard band** is a wide band of colour across the middle of the label with the orchard's name and a drawing of one of its trees.
- **The taste line** is a short scale from dry to sweet, with a dot showing where this cider sits.

| Cider | Orchard | Band colour | Taste |
|---|---|---|---|
| Long Meadow | Long Meadow, planted 1952 | Pale green | Dry |
| Kiln Field | Kiln Field, planted 1978 | Brick red | Medium dry |
| Three Oaks | Three Oaks, planted 1931 | Deep green | Medium |
| Mill Bank | Mill Bank, planted 1990 | Straw yellow | Medium sweet |
| Hollow End | Hollow End, planted 2004 | Plum | Sweet |
| Orchard Blend | All six | Cream | Medium |

We drew each tree from photographs Hana took in the orchards in March, before the blossom. Bare branches show the shape of each tree, and the six shapes are different enough to tell apart even without colour.

## Paper, glue and ink

Every part of the cider packaging is paper, glass or metal. The labels are printed on an uncoated paper with a natural white tone and a little texture, so they feel like paper rather than plastic in the hand. We wrote about choosing it in [how we choose paper stock](/blog/choose-paper-stock/).

The labels stay on through a night in an ice bucket. We tested six papers and three glues on our [workshop bench](/blog/studio-workshop-tools/), in the studio fridge and in an ice bucket before we chose, and two of the papers peeled within an hour.

## Cider packaging for gifts and shops

The two-bottle gift box is plain kraft card with a printed paper sleeve in the orchard's colour. The wooden crates the shops receive carry a stencilled orchard name and the same colour band, so staff can shelve the bottles without opening the crate.

| Item | Material | Printing | Recycling |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bottle label | Uncoated paper, water-resistant glue | Four colours | Comes off in the glass wash |
| Neck tag | Card, cotton thread | One colour | Paper recycling |
| Gift box | Kraft card, no lamination | None | Paper recycling |
| Gift box sleeve | Uncoated paper | Four colours | Paper recycling |
| Shop crate | Reused wooden crate | Stencil, one colour | Returned and reused |

## What changed

The first shops had the new bottles in May. Two of them now shelve Orchard Lane by orchard, left to right from dry to sweet, which is exactly what the taste line was for. The owners told us the question they hear most at markets has changed from "which one is the dry one?" to "which orchard is nearest?". That is the conversation they wanted.

Like most of our work, this cider packaging started with a [client workshop](/blog/first-client-workshop/), and the colour bands grew from the method we describe in [how we build a colour palette a client can keep using](/blog/build-colour-palette/). [Kraft paper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kraft_paper) gets its strength from long wood fibres, which is why a plain kraft box survives the post without a plastic tray.
