To make compost, mix soft green waste, such as grass cuttings and vegetable peelings, with dry brown waste, such as cardboard, straw and dead leaves, in roughly equal amounts. Keep the heap as damp as a wrung-out sponge, turn it once a month if you can, and you will have dark, crumbly compost in six to twelve months.

Compost is the single most useful thing you can make for a vegetable garden. It feeds the soil, holds water in dry weather and opens up heavy clay. Spring is the best time to start, because the first grass cuttings arrive just as you clear last year's crops.

::toc

:::callout{kind=summary}
- Mix greens and browns. Too much green turns slimy; too much brown does nothing.
- Keep the heap moist and give it air.
- Bigger heaps work faster: aim for at least 1 m across.
- Two or three bays let one heap rot while you fill the next.
:::

## Choose where to make compost: a bin or a heap

You can make compost in a plain heap, a plastic bin or a bay built from old pallets. They all work. What matters is the size and where you put it.

- **Size:** a heap smaller than about 1 m by 1 m by 1 m does not hold enough warmth to rot quickly. A plastic bin of 300 litres or more is fine for a small garden.
- **Place:** on bare soil, so worms can come up into it, in a spot that is neither in full sun all day nor in deep shade.
- **Number:** two bins or bays is better than one. Fill one while the other rots, then use the finished compost and start again.

Bays in a row let you fill one, leave the next to rot and empty a third. Lids keep heavy rain out in winter.

## What goes in: greens and browns

[Compost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compost) is made by bacteria, fungi and worms. They need both nitrogen, which comes from soft green material, and carbon, which comes from dry brown material. Get the mix roughly right and they do the rest.

| Greens (wet, rich in nitrogen) | Browns (dry, rich in carbon) | Leave out |
|---|---|---|
| Grass cuttings, in thin layers | Cardboard and egg boxes, torn up | Meat, fish and cooked food, which bring rats |
| Vegetable and fruit peelings | Straw and hay | Dairy products |
| Annual weeds before they set seed | Dead leaves, in small amounts | Diseased plants, such as blighted potato leaves |
| Old bedding plants and spent crops | Shredded paper and newspaper | Roots of perennial weeds such as bindweed and couch grass |
| Tea leaves and coffee grounds | Woody prunings, chopped small | Cat and dog droppings |
| Comfrey and nettle leaves | Sawdust from untreated wood | Glossy or coloured paper |

A good rule is to add one bucket of browns for every bucket of greens. In summer you will have far more greens, so keep a sack of torn cardboard beside the bin and add a layer whenever you tip in grass.

## How to make compost, step by step

1. **Start with a layer of twigs.** A layer of coarse stems at the bottom lets air in from below.
2. **Add material in layers or mixed.** Mixed is better, but layers 10 to 15 cm thick also work.
3. **Chop large pieces.** Cabbage stalks and squash vines rot faster if you cut them into short lengths with a spade.
4. **Water dry layers.** Straw and cardboard need a can of water as they go in.
5. **Cover the top.** An old piece of carpet or a sheet of cardboard keeps the heat and moisture in.
6. **Turn it.** Once a month, fork the heap into the empty bay next to it, putting the outside material in the middle. This adds air and speeds everything up.

:::callout{kind=tip}
Grab a handful from the middle of the heap and squeeze it. A few drops of water should come out. If none does, it is too dry: add greens and water. If it runs, it is too wet: add browns.
:::

## Fixing common problems

| Problem | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Smells of rot or ammonia, slimy | Too many greens, too little air | Mix in torn cardboard or straw and turn the heap |
| Nothing happens for months | Too dry, or too many browns | Water it and add grass cuttings or peelings |
| Fruit flies in summer | Peelings left on the surface | Bury kitchen waste in the middle and cover it |
| Rats | Cooked food, or a dry heap with space beneath | Stop adding food scraps, keep it moist and stand the bin on wire mesh |

## When it is ready, and how to use it

Compost is ready when it is dark, crumbly and smells of a wood floor, and you can no longer see what went in, apart from the odd twig or eggshell. A hot, often-turned heap can be ready in six months; a cool heap left alone takes a year or more.

Sieve out anything that has not rotted and throw it back on the new heap. Then spread the finished compost 5 cm deep over your beds in autumn or late winter. [The guide to soil tests](/blog/read-improve-soil/) explains why a surface layer works better than digging it in.
