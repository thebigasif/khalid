To sow green manure, rake an empty bed in late summer or early autumn, scatter the seed thinly over the surface or sow it in rows, rake it in and water it. Let it grow over winter, then cut it down in early spring, leave it on the surface or dig it in lightly, and wait two to four weeks before you sow or plant into the bed.

A [green manure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_manure) is a crop grown to feed the soil, not to eat. Its roots hold the soil together through winter rain, it stops the food in the soil washing away, it smothers weeds, and when it is cut down it adds organic matter. On our plots, it is the easiest way to keep an empty bed working.

::toc

## Choose the right kind for the season

Some green manures are hardy and stand all winter; others are killed by frost and are best for a short gap in summer. Two kinds, field beans and winter tares, are legumes: their roots carry nodules that take nitrogen from the air and leave it in the soil for the next crop.

| Green manure | Sow | Hardy over winter | Adds nitrogen | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Winter field beans | September to November | Yes | Yes | Heavy soil; sown late, after squash and potatoes |
| Winter tares (vetch) | July to September | Yes | Yes | Most soils; good weed cover |
| Grazing rye | August to October | Yes | No | Sown latest; deep roots break up clay |
| Crimson clover | March to August | Mostly | Yes | Light soil; flowers feed bees |
| Phacelia | March to September | No, killed by hard frost | No | Short summer gaps; flowers feed bees |
| Mustard | March to September | No | No | Quick cover of four to eight weeks |

For a bed that will stand empty from October to March, sow field beans, tares or grazing rye. Before brassicas, avoid mustard, which is a brassica itself and can carry the same diseases.

Crimson clover adds nitrogen to the soil and flowers in late spring if it is left to grow. Cut it down as the first flowers open, before the stems toughen.
:::

## How to sow green manure

1. **Clear the bed.** Remove the old crop, pull out perennial weeds, and rake the surface level.
2. **Sow small seed broadcast.** Scatter phacelia, clover and mustard thinly over the surface, as evenly as you can, then rake them in lightly.
3. **Sow large seed in rows.** Push field beans into the soil 5 cm deep and 15 cm apart in rows 20 cm apart.
4. **Water** if the soil is dry, and keep birds off with a net until the seedlings are up.

Sow early enough for the plants to grow a good cover before the cold stops them. On our plots, we aim to have every green manure in by mid October, apart from field beans and grazing rye, which come up in cold soil.

:::callout{kind=tip}
You do not have to clear a whole bed. Sow green manure in the gaps between leeks and winter brassicas, or along the edge of a bed, and it still protects the soil there.
:::

## Over winter

Green manure needs almost nothing over winter. Pull out any large weeds, and walk on the bed as little as possible. If birds or pigeons strip it, a net over hoops keeps them off.

## Cut it down in spring

Cut the green manure down before it flowers, or at the latest as the first flowers open. Old, woody plants rot slowly and lock up nitrogen while they do.

1. **Cut it low.** Use shears, a strimmer or a sharp spade to chop the plants off at ground level, about four to six weeks before you want to sow.
2. **Chop the tops.** Cut the leaves and stems into short pieces with a spade.
3. **Choose: leave or dig.** Leave the chopped tops on the surface as a mulch, and cover with a sheet of card to rot down. Or dig them into the top 10 to 15 cm of soil.
4. **Wait.** Leave the bed for two to four weeks before you sow seeds. Rotting green manure can hold back small seeds such as carrots; larger transplants are less affected.

## Which crops follow which green manure

| After | Grow next |
|---|---|
| Field beans or tares | Brassicas or squash, which use the nitrogen |
| Grazing rye | Potatoes or transplants; wait four weeks before small seeds |
| Phacelia or mustard | Almost anything, after two weeks |
