To grow tomatoes outdoors, plant hardened-off plants in the sunniest, most sheltered spot you have once nights stay above 7°C, usually late May. Tie them to a stake, pinch out the side shoots, water deeply twice a week and feed weekly once the first fruit sets, and you will be picking from late July until the first frost.

If you grow tomatoes outdoors, the crop is less certain than under glass, because a cool, wet summer can bring blight. But a good outdoor crop tastes better than anything from under glass, and on a warm, sheltered plot it is reliable more often than not.

::toc

## Choose the right kind to grow tomatoes outdoors

Tomatoes grow in two ways, and the difference decides how you look after them.

| Type | How it grows | Support | Side shoots | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cordon (also called indeterminate) | One tall stem that keeps growing | A 1.8 m stake or string | Pinch out every week | Most outdoor and greenhouse crops |
| Bush (also called determinate) | A low, spreading plant that stops at about 60 cm | None, or a short cane | Leave them | Pots, windy plots, beginners |
| Tumbling | A trailing bush | None | Leave them | Hanging baskets and window boxes |

To grow tomatoes outdoors in a short summer, choose cherry and small-fruited kinds. They ripen weeks earlier than large beefsteak tomatoes, which often fail to ripen outdoors before autumn.

## Plant out in late May or early June

Wait until the last frost has passed and the nights stay above 7°C. Tomatoes planted into cold soil sit still for weeks and catch up with nothing. Harden them off for two weeks first, as in our [guide to hardening off](/blog/harden-off-seedlings/).

1. **Pick the warmest spot.** Full sun, against a south-facing wall or fence if you can, and out of the wind.
2. **Prepare the soil.** Dig a bucket of compost into each planting hole. Tomatoes belong in the potato bed of [the rotation plan for your vegetable plot](/blog/plan-vegetable-plot/), never where potatoes grow next year, because they share blight.
3. **Plant deep.** Set each plant so the soil comes up to its lowest leaves. The buried stem grows extra roots.
4. **Space them out.** Cordons 45 cm apart in a row; bush kinds 60 cm apart each way.
5. **Stake at once.** Push a stake in beside each cordon on planting day, before the roots spread.

## Tie in and pinch out side shoots

A cordon tomato puts out side shoots in the angle where each leaf joins the main stem. Left to grow, they turn the plant into a tangle with too many small fruits to ripen in a short summer. Pinch them out with your finger and thumb when they are 2 to 5 cm long.

:::figure{src="flower.jpg" alt="A yellow tomato flower and a cluster of buds on a hairy green stem, the first sign of fruit when you grow tomatoes outdoors, lit by sunshine, with leaves and a wire support behind"}
The first truss of flowers is your signal to start feeding. Leave flower trusses alone and pinch out only the shoots that grow in the angle between a leaf and the stem.
:::

- **Every week:** tie the main stem loosely to its stake with soft string, in a figure of eight, every 30 cm.
- **Every week:** pinch out new side shoots.
- **In early August:** pinch out the top of the main stem two leaves above the fourth or fifth truss of flowers. Any flowers that open after that will not have time to ripen.

## Water and feed

When you grow tomatoes outdoors, uneven watering causes most problems, from split fruit to black patches on the base of the fruit, called blossom end rot. Water deeply and regularly rather than a little every day.

- **Water at the base,** never over the leaves, which spreads disease.
- **Twice a week in dry weather,** a full can per plant, more for plants in pots.
- **Feed weekly** with a liquid tomato feed or comfrey tea from the first truss of flowers until late August.
- **Mulch** around each plant with compost or grass cuttings, to keep the roots cool and the soil evenly moist.

:::callout{kind=tip}
Sink a small plant pot into the soil beside each tomato on planting day, and water into the pot. The water goes straight to the roots instead of running off the surface.
:::

## Watch for blight

Tomato blight is the same disease as [potato blight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phytophthora_infestans), and it is the main risk to outdoor tomatoes. It arrives in warm, humid, wet weather, usually from late July. Brown patches spread on the leaves and stems, and the fruit turns brown and rots within days.

1. **Prevent it** by spacing plants well, removing the lowest leaves once the first truss has set, and keeping water off the foliage.
2. **Check every day** in warm, wet weather.
3. **Act at once.** Pick every fruit, green or ripe, and remove the plants. Do not compost them; put them in the council waste.

Green fruit picked from a blighted plant can still ripen indoors if it shows no brown patches.

## Picking and the end of the season

Pick tomatoes when they are fully coloured and give slightly when pressed. Pick every two or three days in August, so the plant keeps putting its energy into the green fruit.

When frost is forecast in late September or October, pick every remaining fruit. Ripen the large green ones in a drawer with a banana, which gives off the gas that ripens fruit, and turn small green ones into chutney. Then pull up the plants and put them on the [compost heap](/blog/make-compost/), as long as they are free of blight.
