To save seed from beans and peas, leave the best pods on the plant until they are dry and rattle, then shell the seeds and dry them indoors for two more weeks. To save seed from tomatoes, scoop the seeds from a ripe fruit, let them ferment in a little water for three days, then rinse and dry them on a plate. Store all three in paper envelopes in a cool, dry, dark place.

Beans, peas and tomatoes are the easiest crops to save seed from, because their flowers mostly pollinate themselves, so the seed grows into plants like the parent. Late summer is the time to do it.

::toc

## Which seed is worth saving

Save seed only from open-pollinated kinds, sometimes called heritage or heirloom kinds. Their seed comes true, meaning the plants grow like their parents. Seed from an [F1 hybrid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F1_hybrid), marked as such on the packet, grows into a mix of plants unlike the one you saved it from.

| Crop | How easy | Distance from other kinds | When to collect | Keeps for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Peas | Very easy | 2 m is enough | July to August, when pods are dry | 3 to 4 years |
| French beans | Very easy | 2 m is enough | August to September | 3 to 4 years |
| Runner beans | Easy, but bees cross them | 300 m, or grow one kind | September to October | 3 to 4 years |
| Tomatoes | Easy | 2 m for most kinds | August to September | 4 to 6 years |
| Lettuce | Medium | 2 m | August to September | 3 years |
| Squash and courgettes | Hard, bees cross them | Hand-pollinate | September | 5 years |

## Choose the best plants

Seed carries the habits of its parent. Mark the plants you want to save from early in the season with a piece of string, and choose:

- the healthiest plants, free of disease;
- the plants that cropped first, or most heavily;
- pods and fruits that are typical of the kind, not odd ones.

Never save from a plant that ran to seed early or caught a disease. You would be selecting for exactly the habits you do not want.

## How to save seed from beans and peas

1. **Leave the pods on the plant.** Stop picking from your marked plants in August. Let the pods swell, turn yellow, then brown and papery.
2. **Wait for the rattle.** Pods are ready when they are dry and the seeds rattle inside. In a wet autumn, pull up the whole plant and hang it upside down in a shed or greenhouse to finish.
3. **Shell the seeds.** Split the pods and tip the seeds onto a tray.
4. **Dry them indoors.** Spread them in a single layer somewhere warm and airy for two weeks. Dry seed is hard; test one by biting it, and it should leave no dent.
5. **Sort.** Throw away any seed that is shrivelled, mouldy or has a small round hole, which is the sign of a pea or bean weevil.

## Tomato seed needs a few days to ferment

Tomato seeds are wrapped in a gel that stops them sprouting inside the fruit. Fermenting the seeds for a few days breaks down the gel and kills some seed-borne diseases.

1. **Pick a ripe fruit,** a little riper than you would eat, from a healthy plant. Our guide to [growing tomatoes outdoors](/blog/grow-tomatoes-outdoors/) explains how to keep plants healthy.
2. **Scoop out the seeds** and their juice into a jar. Add a splash of water.
3. **Leave it for two to four days** at room temperature, stirring once a day. A white mould forms on top; this is normal.
4. **Rinse.** Fill the jar with water. Good seeds sink; pour off the mould, pulp and any floating seeds. Repeat until the water is clear.
5. **Dry.** Tip the seeds into a sieve, then spread them on a ceramic plate or a sheet of baking paper. Paper towel sticks to them. Leave them for a week, then rub them apart.

## Label and store

Put each kind in its own paper envelope. Write the crop, the kind and the year on the outside. Paper lets any last moisture out; plastic bags trap it and the seed goes mouldy.

Store the envelopes in a tin or a jar with a tight lid, in a cool, dry, dark place, such as a cupboard in an unheated room. Damp and warmth are the two things that shorten a seed's life. A sachet of dried rice in the tin soaks up stray moisture.

:::callout{kind=note}
How we test our saved seed: each February, Edith counts out ten seeds of each kind onto damp kitchen paper in a closed box on a warm shelf. If seven or more sprout within two weeks, the seed is good to sow. If fewer do, sow twice as thickly or replace it.
:::

## Share what you save

You will almost always save far more seed than you need. A seed swap, or a seed library such as the one in the Long Acre trading hut, turns your spare seed into other growers' plants and brings you kinds that are proven on local soil.
