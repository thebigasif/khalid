To protect seedlings from slugs and snails without pellets, plant out sturdy young plants rather than tiny ones, go out with a torch after dark to pick slugs off, and remove the damp hiding places near your beds. Add simple traps and barriers for the most precious crops, and give frogs, birds and beetles reasons to visit.

No single method stops every slug. What works is a few steady habits, used hardest in the weeks when slugs do the most damage: April to June, when seedlings are small and nights are damp.

::toc

## Know your enemy

[Slugs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slug) and snails feed mostly at night and after rain. They hide by day under boards, stones, pots, thick weeds and the edges of raised beds. Most of the damage in a vegetable garden is done by a few kinds of small slug that live in the soil and come up to eat seedlings at ground level.

Slugs also do good work. They eat dead leaves and rotting plants, and they feed hedgehogs, toads, frogs, beetles and thrushes. The aim is to keep them off your seedlings, not to clear the garden of them.

| Crop | How much slugs like it | When it is at risk |
|---|---|---|
| Lettuce and salad leaves | Very much | All season |
| Young beans and peas | Very much | The first three weeks after they come up |
| Courgettes, squash and cucumbers | Very much | The first two weeks after planting |
| Brassica seedlings | Much | Until they have five or six leaves |
| Potatoes | Much, below ground | From August, in damp soil |
| Onions, garlic, leeks | Little | Rarely |
| Tomatoes | Little | Only ripe fruit near the ground |

## Raise sturdier plants

A slug can eat a seedling with two leaves in one night. A plant with six leaves and a thick stem survives the same visit. This is the most useful change you can make.

1. Start slug favourites, such as lettuce, beans and squash, in [pots indoors](/blog/sow-seeds-indoors/) rather than sowing them in the soil.
2. Grow them on until they are 10 to 15 cm tall before planting out.
3. Raise trays of seedlings on a table or bench, away from the ground, while you [harden them off](/blog/harden-off-seedlings/).

## Go on a night patrol

The most effective control is also the simplest: go out after dark with a torch and a jar. Two hours after sunset on a damp evening, slugs are out in the open on your seedlings and easy to pick off.

- **When:** every evening for a week after planting out, then twice a week.
- **Where:** along the edges of beds and around the youngest plants first.
- **What to do with them:** take them well away from the plot, or drop them into a jar of salty water.

On our plots, three weeks of nightly patrols in May cut the damage to lettuce to almost nothing for the rest of the summer.

## Remove the places where slugs hide

Walk round the beds and lift anything slugs can hide under: boards, upturned pots, bricks, loose sacks and piles of weeds. Keep grass paths short and edges trimmed. A strip of bare, raked soil 30 cm wide around a seed bed leaves slugs nowhere to rest on their way in.

:::callout{kind=tip}
Turn the trick around. Lay a few boards or upturned grapefruit halves between the rows on purpose, then lift them every morning and collect the slugs gathered underneath.
:::

## Traps and barriers

These help for small areas and precious plants, but none is a complete answer on its own.

| Method | How it works | How well it works for us |
|---|---|---|
| Beer traps | A pot sunk in the soil with its rim 2 cm above the surface, half full of beer | Catches many slugs; needs emptying every two days |
| Bottle collars | A clear plastic bottle with the ends cut off, pushed over a young plant | Good for squash and courgettes for their first two weeks |
| Copper tape | A strip around pots and the edges of raised beds | Helps on pots; slugs cross it if it is dirty or broken |
| Wool pellets and grit | A ring around each plant | Some help in dry weather, little in wet |
| Nematodes | A natural parasite watered onto warm, moist soil | Works for about six weeks on soil-living slugs; costly |

:::callout{kind=warning}
Avoid metaldehyde slug pellets. They harm the birds, hedgehogs and pets that eat poisoned slugs. If you must use pellets, choose ones based on ferric phosphate, scatter them thinly, and never in heaps.
:::

## Invite the slug eaters

A garden with somewhere for wildlife to live has fewer slug problems each year. A small pond brings frogs and toads within a season. A log pile in a corner shelters ground beetles, which eat slug eggs. Thrushes break snails on stones, and hedgehogs travel through gaps 13 cm wide at the bottom of fences.

## Keep the soil from staying wet

Slugs thrive in wet, cold soil covered in dead material. Water seedlings in the morning, so the surface is drier by night, and at the base rather than over the whole bed. Keep a thick mulch away from the stems of young plants until they are growing strongly. Our guide to [watering in a dry summer](/blog/water-vegetable-garden/) has more on when and how to water.
