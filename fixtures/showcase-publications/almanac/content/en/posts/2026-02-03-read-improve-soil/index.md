To read and improve soil, start with three tests: rub a moist pinch between your fingers to find its texture, pour a bucket of water into a hole to see how fast it drains, and count the earthworms in a spadeful. Then improve soil the same way whatever the result: add organic matter every year, keep it covered, and walk and dig on it as little as you can.

February is a good month for both jobs. The soil is wet enough to show you its texture and drainage honestly, and anything you spread now has two months to settle in before you sow.

::toc

:::callout{kind=summary}
- Texture tells you what your soil is made of. You cannot change it, only work with it.
- Drainage and structure tell you how the soil behaves. You can change those.
- Earthworms are the simplest sign of healthy soil.
- Every soil, clay or sand, is improved by the same thing: a yearly layer of compost on the surface.
:::

## Test one: feel the texture

Soil is a mix of three sizes of mineral grain: sand, silt and clay. Their mix is the [soil texture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_texture), and it decides how the soil holds water and food. You can judge it in a minute with your hand.

1. Take a walnut-sized lump of soil from 10 cm down and pick out stones and roots.
2. Wet it until it is like putty, then rub it between finger and thumb.
3. Try to roll it into a sausage, then bend the sausage into a ring.

| What you feel | What it means | How it behaves |
|---|---|---|
| Gritty, will not hold a shape | Sandy soil | Drains fast, warms early in spring, loses food quickly |
| Smooth and silky, holds a shape but cracks when bent | Silty soil | Holds water well, crusts on the surface after rain |
| Sticky, rolls into a ring without cracking | Clay soil | Holds water and food, slow to warm, heavy to work when wet |
| A little gritty and a little sticky, holds a loose shape | Loam | Easy to work; the soil every gardener hopes for |

Most garden soils are somewhere between these. Our plot at the top of Long Acre is sandy loam; at the bottom of the hill it is clay you could make pots from.

## Test two: watch the drainage

Dig a hole 30 cm deep and 30 cm wide. Fill it with water, let it drain, then fill it again and time how long the second filling takes to disappear.

- **Under an hour:** free-draining. Water often in summer and mulch thickly.
- **One to four hours:** good drainage for most vegetables.
- **Over four hours, or still full the next day:** poor drainage. Grow in raised beds, add organic matter and never work the soil when it is wet.

If water sits on the surface but drains once you dig through the top layer, the problem is a compacted crust or pan, not the soil itself. A fork pushed in and rocked every 20 cm breaks it without turning the soil over.

## Test three: count the life

Dig out one spadeful of soil, 20 cm deep, onto a sheet and break it apart. Count the earthworms.

:::figure{src="earthworm.jpg" alt="An earthworm stretched across dark soil under a layer of straw mulch, which feeds the worms that improve soil, with young bean plants growing above it"}
Earthworms feed on mulch at the surface and pull it down into the soil. A mulched bed often holds far more of them than a bare one.
:::

Ten or more in a spadeful is a sign of healthy soil. Fewer than five usually means the soil is short of organic matter, has been dug hard, or is waterlogged. Smell the soil too: good soil smells sweet and earthy, while a sour or rotten smell means it lacks air.

## How to improve soil, whatever its type

The good news is that one method will improve soil of every kind. Organic matter, such as compost, leaf mould or well-rotted manure, opens up clay so it drains, and it holds water in sand so it does not dry out.

1. **Spread 5 cm of compost on the surface each year.** Late winter is ideal. You do not need to dig it in; worms will do that for you.
2. **Keep the soil covered.** Bare soil crusts in rain and blows away in wind. Cover it with mulch, a growing crop or a [a green manure crop](/blog/sow-green-manure/) over winter.
3. **Dig less.** Every time you turn soil over, you break the tunnels and threads that hold it together. Use a fork to loosen, and turn only where you must.
4. **Stay off the beds.** Make beds narrow enough to reach across, so you never tread on the soil you grow in.
5. **Never work wet clay.** If soil sticks to your boots, wait.

:::

## When to test your soil's acidity

Most vegetables grow well in soil that is slightly acid to neutral, a pH of about 6 to 7. A cheap test kit from a garden shop gives a fair reading. You only need to act if the result is below 5.5, when brassicas struggle; a dusting of garden lime on the brassica bed in winter is enough. Do not lime the potato bed, because potatoes grow scab in limy soil.

## What to do this month

- Run the three tests on each bed, and write the results on your [plot plan](/blog/plan-vegetable-plot/).
- Spread compost to improve soil on the beds that will be sown first.
- Cover any bare beds with card or a mulch until you sow.
- Start a [compost heap](/blog/make-compost/) if you do not have one; you will want its compost next winter.
