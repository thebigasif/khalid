To water a vegetable garden in a dry summer, water less often but more deeply: give each bed a long soak once or twice a week in the early morning, at the base of the plants, rather than a light sprinkle every evening. Then cover the wet soil with mulch, and save your water for the crops and growth stages that need it most.

A dry June is now normal on our plots, and the tap at Long Acre is shared by sixty plot holders. Knowing where water does the most good in a vegetable garden, and where it is wasted, is one of the most useful skills a grower can learn.

::toc

## Water deeply, not often

A light sprinkle wets only the top few centimetres. Roots grow up towards it, where the soil dries fastest, and the plants wilt every afternoon. A deep soak reaches 20 to 30 cm down, so roots grow deep and find water for days afterwards.

As a rule, each bed in a vegetable garden needs about 20 litres of water per square metre each week in dry weather, given in one or two soakings. That is two full cans for each square metre.

:::callout{kind=tip}
Not sure if you watered enough? An hour after watering, push a trowel into the soil and lever it back. Moist soil should reach the depth of your hand. If only the top 5 cm is dark, water again.
:::

## When to water

- **Early morning** is best. Less water is lost to the sun, and leaves dry before evening, which keeps slugs and mildew down.
- **Evening** is the second choice. Water at the base and avoid wetting the leaves.
- **Midday** loses the most to evaporation, but a wilting seedling should be watered whatever the hour.

## Which crops in a vegetable garden need water, and when

Each crop has a stage when water turns into harvest. Water at that stage, and you can ease off at others.

| Crop | When water matters most | What happens if it goes short |
|---|---|---|
| Seeds and seedlings | Every day until they come up, then every two to three days | They fail to come up or die off |
| Lettuce, spinach, salad leaves | All the time | Leaves turn bitter and plants run to seed |
| Peas and beans | When the flowers open and the pods swell | Flowers drop and pods stay empty |
| Potatoes | When the flowers open, as tubers start to form | Small tubers and scab on the skin |
| Tomatoes, courgettes, squash | When fruit is swelling | Split fruit, blossom end rot and fewer fruit |
| Sweetcorn | When the tassels appear and the cobs swell | Gappy cobs |
| Carrots, parsnips, beetroot | Only in long droughts | Roots split if heavy water follows a long dry spell |
| Onions and garlic | Until mid July, then not at all | Small bulbs; late water spoils storage |

Established root crops need surprisingly little. Their deep roots find water that others cannot, and too much water makes carrots grow leaf instead of root.

## Make every can count

1. **Water at the base.** Hold the spout close to the soil and pour slowly, so it soaks in instead of running off.
2. **Make a well.** Draw a ring of soil around large plants such as squash, so water pools where the roots are.
3. **Use a sunken pot.** A small pot or a bottle with its base cut off, sunk beside each tomato or courgette, takes water straight to the roots.
4. **Weed.** Every weed drinks water meant for your crops. Hoe on dry, sunny mornings, when cut weeds shrivel at once.

## Mulch after you water

[Mulch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mulch) is any layer laid on the soil surface: compost, grass cuttings, straw or leaf mould. Spread on moist soil, it keeps the water in for days longer and stops a crust forming.

- Water the bed thoroughly first; mulch on dry soil keeps it dry.
- Lay grass cuttings in thin layers, no more than 2 cm at once, so they do not turn slimy.
- Keep mulch a few centimetres away from the stems of young plants, where slugs might shelter.

Over the years, a mulched bed also builds up organic matter, which holds even more water. [A yearly soil test](/blog/read-improve-soil/) shows the change.

## Collect rainwater

A water butt on every shed and greenhouse roof is the cheapest way to have water when the tap is busy or banned. A 10 square metre shed roof collects about 600 litres from a 60 mm rainfall, which is roughly a summer month's rain on our plot. Rainwater is also better for most plants than tap water, because it is soft and at air temperature.

:::callout{kind=note}
How we worked out the figures: the litres per square metre and the roof figure come from Bram's own water butt and rain gauge records on plot 22 over four summers. They are a guide; sandy soil needs more water, clay less.
:::

## What to do in a hosepipe ban

Rank your crops. Water seedlings and newly planted crops first, then fruiting crops at the swelling stage, then salads. Leave established roots, onions and squash with deep roots to fend for themselves. Use washing-up water on ornamental plants and fruit bushes, but not on salad crops you eat raw. And keep [slugs and snails](/blog/slugs-without-pellets/) in mind: a dry spell is a good time to clear their hiding places.
