To plan a vegetable plot for the year, split it into four beds, give each bed one group of crops, and move every group one bed along each year so no family grows in the same soil twice running. Then draw a simple map, list what you will sow each month, and leave room for a second crop when the first one is cleared.

January is the best month to do it. The ground is too wet or frozen to work, the seed you need is easy to find, and you still remember last year's mistakes. One evening with a pencil saves weeks of dithering in April.

::toc

:::callout{kind=summary}
- Grow what you eat, and grow less of each crop than you think.
- Split the plot into four beds and rotate the crop groups around them.
- Draw a map to scale, with paths, and mark the sunny and shady ends.
- Write a month-by-month sowing list and plan a second crop for each early bed.
:::

## Start with what you eat

The best way to plan a vegetable plot is to begin in the kitchen, not the seed list. Write down the vegetables your household eats every week, then the ones you buy only in season, then the ones that taste far better fresh. Peas, salad leaves, new potatoes, beans and tomatoes almost always earn their space. Maincrop onions and large cabbages are cheap to buy and slow to grow, so they come lower on the list.

Next, be honest about time. A full allotment plot needs about four hours a week from April to September. If you have two, plan for half the vegetable plot and cover the rest with [a winter cover of green manure](/blog/sow-green-manure/) or a sheet of card and compost until you are ready.

## Split your vegetable plot into four rotation beds

[Crop rotation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crop_rotation) means growing each family of vegetables in a different bed each year. It stops the pests and diseases of one family from building up in the soil, and it lets crops with different needs follow each other. Hungry crops come after compost; roots come after the hungry crops, when the soil is less rich.

| Bed | Crop group | Main crops | What the bed gets before planting |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Potatoes | [Early and maincrop potatoes](/blog/chit-plant-seed-potatoes/) | A thick layer of compost or rotted manure |
| 2 | Legumes and fruiting crops | Peas, broad beans, runner and French beans, squash, sweetcorn | Compost in the planting holes |
| 3 | Brassicas | Cabbages, kale, broccoli, swede, radish | Firm ground and a dusting of lime if the soil is acid |
| 4 | Roots and onions | Carrots, parsnips, beetroot, onions, garlic, leeks | Nothing extra; a rake to a fine tilth |

Each year, every group moves one bed along: potatoes go where the roots were, legumes follow potatoes, and so on. Over four years, each bed has every group once.

:::callout{kind=tip}
Tomatoes and potatoes are in the same family and share blight. Keep [outdoor tomatoes](/blog/grow-tomatoes-outdoors/) in the potato bed or in pots, never in the bed where potatoes grow next year.
:::

## Draw a map of your vegetable plot to scale

Measure the plot and draw it on squared paper, one square to 50 cm. Mark north, the water tap, the compost bins and any shade from hedges or sheds. Then draw your four beds and the paths between them.

- **Beds:** no wider than 1.2 m, so you can reach the middle from both sides without stepping on the soil.
- **Paths:** at least 45 cm wide, or 60 cm if you will push a wheelbarrow along them.
- **Tall crops:** [climbing beans](/blog/grow-climbing-beans/) and sweetcorn go at the north end of a bed, so they do not shade the rest.
- **Permanent crops:** rhubarb, asparagus and fruit bushes sit outside the rotation, at one end of the plot.

:::figure{src="raised-bed.jpg" alt="One narrow raised bed of a vegetable plot, packed with spinach, rocket and orange and yellow nasturtiums, with a spade standing in the bare soil of the path beside it"}
A bed narrow enough to reach across is never trodden on, so its soil stays loose and needs no digging.
:::

## Write a sowing list for each month

Turn your crop list into a month-by-month list of jobs. Our [month-by-month sowing calendar](/blog/vegetable-sowing-calendar/) has the dates for every common crop; copy the lines you need into your own notebook. For each crop, write when to sow, where (indoors, in a seed bed or straight into the plot) and how many.

How many is the hard part. Most first plans grow far too much of one thing at once. These are the amounts we sow for a household of two to four people.

| Crop | How much to sow at once | How often |
|---|---|---|
| Lettuce | Half a row, about 1.5 m | Every three weeks, March to August |
| Radish | 1 m of row | Every two weeks, March to September |
| Carrots | Two 3 m rows | Twice: April and June |
| Beetroot | One 3 m row | Three times: April, May and July |
| Peas | Two 2 m rows | Twice: March and May |
| Runner beans | Twelve plants | Once, in May |
| Courgettes | Two plants | Once, in May |
| Tomatoes | Six plants | Once, sown in March |

## Plan the gaps: catch crops and second crops

A bed that is cleared in July can grow a second crop before winter. Write the second crop into the plan now, so you have the seed ready.

1. **After early potatoes (late June):** sow French beans, carrots for autumn, or plant out leeks.
2. **After peas and broad beans (July):** plant out kale or sow beetroot and chard.
3. **After garlic and onions (July and August):** sow salad leaves, spinach or a green manure.
4. **Between slow crops:** radish and lettuce fit between rows of parsnips and leeks, and are picked before the slow crop needs the space.

## Keep the plan where you can see it

Pin the map in the shed and keep last year's beside it. At the end of each month, note what did well, what failed and why. Those notes are the start of next January's plan, and after three years they will tell you more about your vegetable plot than any book. The [monthly jobs list](/blog/november-jobs/) is a good prompt for what to check.
