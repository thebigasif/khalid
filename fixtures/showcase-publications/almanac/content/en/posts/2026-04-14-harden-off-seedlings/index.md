To harden off seedlings, put them outside in a sheltered, shady spot for two or three hours on the first day, then leave them out a little longer and in a little more sun each day, for seven to fourteen days. By the end, they stay out day and night, and they are ready to plant.

Plants raised on a windowsill have soft leaves that have never felt wind, full sun or a cold night. Planted straight out, they wilt, scorch or sit still for weeks. Hardening off thickens their leaves and stems slowly, so they carry on growing when you move them.

::toc

## Which plants need hardening off

Harden off seedlings of every plant raised indoors or in a heated greenhouse, including hardy ones such as cabbages and lettuce. Tender plants, such as tomatoes, courgettes, squash, beans and sweetcorn, need it most, and they also need to wait until the last frost has passed.

| Plant | Start hardening off | Plant out | Lowest night temperature it will take |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lettuce, cabbages, leeks, onions | Early April | Mid to late April | Light frost, about minus 2°C, once hardened |
| Peas and broad beans sown indoors | Late March | Early to mid April | Light frost |
| Celery and celeriac | Early May | Mid to late May | 5°C; cold nights make it bolt later |
| Tomatoes | Mid May | Late May to early June | 7°C |
| Courgettes, squash, cucumbers | Mid May | Late May to early June | 7°C |
| Runner and French beans | Mid May | Late May | 7°C |
| Sweetcorn | Mid May | Late May | 7°C |

## A two-week plan to harden off seedlings

This is the plan we use at Long Acre for tender crops. Hardy crops can move through it in a week by doubling each step.

1. **Days 1 and 2:** put the plants outside for two to three hours in the middle of the day, in light shade and out of the wind. Bring them in for the night.
2. **Days 3 and 4:** leave them out for half the day, with an hour or two of morning sun.
3. **Days 5 to 7:** leave them out from morning until early evening, in sun for half that time.
4. **Days 8 to 10:** leave them out all day in full sun. Bring them in, or close the cold frame, at night.
5. **Days 11 to 14:** leave them out day and night, unless the night forecast is below the temperature in the table.

:::callout{kind=warning}
Skip a day and bring the plants in if the forecast is for strong wind, hail or heavy rain. A single cold, windy afternoon can undo a week of hardening off.
:::

## Use a cold frame to harden off seedlings

A [cold frame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cold_frame) is a low box with a clear lid. It makes hardening off much less work, because you only have to open and close the lid instead of carrying trays in and out.

- **Week one:** lid closed at night and propped open by a hand's width in the day.
- **Week two:** lid fully off in the day, propped open at night.
- **At the end:** lid off day and night for two days before planting.

If you do not have a cold frame, a sheet of horticultural fleece pegged over the trays at night does the same job for hardy crops. Tender crops still need to come indoors on cold nights.

## Signs that something is wrong

- **White or pale patches on the leaves:** sun scorch. Give the plants more shade for two days, then build up the sun more slowly.
- **Leaves that turn purple or blue:** the plants are too cold. Bring them in at night for longer.
- **Wilting in the afternoon:** the pots are too dry, or the sun came too fast. Water and move them into shade.
- **Holes in the leaves:** slugs have found them. Raise the trays off the ground on a table or bench; our guide to [slugs and snails](/blog/slugs-without-pellets/) has more.

## Planting out

Once you harden off seedlings fully, plant on a calm, cloudy day or in the evening, so the plants have a night to settle before they meet the sun. Water each plant in its pot an hour before, then water again once it is in the ground.

1. Dig a hole a little larger than the pot.
2. Knock the plant out of its pot, holding it by the root ball, not the stem.
3. Set it at the same depth it grew in the pot. Tomatoes and leeks are the exceptions: plant them deeper, because they root along the buried stem.
4. Firm the soil around the plant with your fingers and water it in.

:::callout{kind=tip}
Cut the bottom out of a plastic bottle and push it over a young courgette or squash for its first week outdoors. It keeps off wind and slugs, and it warms the plant at night.
:::

## After planting

Water newly planted seedlings every two or three days in dry weather for the first fortnight, until their roots grow into the surrounding soil. Watch them closely for the first week; this is when slugs and cold nights do the most damage.
