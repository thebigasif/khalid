A church hall recording was not our first plan for [the second album](/blog/glass-weather/). We had booked a studio, and it flooded a week before the sessions. A friend who sings in a choir told us her church hall was free in March. We went to see it the next day and booked it on the spot.

## Why a church hall recording works

A hall like that has a long, warm echo, about two seconds from a hand clap. Most studios try to kill that sound. We wanted to keep it. So instead of putting each player in a booth, we set up in a circle in the middle of the floor and played together, the way we do on stage.

That has a cost. Every microphone hears every instrument, so you cannot fix one part later without the others. We decided we would rather have one great take than a perfect patchwork.

## The setup

- **Drums:** three microphones only, one over the kit, one in the kick drum and one on the snare.
- **Room:** a pair of microphones up in the organ loft, which give the record most of its sound.
- **Vocals:** Isla sang live into a ribbon microphone, facing the drums, which kept the spill even.
- **Desk:** a small borrowed mixing desk into my laptop, in the vestry next door.

## What went wrong

The heating clanked every twenty minutes, so we learned to start songs right after it stopped. A choir rehearsal on Thursday nights meant packing everything away twice a week. And on day nine the organ tuner arrived without warning. We recorded him too; he is the low hum at the start of "Low Light on the Minch".

## What I would do again

I would make a church hall recording again tomorrow. The room made the band play quieter and listen harder, and you can hear that on every song. If you want to try it, read about [room acoustics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Room_acoustics) first, ask about the heating, and bring a lot of extension leads. Our own [room under the railway](/blog/rehearsal-room/) is where we worked the songs out before we went in.
