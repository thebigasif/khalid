This country sourdough loaf is the bread we bake most weeks. It needs a lively starter, a little white and wholemeal flour, and two days of mostly waiting. The hands-on time is under an hour.

Feed your starter the night before you mix. If you are new to bread, bake our [overnight tin loaf](/blog/overnight-tin-loaf/) first, and read [how to tell when kneaded dough is ready](/blog/kneading-dough/).

## Ingredients

- 100 g active sourdough starter, fed 8 to 12 hours before
- 375 g water, at about 27°C
- 450 g strong white bread flour
- 50 g wholemeal flour
- 10 g fine sea salt
- Rice flour, for the banneton

## Method

1. **Mix.** Stir the starter into 350 g of the water, then add both flours and mix until no dry flour is left. Cover and rest for 45 minutes.
2. **Add the salt.** Sprinkle the salt over the dough with the last 25 g of water and squeeze it in with wet hands.
3. **Stretch and fold.** Every 30 minutes for 2 hours, lift one side of the dough and fold it over, turning the bowl four times.
4. **Rise.** Leave the dough covered until it has grown by about half and shows bubbles at the sides, 2 to 4 hours more.
5. **Shape.** Turn the dough out, shape it into a tight round and set it seam side up in a floured banneton.
6. **Cold rise.** Cover and chill overnight, 12 to 16 hours.
7. **Bake.** Heat a lidded cast iron pot at 250°C for 45 minutes. Tip the loaf onto paper, slash the top and bake for 20 minutes with the lid on, then 20 to 25 minutes with it off at 230°C.
8. **Cool.** Leave the loaf on a rack for at least an hour before you cut it.

## How a good country sourdough loaf looks

The crust should be a deep brown, darker than you think, with a raised ear where you slashed it. A pale loaf tastes flat. The crumb should be glossy, with holes of mixed sizes.

## When it goes wrong

A dense loaf usually means the dough rose too little. Give it more time at room temperature next bake, rather than more yeast or a warmer spot. A flat loaf that spreads usually rose too long, so shorten the first rise by half an hour.

Wikipedia's page on [sourdough](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sourdough) explains why a starter makes bread rise.
