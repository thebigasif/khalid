Salt marshes survive rising seas by building up: mud and plant roots raise the surface a little each year. Our paper in Coastal Carbon Letters uses fifteen years of marsh elevation records to ask whether our marshes are keeping pace with the sea. Most of the marsh is, just. The lowest parts are not.

## How we measure marsh elevation

In 2009 we set 30 fixed benchmarks across four marshes. Each spring we measure the surface around each one to the nearest millimetre, using a [surface elevation table](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_elevation_table). Over the same years, the local tide gauge shows the sea rising by about 4 millimetres each year.

The method is slow but simple. A metal arm fixed to the benchmark holds nine pins, which are lowered to the mud. The length each pin travels tells us the height of the surface.

## What we found

- **The high marsh is keeping up.** Its surface rose by about 4.2 millimetres each year, matching the sea.
- **The low marsh is falling behind.** It rose by about 2.8 millimetres each year, so each spring it sits a little deeper in the tide.
- **Mud supply matters most.** Marshes near the river mouth, where the water carries more mud, kept up best.

## What it means

The low marsh is where cordgrass grows thickest and where small fish shelter at high tide. If it keeps falling behind, it will slowly turn into mudflat. Two things can help: letting more mud reach the marsh, and giving marshes room to move inland where the land allows. Planting also helps a bare marsh build up faster, as our [cordgrass planting trial](/blog/cordgrass-planting-density/) shows.

## Update: spring 2026

The spring 2026 readings follow the same pattern. The high marsh gained a little more than the sea, and the low marsh a little less. One benchmark near the river mouth was lost to a slumping bank, and we have set a new one beside it.

## What comes next

We keep measuring every spring. The benchmarks are now part of a regional network, and the full record is open in the lab's data archive. Our newer work on [carbon in a rebuilt marsh](/blog/restored-marsh-carbon/) builds on these plots.
