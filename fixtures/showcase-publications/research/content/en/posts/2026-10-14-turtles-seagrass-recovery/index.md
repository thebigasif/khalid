Seagrass recovery after a storm was about a third faster in meadows that green turtles grazed lightly than in meadows where we kept turtles out. That is the main result of our new paper in Marine Habitat Review, from three years of fenced and open plots in two meadows of the sound.

We expected the opposite. Turtles eat seagrass, and heavy grazing can strip a meadow bare. However, light grazing seems to work like mowing a lawn: it keeps the shoots young and short, lets light reach the bed, and leaves more stored food in the roots for regrowth.

## How we measured seagrass recovery

In 2023 we marked 36 plots, each four metres square, across two meadows. A third of the plots had a mesh fence that kept turtles out. A third had a fence with gaps a turtle could pass through, so we could check that the fence itself changed nothing. The rest were open.

Every month we counted shoots, measured leaf length and photographed each plot from above. Then, in September 2024, a storm buried part of both meadows under sand. That gave us a natural test, because every plot started again from the same point.

## What we found

- **Grazed plots regrew faster.** Open plots were back to their shoot count from before the storm in about 14 months. Fenced plots took about 21 months.
- **Roots made the difference.** Grazed shoots stored more starch in their roots, which feeds new growth after burial.
- **Too much grazing still harms.** Where turtles came back to the same patch week after week, the bed thinned instead.

In short, recovery depends on light grazing spread over a large meadow, not on keeping every grazer away.

## Why it matters

Seagrass meadows store carbon, shelter young fish and hold sand in place. Many restoration plans treat grazers as a threat to fence out. Our results suggest that a healthy turtle population, spread over a big enough meadow, helps the meadow bounce back after storms. You can read more about [green turtles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_sea_turtle) and what they eat.

The study has limits. We followed only two meadows, both in sheltered water, and one storm. A meadow on an open coast, or one hit by two storms in a row, may behave differently. For that reason we have added two more meadows to the survey this year.

## Data and methods

The plot counts, photographs and root starch values are open in the lab's data archive, each with a note on how it was collected. The plot photographs were turned into shoot counts with the method in our [drone mapping paper](/blog/eelgrass-drone-mapping/). The next step is a [funded PhD place in the lab](/blog/phd-position-seagrass/), which will follow these meadows for three more years.
