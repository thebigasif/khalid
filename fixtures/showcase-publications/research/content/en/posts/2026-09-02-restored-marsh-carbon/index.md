A rebuilt salt marsh stores carbon faster than many people expect. Our paper in Coastal Carbon Letters measures marsh carbon at Eastfield, a marsh rebuilt in 2015, and compares it with the natural marsh beside it. After ten years, Eastfield buries carbon at about two thirds of the natural rate.

## Why marsh carbon matters

Salt marshes bury carbon in their mud, where waterlogged soil keeps it from rotting. This store is often called [blue carbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_carbon). If rebuilt marshes store carbon quickly, restoring them is a useful way to take carbon out of the air. If they take decades, the case is weaker, so the speed matters as much as the total.

## How we measured it

Rafael and the field team took 48 sediment cores, half at Eastfield and half in the natural marsh. They took the water samples beside each core with our [small-boat sampler](/blog/small-boat-carbon-sampling/). In each core we measured three things:

1. how much organic carbon the mud holds at each depth;
2. how fast the mud built up, from marker layers of white clay we laid on the surface in 2015, 2018 and 2022;
3. how dense the mud is, so we could turn shares of carbon into grams.

Multiplying the three gives the carbon buried each year in each square metre.

## What we found

The natural marsh buried about 210 grams of carbon per square metre per year. Eastfield buried about 140 grams. In its first three years the rate was much lower, about 60 grams, so the new marsh has been catching up steadily.

Most of the gap comes from plant cover. Eastfield still has bare patches where cordgrass has not spread. Where plants cover the ground, burial rates are almost the same as next door.

## What it means for restoration

Plant cover is the thing to watch, because bare mud buries little and washes away in winter. Our [cordgrass planting trial](/blog/cordgrass-planting-density/) shows how to close bare patches faster. Height in the tide matters too: a marsh that sits too low drowns, as our [marsh elevation record](/blog/marsh-elevation-sea-level/) shows.

## The data

All 48 cores are listed in the open dataset, with depths, carbon values and the dates of each marker layer. Other groups can use the same marker method on their own sites for the price of a few bags of clay. We will core Eastfield again in 2027, from the lab's new boat, to see whether the gap keeps closing.
