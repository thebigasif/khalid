The lab's new research boat, the Petrel, went into the water on Monday. She is a nine-metre aluminium [catamaran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catamaran) with a shallow draft, a covered cabin and a small winch on the stern.

## Why we needed a new research boat

Our old boat drew too much water to reach the shallow seagrass meadows at low tide, and it had no winch, so every sediment core was pulled up by hand. The Petrel floats in 60 centimetres of water and can lift a two-metre core in a few minutes. She also has room for six people, so students can come along on survey days.

The old boat is not going far. A local sailing club has taken her on as a safety boat.

## What she will do first

- **Monthly seagrass flights.** Mei will launch the drone from the deck for the [drone mapping](/blog/eelgrass-drone-mapping/) surveys, which can now reach the outer meadows.
- **Winter coring.** The next round of marsh cores for the carbon project starts in January.
- **Water sampling.** The [small-boat sampler](/blog/small-boat-carbon-sampling/) fits on the side rail, so every trip can bring back water samples too.
- **Fish counts.** Lukas will use her to reach the more exposed reefs next summer.

## How we chose her

Rafael spent a year asking other coastal labs what they would change about their own boats. The answers were the same: a shallow draft, a flat working deck, and a cabin to keep sensors and people dry. So the Petrel has all three, and very little else.

## Thank you

The research boat was paid for by the university's field equipment fund and a grant from the regional coastal trust. Rafael wrote the specification and spent most of the summer at the boatyard. The first student trips start in spring, and the new [PhD student on the seagrass project](/blog/phd-position-seagrass/) will use her from day one. Students who want to join a trip can ask Rafael through the [about page](/blog/about/).
