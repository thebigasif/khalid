Oyster reefs are nurseries. Our paper in Estuarine Field Studies counts juvenile fish and crabs on twelve natural oyster reefs and on bare mud close to each reef. Over the summers of 2024 and 2025, traps on the reefs held far more animals than traps on the mud.

## Counting juvenile fish

Lukas and the field team set small lift traps at high tide on each reef and on mud 30 metres away, then lifted them two hours later. Every animal was measured, named and let go. We repeated this every two weeks from June to September in both years.

The traps are simple frames of mesh on the bottom, so nothing is hurt. Students from the [summer field school](/blog/summer-field-school/) now help with the counts each June.

## What we found

- **Many more animals.** Reef traps held a mean of 23 small fish and crabs in 2024 and 2025. Mud traps held a mean of 4.
- **More kinds.** Reefs held 19 kinds of fish and crab. Mud held 8.
- **Bigger reefs, more life.** Catch went up with reef area, but levelled off above about 400 square metres.

The shell gives hiding places, and the oysters filter the water, which helps the tiny animals that the young fish eat. Gobies, blennies and young flatfish were the most common.

## Why it matters for restoration

If built reefs work like natural ones, they should shelter young fish too. Our later paper shows that [oyster castles cut erosion](/blog/oyster-castles-erosion/) on a sheltered bay. In summer 2026 we began the same trap counts on those built reefs.

## Update: the first counts from built reefs

The first season on the built reefs, in 2026, looks promising. Traps there held about half as many animals as on natural reefs, but already far more than on bare mud. We will publish the full comparison when the second season is in.

The trap counts for every site and date are in the lab's open data archive. You can read more about [oyster reefs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oyster_reef) and how they form.
