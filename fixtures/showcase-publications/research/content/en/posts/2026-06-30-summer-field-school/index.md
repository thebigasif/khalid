Our summer field school ran for two weeks in June, with twelve undergraduate students from three universities. They learned to survey a mudflat, core a marsh and name the animals that live in the mud. They also found something new.

## A day at the field school

Each morning started with the tide table. At low tide the students walked lines across the mudflat, counting worm casts and cockles in square frames. In the afternoon they sorted samples under the microscope at the marine station and entered the counts into the lab's database.

On two days they joined Lukas on the reefs for the trap counts behind our paper on [juvenile fish on oyster reefs](/blog/oyster-reefs-juvenile-fish/). Each evening one group presented what they had found that day.

## What the students found

The students noticed that [lugworm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arenicola_marina) casts were far more common within about 50 metres of the oyster reefs than further out. We had walked those flats for years without counting worms there.

It fits with our work on [oyster castles](/blog/oyster-castles-erosion/): calmer water behind a reef lets finer mud settle, and lugworms like fine mud. Two of the students are now writing it up as a short note, with Lukas as their supervisor.

## Who came

The twelve students came from biology, geography and environmental science courses. Half had never worked on a coast before. Most had never used a corer, and none had sorted mud under a microscope. By the end of the first week each group ran its own survey line without help, and by the second week they were planning their own sampling.

## What we learned about teaching

The best days were the ones with a real question. When the students knew their counts would be used, they counted more carefully and argued about every doubtful worm cast. So next year every group will own one question from the start.

## Next year
